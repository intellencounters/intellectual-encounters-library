# 5. Arguing Well: Logic and Its Abuses

It starts, as it always starts, with one message sent at the wrong hour. Someone in the group posts an article — about politics back home, or eating meat, or whether children should be raised in Germany or in India — and adds one careless sentence of opinion. By the time you surface from your shift, there are ninety-four messages. Two cousins are no longer speaking. Someone has typed *so you think our parents were fools?* — which nobody said. Someone else has left the group, which in a family is not an exit but an announcement. And the original question, which was actually interesting, has not been advanced by a single line.

Chapter 1 taught you to see arguments as structures; Chapter 4, what makes beliefs worth holding. This chapter is about the collision of the two: what happens when people who disagree actually engage — and how that engagement goes wrong in the same few ways, over and over, in every language and every group chat on earth. The bad news is that the failures are ancient; Aristotle was cataloguing them more than two thousand years before anyone hit *forward*.[^aristotle5] The good news is that they are learnable, spottable, and — hardest and most valuable — avoidable in your own messages, not just detectable in other people's.

## 5.1 Validity: the plumbing of an argument

First, a precision tool that Chapter 1 left in its case. When you laid out the family-timing argument, you asked two questions: are the premises true, and do they support the conclusion? Logic gives the second question a sharp name. An argument has :::glossary term="Validity"::: when the conclusion *follows from* the premises — when it is impossible for the premises all to be true and the conclusion false. Validity is the plumbing, not the water: it asks whether the pipes connect, not whether what flows through them is clean.

The two questions come apart in both directions. An argument can be perfectly valid with a false premise: *All engineers earn well; your cousin is an engineer; so your cousin earns well* — flawless pipes, but if the first premise is false, the conclusion is not established. And an argument can have entirely true premises and still be invalid: *Some arranged marriages are happy; your family wants to arrange yours; so yours will be happy* — every premise can be granted and the conclusion still does not follow, because "some" carries no guarantee about *yours*. Only when both tests pass — valid structure, true premises — does the argument achieve the :::glossary term="Soundness"::: you met in Chapter 1, and only soundness obliges you to accept the conclusion.

:::definition term="Validity"
A property of an argument's structure: an argument is valid when the conclusion follows necessarily from the premises — when there is no possible situation in which the premises are all true and the conclusion false. A valid argument can run on false premises, and true premises can sit in an invalid structure. Validity plus true premises equals soundness, and only soundness settles anything.
:::

Why does this bookkeeping matter at 11 p.m. in a group chat? Because almost every heated exchange confuses the two levels. One person attacks a conclusion by shouting at a premise the argument does not even need; another thinks that because one premise wobbled, the opposite conclusion is proven. The difference between *your premise is false* and *your conclusion doesn't follow* is the difference between a fuse and a leak: you fix them differently.

## 5.2 The gallery of familiar failures

A :::glossary term="Fallacy"::: is an argument-shaped move that persuades without supporting — a pattern that *feels* like reasoning while doing none. What follows is not an exhaustive catalogue but the five you will actually meet this month. As you read, resist the first temptation, which is to recognise your relatives. The exercise only pays when you also recognise yourself.

The :::glossary term="Ad hominem"::: attacks the speaker instead of the claim. *You've been in Germany three years and you think you can lecture us about our politics?* Nothing about the argument has been touched; the arguer has been disqualified instead. It travels both ways across the generational border — *what would you know, you've never left the district* is the same fallacy wearing the other uniform. The crucial subtlety: a speaker's bias or ignorance is sometimes *relevant* — Chapter 4 said testimony must be weighed by the speaker's position to know. The fallacy is not mentioning the person; it is treating facts about the person as if they refuted an argument that stands or falls on its own premises.

The :::glossary term="Straw man"::: refutes a distorted copy of the opponent's claim. Someone says *I'm not sure I want the family to shortlist candidates before I've met anyone* and is answered as if they had said *so our parents' whole marriage was a mistake?* The copy is easier to defeat than the original, which is exactly why the substitution happens — usually not from malice but from hurt, because a question about a practice lands as an attack on the people who lived it. Chapter 1's distinction between examining and rejecting is the antidote.

The *appeal to tradition* argues that a practice's age is its justification — Chapter 1's "despotism of custom" in debate dress: *this is how it has been done for generations*, offered as if it settled the matter rather than opened it. Its sibling, the *appeal to nature*, treats "natural" as a synonym for "good" — *it's natural for a woman to put family first; it's not natural for a man to stay home with children* — though nature also includes cholera, and much that we rightly value, from insulin to the video call that keeps your family within reach, is proudly unnatural. In both, a description (old, natural) is silently promoted to a verdict (right), without the argument that would have to connect them.[^naturalistic]

The :::glossary term="False dilemma"::: compresses a spectrum into two options: *either you respect your family or you live for yourself; either she's a career woman or she's a family woman; either you're with us or you've become German.* The falsehood is not in either option but in the word *either* — the menu has been shortened before being handed to you. Most real decisions live in the amputated middle, which is why this fallacy does more quiet damage in families than any other: it converts negotiable situations into loyalty tests.

## 5.3 The motte and the bailey

One more pattern deserves its own section, because it is subtler and everywhere once seen. Mediaeval castles had a *bailey* — the pleasant, productive courtyard where daily life happened — and a *motte*, a cramped defensive tower for when attackers came. A :::glossary term="Motte-and-bailey"::: argument works the same way.[^shackel] The arguer normally advances a bold, contentious claim (the bailey): *you must marry within our community, anything else ends in disaster.* When challenged, they retreat to a modest, almost undeniable claim (the motte): *I'm only saying shared background makes some things easier — you can't deny that!* You cannot deny the motte. But the moment the challenge passes, life resumes in the bailey, as if the modest claim had defended the bold one.

The pattern is even-handed in whom it serves. The traditionalist's bailey may be *disobedience destroys families*, with the motte *family opinions deserve respect*; the moderniser's bailey may be *every traditional expectation is oppression*, with the motte *people should be free to choose*. What you are watching for is the *retreat and re-advance*: a claim that shrinks under fire and silently regrows afterwards. The repair is gentle and devastating: name the two claims apart. *I agree shared background makes some things easier. Do you want to defend the stronger thing you said — that anything else ends in disaster?* Most motte-and-bailey structures cannot survive being described.

:::check reread="5.2"
question: In the group chat, your cousin writes: "I think we should at least discuss whether the dowry-adjacent gift-giving at weddings has become too expensive." An aunt replies: "So you want to abolish our weddings and shame every family that ever gave gifts?" Which failure is this?
- Ad hominem — the aunt attacked the cousin's character rather than the claim.
- Straw man — the aunt replaced a modest proposal (discuss whether costs have grown) with an extreme copy (abolish weddings, shame families) and answered the copy.
- Appeal to nature — the aunt claimed gift-giving is natural and therefore good.
- Valid deduction — the extreme version really does follow from the cousin's proposal.
answer: 2
explanation: The cousin proposed a discussion about scale; the reply answers a demand for abolition that no one made. Substituting an exaggerated, easier-to-attack version of a claim and refuting that is the straw man. It is not ad hominem — the attack targets a (distorted) position, not the person — and the diagnosis matters because the repair here is specific: restate the original claim and ask for a response to that.
:::

## 5.4 The principle of charity

Now point the machinery at yourself — because spotting fallacies in others is cheap, and a person armed only with fallacy names becomes a worse arguer, not a better one. The discipline that prevents this is the :::glossary term="Principle of charity":::: before you answer anyone, interpret what they said in its *strongest* reasonable form — repair the obvious slips, fill the unstated premise generously, assume intelligence and good faith until shown otherwise — and answer *that*.

:::argument title="The case for arguing against the strongest version"
conclusion: You should interpret and answer the strongest reasonable version of any position you oppose, not its weakest expression.
premise: Your goal in genuine disagreement is to find the truth, not to win the exchange — a defeated weak version leaves you no wiser about whether the position is right.
premise: A strong version of the opposing position usually exists whether or not this opponent expressed it, and truth requires answering the position, not the person's worst sentence.
premise: Only an opponent who feels accurately heard will engage with your actual reasons, so charity is the only strategy that actually changes minds — including, when you are wrong, your own.
:::

:::counter title="Objection: charity can be naivety with good manners" to="the case for arguing against the strongest version"
The strongest objection comes from experience, not theory. Some interlocutors are not reasoning in good faith: they move goalposts, weaponise your patience, and treat every charitable repair you make as ground gained. In such hands the principle of charity becomes unpaid labour — you build their argument better than they did, and they pocket the improvement. There is also a power dimension: those with less standing in a family — often the young, often women, sometimes the son whose struggles are unspeakable — are *already* expected to be endlessly understanding, and telling them to be more charitable can reinforce the servility Chapter 4 warned against. Both points are right, and they discipline the principle rather than destroy it: charity is a policy for engaging *arguments*, and it is compatible with firmly exiting an exchange where no argument is actually being had. Steel-man the position; you owe no infinite patience to the performance.
:::

## 5.5 Disagreeing without contempt

Why go to all this trouble for people who will not return it? Mill supplies the deepest reason, and it is selfish in the best sense: the person who knows only their own side of a question, he argued, does not really know even that — their belief stays untested, a slogan rather than an understanding, until it has met the opposing view in its strongest form.[^mill5] Your disagreeing relatives and group-chat opponents are a free service: the only people motivated to find holes in your reasoning. Contempt cancels the service. The moment you decide the other side is stupid or wicked, you have not won; you have unsubscribed from the only mechanism that could improve your own position — and begun building Chapter 4's pre-discrediting shield from the inside.

Here is the chapter's practical residue, for the next ninety-four-message night. Argue about claims, not persons; when tempted to type *you always* or *your generation*, return to the sentence actually written. Restate the other side's point to their satisfaction before answering it — in a chat this costs one message and changes everything. Locate the disagreement precisely: false premise, or broken connection? Much heat comes from two people defending different parts of the same argument. Refuse shortened menus. And when a claim retreats under fire, let it — but name the bailey before life resumes in it. None of this guarantees agreement — only something better: that whatever disagreement remains is *real*, about the world rather than about who misheard whom, and that the people on the other side of it are still people you can eat with. In a family stretched across continents, that is not a small technique; it is load-bearing.

:::check reread="5.1"
question: Your friend argues: "Every argument my father makes for staying in the family business contains a fallacy or a false premise. Therefore staying in the family business is the wrong choice." What has your friend overlooked?
- Nothing — refuting every stated argument for a conclusion establishes that the conclusion is false.
- That bad arguments can exist for true conclusions: demolishing the father's stated arguments shows the conclusion is unsupported by them, not that it is false — a sound case for staying might still exist, unstated.
- That fathers cannot commit fallacies, so the premise of the friend's own argument must be wrong.
- That validity and soundness are the same property, so the question cannot arise.
answer: 2
explanation: From "the arguments offered are bad" it follows only that the conclusion is not established by them — not that it is false. A true conclusion can be defended incompetently, and the principle of charity asks precisely this: is there a stronger case for the father's view than the one he stated? Treating the refutation of weak arguments as refutation of the position is itself a recognisable mistake — sometimes called the fallacy fallacy.
:::

:::reflect
Find a real disagreement you are currently inside — in the family group, with a partner, with a colleague — and do the exercise you least want: write the *strongest* version of the other side's position, in their voice, until you are confident they would sign it. Then locate, in one sentence, the exact point where you and that strongest version part ways: which premise do you deny, or which inference do you reject? Finally, reread your own last three messages in the dispute and name honestly any move from this chapter's gallery that you yourself made. If the answer is "none," check again; almost no one's answer is truly none.
:::

[^aristotle5]: Aristotle's *Sophistical Refutations* (4th century BCE) is the founding catalogue of fallacious reasoning — arguments that appear genuine but are not — and the ancestor of every list of fallacies since, including this one.
[^naturalistic]: The gap between descriptive claims and evaluative conclusions — from "is" to "ought" — was pressed classically by David Hume in *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40), Book III; both the appeal to tradition and the appeal to nature founder on it.
[^shackel]: The castle analogy and the name were introduced by the philosopher Nicholas Shackel in "The Vacuity of Postmodernist Methodology" (2005); the pattern is far older than its name.
[^mill5]: John Stuart Mill, *On Liberty* (1859), chapter 2: the person "who knows only his own side of the case, knows little of that," and a belief unexamined against the strongest contrary view is held as dead dogma rather than living truth. The same book supplied Chapter 1's "despotism of custom" — sibling arguments about what makes a belief genuinely one's own.
