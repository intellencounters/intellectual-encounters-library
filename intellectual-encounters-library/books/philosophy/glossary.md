# Glossary

## Argument

In philosophy, not a quarrel but a structure: a set of statements in which one or more *premises* are offered as reasons to accept a *conclusion*. An argument is a public object — it can be inspected by anyone, part by part, to see whether the reasons genuinely support the claim. Distinguishing an argument (which asks to be tested) from an opinion (which asks only to be held) is the first move in thinking clearly.

## Premise

A starting claim that an argument leans on — a reason offered in support of its conclusion. An argument may have one premise or many. Each premise can be examined on its own: is it actually true? A single false premise can bring down an otherwise well-built argument.

## Conclusion

The claim an argument is trying to establish — the thing you are being asked to accept on the strength of the premises. Isolating the conclusion from the premises lets you ask the two separate questions that matter: are the premises true, and do they really support this conclusion rather than merely sitting near it?

## Soundness

The standard a good argument must meet. An argument is *sound* when both conditions hold: its premises are all true, and the premises genuinely support (in the strongest case, force) the conclusion. Loud confidence, long tradition, or strong feeling can accompany an argument without making it sound; only true premises plus a real connection to the conclusion do that.

## Justification

The reasons that would show a belief or practice is warranted — the answer to "why should we?" as opposed to the merely descriptive "what do we do?" Justification is what a custom may or may not turn out to have underneath it. A practice can be widespread, old, and deeply felt and still lack justification; examining it is how you find out which.

## Despotism of custom

John Stuart Mill's phrase (in *On Liberty*, 1859) for the way settled habit can rule a life without ever giving reasons — the way a despot rules by say-so alone. The worry is not that customs are bad but that their age and reach get mistaken for justification, so that "how it has always been done" quietly replaces thought. Mill's target is the *manner* in which a practice is held — by habit or by examination — not the content of any particular tradition.

## Autonomy

A person's standing as the author of their own life — the capacity to give their own considered answer on the choices that shape it. Autonomy is not the freedom to feel no pressure (no life offers that) but the narrower, realer freedom to know which pressures one has weighed and endorsed as one's own, and which one is merely carrying by default. A choice is autonomous when it has passed through one's own reasons on the way to being made — which can be true even when it aligns with what others wanted.

## Consequentialism

The family of ethical theories holding that the rightness of an act depends entirely on its consequences — how much good and harm it produces. Utilitarianism, its best-known form, says: maximise overall well-being, counting each affected person's interests equally, including your own. Its strength is impartiality and honesty about trade-offs; its characteristic danger is appearing to license the sacrifice of a person whenever the sums favour it.

## Deontology

The family of ethical theories holding that some acts are right or wrong in themselves — by principle or duty — regardless of consequences. In Kant's version, one must act only on principles one could will everyone to follow, and always treat persons as ends in themselves, never merely as means. Its strength is that it protects individuals from being sacrificed to a total; its difficulty is that duties can collide without the theory always saying which one yields.

## Virtue ethics

The ethical tradition, rooted in Aristotle, that takes the central question to be not "which act is right?" but "what character is good, and what would a person of good character do?" Virtues such as courage, honesty, and generosity are stable traits lying between extremes of excess and deficiency, formed by habit over time. Its strength is realism about how moral life is actually lived and grown; its difficulty is specifying what the virtuous person would do exactly when you most need the answer.

## Filial duty

The obligation to honour, care for, and (on stronger versions) defer to the parents and elders who made one's life possible. Among the most widely held moral ideas across human history — central to Confucian ethics and woven through the duties of the *Bhagavad Gita*, among many traditions. Filial duty names something real and is not dissolved by calling it old-fashioned; the live question is how far it extends, and how it weighs against a person's authorship of their own central choices.

## Eudaimonia

The ancient Greek term, central to Aristotle, usually translated "flourishing" or "living well" — the good life assessed as a whole, across its full length, rather than as momentary pleasure or feeling. On this view the good life is not a mood that befalls you but an activity you carry out well over years: the exercise of your human capacities in accordance with virtue. Aristotle held it also requires some external goods and good fortune, so circumstances are not nothing.

## Determinism

The thesis that, given the past and the laws of nature, only one future is actually possible — so that every event, including every human choice, is the product of prior causes. If true in the strong sense, it raises the question of whether any choice is genuinely free. Its scope is total: whatever it implies about your responsibility, it implies equally about everyone else's, which is one reason it is hard to turn into practical guidance.

## Free will

The capacity, if we have it, to make genuine choices for which we can be held responsible — choices that are in some meaningful sense up to us. Whether free will exists, and whether it could survive the truth of determinism, is one of philosophy's oldest unresolved disputes. *Compatibilists* hold that freedom worth the name is consistent with determinism (freedom as acting on, and endorsing, one's own reasons); *hard determinists* deny there is any free will; *libertarians* about free will hold that some choices are genuinely undetermined. The debate remains open.

## Knowledge

In the philosophical tradition descending from Plato, knowledge is standardly analysed as belief that is *true* and *well-grounded* — held for reasons that genuinely connect it to the way things are, not merely by luck, habit, or trust in whoever spoke loudest. The strength of a conviction is a fact about the believer; knowledge requires in addition a fact about the world and a sound route between the two. Confidence, by itself, is evidence of nothing except confidence.

## Testimony

Knowledge accepted on the word of another person — the source of almost everything any individual knows, from history to science to family memory. Testimony is indispensable, not a defect; the epistemic work lies in *calibrating* trust to the speaker's actual position to know, claim by claim. A forwarded message is testimony at its weakest: stripped of author, date, evidence, and accountability, so that every link in the chain vouches for sincerity while no link checks the claim.

## Updating

Revising a belief's degree of confidence in response to evidence, rather than flipping it on or off. Associated with Thomas Bayes, whose everyday moral is: weigh new evidence by asking how much more expected it is if the claim is true than if it is false. Evidence you would encounter just as readily either way — like a sensational forward — carries almost no weight. Treating beliefs as degrees makes changing one's mind ordinary accounting rather than public defeat.

## Intellectual humility

An accurate, working sense of the limits of what one knows, and a willingness to treat one's own certainty as one more thing needing checking. Not thinking little of oneself, and sharply distinct from *servility* — deferring to whoever holds rank regardless of evidence. Humility defers to truth; servility defers to hierarchy. It sits between two failures: arrogance treats my beliefs as beyond question, servility treats someone else's as beyond question; humility treats no one's as beyond question.

## Echo chamber

A social structure in which contrary voices are not merely absent but *pre-discredited*: members are taught in advance that outsiders are fools, liars, or enemies, so objections arrive already defused. Distinct from a mere epistemic *bubble*, where opposing views are simply not heard and exposure is the repair; an echo chamber neutralises exposure itself. The distinction is drawn from C. Thi Nguyen's work. A practical test for either: what did this group last change its mind about, and what would dissent cost here?

## Validity

A property of an argument's structure: an argument is valid when the conclusion follows necessarily from the premises — no possible situation makes the premises all true and the conclusion false. Validity concerns the plumbing, not the water: a valid argument can run on false premises, and true premises can sit in an invalid structure. Validity plus true premises equals soundness.

## Fallacy

An argument-shaped move that persuades without supporting: a pattern that feels like reasoning while doing none — attacking the speaker, distorting the claim, shortening the menu of options. Fallacies are worth learning to spot, with one caution: refuting a fallacious argument shows a conclusion *unsupported*, not false (bad arguments can exist for true conclusions), and fallacy-naming aimed only at others makes a worse arguer, not a better one.

## Ad hominem

The fallacy of attacking the speaker instead of the claim — treating facts about a person (their age, residence, education, motives) as if they refuted an argument that stands or falls on its own premises. Distinct from legitimately weighing a *testifier's* reliability: who is speaking matters when the question is whether to trust their word, not when the question is whether their stated argument holds.

## Straw man

The fallacy of refuting a distorted, weakened copy of an opponent's claim instead of the claim itself — answering "I'd like to meet someone before the family shortlists candidates" as if it were "our parents' marriage was a mistake." The substitution is often driven by hurt rather than malice, which makes it no less a substitution. Its antidote is the principle of charity.

## False dilemma

The fallacy of compressing a spectrum of options into exactly two and demanding a choice — "either you respect your family or you live for yourself." The falsehood lies not in either option but in the shortened menu. Especially corrosive in families, where it converts negotiable situations into loyalty tests; most real decisions live in the amputated middle.

## Motte-and-bailey

An argumentative pattern named after the mediaeval castle: an arguer normally advances a bold, contentious claim (the bailey), retreats under challenge to a modest, near-undeniable one (the motte), then resumes the bold claim once the challenge passes — as if defending the modest claim had defended the bold one. Named by the philosopher Nicholas Shackel. The repair is to name the two claims apart and ask which one is actually being defended.

## Principle of charity

The discipline of interpreting what someone said in its strongest reasonable form — repairing obvious slips, filling unstated premises generously — and answering *that*, rather than the weakest available version. Grounded in the aim of finding truth rather than winning exchanges: a defeated weak version leaves the strong version standing. Charity is a policy toward arguments; it does not oblige infinite patience toward interlocutors who are not actually reasoning.

## Deprivation account

The view, classically stated by Thomas Nagel, that death is bad for the one who dies not as an experience (it is not an experience) but as a *deprivation*: it takes away the goods — time, relationships, projects, futures — the person would otherwise have had. Its key move against Epicurus is that some harms need not be experienced to be harms, as when a person is betrayed without ever learning of it. It also explains why an early death and a death after a full life are not equally "nothing."

## Narrative identity

The idea, developed by Alasdair MacIntyre among others, that a self is held together over time the way a story is — by meaning, not merely memory — and that lives are evaluated partly by their *shape*: sequence, development, and the resolution of threads, not just summed totals of good and bad hours. Contested: critics such as Galen Strawson argue that many people are genuinely "episodic" and that narrativity is a tool, not a requirement for a good life.

## Memento mori

Latin: "remember that you die." The long tradition — Stoic practice, later centuries of European art — of keeping mortality deliberately in view, not to darken life but to focus it, the way a frame focuses a picture. Connected to the ancient characterisation of philosophy (from Plato's *Phaedo* onward) as a preparation for death: finitude is what gives choices their weight.
