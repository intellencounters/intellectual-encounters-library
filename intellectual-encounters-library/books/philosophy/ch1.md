# 1. What Philosophy Is For

It is a Sunday, and you are on a video call. The kitchen at home is loud behind your mother; someone is asking whether you have eaten. Then the real subject arrives, the way it always does — not as a question but as a settled fact. *You should be married by now. This is just how it's done.* You are tired from a closing shift, you have an exam on Tuesday, and you can feel the old reflex: either argue and be called ungrateful, or nod and feel a little less like the author of your own life.

This book is not going to tell you to marry, or not to marry, to stay in Germany or go home, to send money or keep it. It has no idea what you should do, and anyone who claims to know your life better than you from the outside is selling something. What it offers is smaller and more durable: a way of thinking that makes the choices genuinely yours easier to see, and the pressures around them easier to weigh without either swallowing them whole or throwing them away in anger.

That is what philosophy, stripped of its intimidating reputation, is for. It is not a body of secret answers. It is a set of tools for asking, of any claim at all — including the ones you inherited and the ones you feel most strongly — *and why?*

## 1.1 The sentence that ends conversations

"This is just how it's done" is a remarkable sentence, because it is built to stop thought rather than start it. Notice what it does. It reports a fact about behaviour — that people around you do a certain thing — and dresses it up as a reason for you to do it too. But a description of what people do is not, by itself, a reason for you to do it. Everyone in your building takes the U-Bahn; that is not an argument that you should. The step from "this is how it is done" to "this is how you should do it" is exactly the step philosophy asks you to slow down and look at.

:::case title="A call that will feel familiar"
A composite scene, drawn from patterns many readers abroad will recognise. A daughter working nights in a warehouse near Leipzig is told, warmly and without malice, that a match has been discussed at home and that she should be grateful, because *this is simply what a good family does at your age*. A son studying for a master's in Frankfurt is told, in the same breath of love, that a real man would already be sending more money back and thinking of a wedding. Neither parent is a villain. Both are passing on something they themselves received. The pressure is real, the affection is real, and the reasoning underneath has never once been said out loud — because it has never had to be.
:::

The love in that scene is not the problem, and this book will not pretend it is. The difficulty is narrower: a genuine reason and a strong feeling of certainty can feel identical from the inside, and telling them apart is a skill. Philosophy is the practice of that skill.

## 1.2 Opinions, and the thing that is not an opinion

We use the word "argument" for a fight — raised voices, a slammed door. Philosophy uses it for the opposite: a calm, checkable structure. An :::glossary term="Argument"::: in this sense is not a quarrel and not a mere opinion. It is a set of reasons offered in support of a claim, laid out so that anyone can inspect whether the reasons actually hold the claim up.

The difference matters because an opinion asks only to be *held*, while an argument asks to be *tested*. "I feel you should stay in Germany" is an opinion; it stands or falls on nobody's inspection but your own. "You should stay in Germany, because your earning power here funds everyone's plans and leaving would undo five years of progress" is an argument — and the moment it is an argument, you can ask whether each part is true and whether the parts really lead where they claim to.

:::definition term="Argument"
A structured set of statements in which one or more **premises** are offered as reasons to accept a **conclusion**. An argument is not an assertion of feeling and not a dispute; it is a public object that can be examined. Its job is to make visible *why* a claim should be believed, so that the "why" can be checked rather than merely felt.
:::

This is the first real freedom the subject offers. Once a demand made of you is stated as an argument, it stops being a wall of authority and becomes something with parts — and parts can be examined one at a time.

## 1.3 Premises, conclusions, and where they can break

Every argument has two kinds of moving part. A :::glossary term="Premise"::: is a reason being offered — a starting claim the argument leans on. A :::glossary term="Conclusion"::: is the claim those reasons are meant to support — the thing you are being asked to accept. Take the pressure from the call and set it out honestly:

:::argument title="The family-timing argument, stated fairly"
conclusion: You should marry soon.
premise: A person your age in our family has always married by now.
premise: Doing what the family has always done keeps the family secure and respected.
premise: You should do what keeps the family secure and respected.
:::

Laid out like this, the argument becomes workable rather than overwhelming — and you can see it has exactly two places it can break. Either a premise is false, or the premises are all true but do not actually force the conclusion. A good argument needs both: true premises *and* a real connection between them and the conclusion. When it has both, philosophers call it **sound**; :::glossary term="Soundness"::: is the standard the family-timing argument has to meet, not just loud confidence.

Look at the second premise. Does doing what the family has always done reliably keep it secure — or did the world the custom was built for (a village, a fixed economy, no daughters on night shifts in another country) simply stop existing? Look at the third: even granting security matters, is *your* marriage timing the thing securing it, or is your salary? You are not being disrespectful by asking. You are doing the one thing that treats the argument as serious enough to be true.

:::check reread="1.3"
question: Your uncle says: "Everyone in our community marries by thirty, so you should too." Treated as an argument, where is its main weak point?
- It has no premises at all, so there is nothing to examine.
- It moves from a premise about what people *do* to a conclusion about what you *should* do, without supplying the missing reason that would connect them.
- Its conclusion is false, which automatically makes the whole argument invalid.
- Arguments about marriage cannot be examined, because they are personal.
answer: 2
explanation: The premise reports a custom (what people do); the conclusion is a claim about what you ought to do. Nothing yet bridges that gap. Naming the missing link is not rudeness — it is the exact move that turns an unanswerable demand into a claim you can actually weigh.
:::

## 1.4 Custom is not justification

Here is the crux of the chapter. A custom answers the question *what do we do here?* A :::glossary term="Justification"::: — the deeper thing — answers *why should we?* These come apart far more often than we notice, and the gap between them is where an entire tradition of thinking lives.

The nineteenth-century philosopher John Stuart Mill gave the danger its sharpest name. He worried that in any settled society the sheer weight of "how it is done" could quietly replace thought altogether — that people would mistake the deep grooves of habit for the voice of reason, and never notice the substitution.[^mill]

:::glossary term="Despotism of custom"::: is his phrase for it, and *despotism* is chosen with care. A despot rules without having to give reasons; his say-so is the end of the matter. Custom can rule the same way — governing marriages, careers, money, and reputation not through any argument that would survive daylight, but simply through the force of always-having-been. The point is not that customs are bad. Many encode hard-won wisdom. The point is that a custom's *age* and *reach* are not themselves reasons, and treating them as reasons is precisely how a mind stops being its own.

:::evidence source="Mill, J. S. (1859). On Liberty, ch. 3."
Mill argues that "the despotism of custom is everywhere the standing hindrance to human advancement," and that a person who lets the world choose their plan of life for them needs no faculty but "the ape-like one of imitation." His concern is not with any particular tradition but with the *manner* of holding one: beliefs and practices carried by habit alone, never having been examined by the person living inside them, are held without their owner ever having made them their own.
:::

To relocate for work, to date across a culture, to negotiate with an office where a German colleague's bluntness still lands like rudeness — modern life abroad is a daily audit of which inherited scripts still fit and which quietly stopped fitting the day you boarded the plane. Mill's warning is simply that you are allowed to run that audit, and that running it is not betrayal.

## 1.5 Examining is not rejecting

This is the most important thing in the chapter, and the easiest to get wrong. To examine an inherited practice is not to have decided against it. Those are different acts, and collapsing them is the mistake that makes the whole enterprise feel like an attack on the people you love.

When you ask *why* your family holds a custom, three things can happen. You may find a genuine reason underneath — that an arranged introduction, for instance, is a way of pooling the judgement of people who know you and want your good — and endorse the custom now as *yours*, no longer merely inherited but chosen. You may find the reason once held but no longer fits your life, and adapt it. Or you may find no reason at all beyond "it has always been so," and set it gently down. Examination does not predict the verdict. It only guarantees the verdict is yours.

:::argument title="Why examining an inherited script is not rejecting it"
conclusion: Questioning a family custom is compatible with — and can even deepen — your commitment to it.
premise: To question a practice is to ask what reasons support it, not to assume it has none.
premise: A practice you keep after finding its reasons good is one you now hold on purpose, not merely by inheritance.
premise: Holding a commitment on purpose is a stronger and more honest form of commitment than holding it by default.
:::

:::counter title="Objection: some goods die under the microscope" to="the claim that examining is harmless"
The honest objection deserves its full weight. Some genuine goods are not built to survive interrogation, and pretending otherwise is its own kind of naivety. Trust, belonging, the felt authority of a parent, the quiet confidence of a shared way of life — these can be real goods that a relentless demand for justification corrodes, the way asking a friend to *prove* every hour that they love you can hollow out the very love you are testing. A person who subjects every inherited tie to cross-examination may end up freer and lonelier, holding a fistful of justified propositions and no home. The reply is not to stop examining but to examine honestly — which means counting the value of trust and belonging *among* the reasons on the scale, not ruling them out as unserious. To weigh what a custom gives you is still to weigh; it is examination done with your eyes open, not examination refused.
:::

Notice that the counterargument does not defeat the chapter — it disciplines it. That back-and-forth, each side stated at its strongest before any verdict, is the whole method. You will meet it on every page of this book, because it is the only way to think about contested things without lying to yourself.

:::uncertainty title="A claim I will not smuggle in"
It is tempting to assert that people who examine their inherited commitments end up more satisfied with the ones they keep, or that unexamined obligations breed more resentment over time. Both are plausible and both are the kind of thing psychology studies — but I do not have a specific, verifiable finding in hand, and this book will not dress an intuition as data — citation needed — verify before publishing. Treat Section 1.5's claim as a piece of reasoning about the *kind* of commitment examination produces, not a measured fact about outcomes.
:::

:::check reread="1.4"
question: A friend says: "If you start asking *why* about your traditions, you've basically already rejected them." What has he got wrong?
- Nothing — asking why a practice exists is the same act as deciding to abandon it.
- He has collapsed two different acts: examining a practice (asking its reasons) and rejecting it (concluding it has none worth keeping). Examination can end in a stronger yes.
- He is right that traditions should never be questioned, only obeyed.
- He has confused a premise with a conclusion.
answer: 2
explanation: Examining and rejecting are distinct. To ask a custom's reasons is to open a question, not to answer it against the custom. One honest outcome of examination is to keep the practice — now held on purpose rather than by default, which is a firmer commitment, not a weaker one.
:::

## 1.6 What you can do with this

You now have the core instrument. When a demand arrives — from a parent, a partner, a boss, a WhatsApp group, or the loudest voice in your own head — you can do four things in order. State it as an argument, so it has parts. Separate its premises from its conclusion. Ask of each premise whether it is *true*, and of the whole whether the premises really force the conclusion or merely sit beside it. And keep custom and justification apart, so that "this is just how it's done" can no longer end a conversation you have every right to continue.

None of this makes the choices easy. Your :::glossary term="Autonomy"::: — your standing as the author of your own life — is not the freedom to feel no pressure; that freedom does not exist for anyone. It is the narrower, realer freedom to know which pressures you have actually weighed and endorsed, and which you are merely carrying because no one ever set them down. The next chapter turns to the hardest of those pressures: what you genuinely owe, and to whom.

:::reflect
Choose one expectation currently pressing on you — about marriage, money sent home, career, or return. Write it out as a three-line argument: two premises and the conclusion it wants you to accept. Then, in one honest sentence for each premise, say whether you think it is *true* — not whether you like it, whether it is true. Finally, write the single sentence you would need to hear to be genuinely persuaded, and keep it: you will test it against Chapters 2 and 3.
:::

[^mill]: The classic source for the gap between custom and justification is John Stuart Mill, *On Liberty* (1859), especially chapter 3, "Of Individuality," where the phrase "the despotism of custom" appears. Mill's target is the manner in which a belief is held — by habit versus by examination — not any particular tradition's content.
