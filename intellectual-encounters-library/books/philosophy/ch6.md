# 6. Death, Time, and the Shape of a Life

The call comes on a Tuesday, in the dead hour between your shift and your lecture. Your grandmother — the one who fed you rice with her hands long after you could feed yourself, who never quite understood what country you were in — is gone. The funeral is tomorrow; the flight you can neither afford nor get leave for is fourteen hours; so you will attend on a phone propped against a window, watching smoke rise through a screen while a German afternoon continues outside, indifferent and mild. In the days after, something shifts that you do not have words for. The arguments about marriage timelines and transfers keep arriving, but they sound different now — smaller and, strangely, more urgent at once.

This final chapter is about that shift. Death is not one topic among others in philosophy; it is the frame that gives every other topic its weight. A choice matters partly *because* you cannot make all choices; you cannot make all choices because the time is finite; and the time is finite because you are going to die. This book owes you an honest chapter about that — not a consoling one — and then it owes you an ending.

## 6.1 The deadline that makes choices weigh

Begin with a thought experiment: unlimited time, every option preservable forever. Would whether to marry this person, take this job, return home this decade, still have weight? With infinite time, nothing is ever forgone: every road not taken could be taken later, every deferral is costless, and "not yet" becomes an answer to everything. Bernard Williams famously argued that an endless life would not be a lucky life stretched out but something stranger — a life in which choice has lost its point, because choosing was always the art of spending what cannot be replaced.[^williams]

You need not settle the question to take the working point: *scarcity is not the enemy of meaning but one of its conditions.* The night shifts feel heavy precisely because the years they occupy are not refundable; the decision about return feels enormous because "later" quietly spends a currency with a fixed supply. This is why the family's urgency about your life is not irrational at its core — the elders pressing you about time have stood closer to the deadline; what they misprice is usually not the scarcity but *whose* scarce life it is. Seneca's accusation still lands on every generation: we guard our money like misers and spend our time like millionaires, though only time cannot be earned back.[^seneca]

## 6.2 Epicurus: the argument that death cannot hurt you

Against all this weight stands one of the most audacious arguments ever made: Epicurus argued to a student that the fear of death — the oldest fear there is — rests on a confusion and dissolves under analysis.[^epicurus]

:::argument title="Epicurus' argument that death is nothing to us"
conclusion: Death is not bad for the person who dies, and so is not a fitting object of fear.
premise: Something can be good or bad for a person only if that person exists to be affected by it.
premise: While you exist, your death has not happened; once your death has happened, you no longer exist.
premise: Therefore there is no time at which you and your death coexist — no subject is ever present to be harmed by it.
:::

His Roman follower Lucretius added a second blade, the *symmetry argument*: you do not regard the eternity before your birth as a misfortune you suffered; why regard the eternity after your death, its mirror image, any differently?[^lucretius] Notice what the arguments are *for*: Epicurus believed the dread of death poisons life, and that a person freed of it could finally live. Steel-manned, this is a liberation argument, and generations have found peace in it. A dying person who says *I was not afraid before I was born* is not reciting a fallacy; they are using one of humanity's oldest medicines.

:::counter title="Objection: death harms by deprivation, not by being experienced" to="Epicurus' argument that death is nothing to us"
The standard modern reply, stated classically by Thomas Nagel, denies the first premise: some things are bad for you without being experienced by you. A friend who betrays you skilfully behind your back, so that you never learn of it, has still wronged and harmed you — a harm can consist in what you are deprived of, not only in what you suffer. Death, on this :::glossary term="Deprivation account":::, is bad not as an experience (there Epicurus is right) but as a subtraction: the years, reunions, degrees, and unmet grandchildren that would otherwise have been yours. This also answers Lucretius: the time before your birth deprived you of nothing you were ever going to have — you could not have existed earlier and still been you — whereas death takes a future genuinely en route to you. The debate remains live and Epicureans have replies, but the deprivation account explains what Epicurus cannot: why a death at ninety and a death at twenty-five are not equally "nothing," and why grief for the young dead is not a confusion but arithmetic.
:::

## 6.3 Regret, and the person you are sending letters to

Bring the deadline down from eternity to something closer: not your death, but your *future self* — the person of sixty who will inherit every decision made on this night shift. Derek Parfit argued that the relation between you-now and you-later is looser than we assume, more like the relation between close relatives than strict identity.[^parfit] That reframes prudence as something warmer: the sixty-year-old is not simply *you*, cashing your cheques, but someone real who will live with your choices and cannot yet speak. Every decision this book has examined — the transfer, the marriage, the return — is also a letter to that person.

Regret is the name for the letters that come back marked *return to sender*, and it needs one distinction. There is regret that is information: the sting of a value violated by a choice you made — painful, useful, the future self writing back. And there is regret that is fantasy: mourning the life where you kept every option — the career *and* the early family, the loyalty *and* the freedom, both countries at once. No such life was on the menu; scarcity was the condition of the choosing, and measuring your real life against an impossible composite is not conscience but a category mistake. The practical test: *does this regret name a choice I would actually unmake, at its actual price?* If yes, it may be actionable — apologies, reversals, changed course. If no, it is not regret about your life but grief about finitude itself, and it belongs in Section 6.2, where you have the tools for it.

:::uncertainty title="What the dying actually regret"
Lists of "the top regrets of the dying" circulate widely — worked less, stayed truer to oneself, kept friendships alive. The best-known versions derive from a palliative nurse's memoir, not systematic research, and how far they generalise across cultures — including to strong family-duty traditions — is an empirical question this book will not hand-wave — citation needed — verify before publishing. This section's distinction between informative and fantastical regret stands on its reasoning, not on any survey of deathbeds.
:::

## 6.4 Narrative identity: a life as a told thing

Why does the video-call funeral hurt the way it does? Not only grief, but *interruption*: a chapter of your story closed on another continent without you in the scene. That instinct — that a life is not a heap of hours but a *story*, with chapters and threads that demand completion — is what philosophers call :::glossary term="Narrative identity":::. Alasdair MacIntyre argued that we can only answer *what should I do?* because we already carry an answer to *what story am I part of?* — and that a self is held together over time the way a narrative is: by meaning, not merely memory.[^macintyre]

This idea explains what mere accounting cannot. It explains why sequence matters: a decade of struggle that leads into arrival reads as one life, the same decades in reverse order as a tragedy, though the total hours of hardship are identical. It explains why the immigrant's life is narratively *hard*: you are a minor character in the family epic back home and the protagonist of an unwritten story here — and much of the pressure this book has examined is, at bottom, a dispute over who holds the pen. And it explains the special wrongness of a life ended early: what is lost is not only years but *shape* — the sentence stopped in the middle.

But the narrative view has an honest limit: not every good life reads well, and the demand for a tidy arc can become one more script imposed from outside — every marriage a milestone, every migration a triumph-or-failure. Some philosophers reject narrativity as a requirement, insisting a life can be fully worth living episode by episode, without plot.[^strawson] Hold the idea as a tool rather than a law: you are entitled to edit your story, and also entitled, some seasons, to put the pen down.

explanation: On a pure accounting view the lives tie; the widespread judgement that they differ is evidence that we evaluate lives partly narratively — by arc, not just amount — linking back to eudaimonia in Chapter 3. Note the caution: sensitivity to shape is a feature of how lives are valued, not a duty to force your life into a tidy plot.

## 6.5 Whole, not merely long

What does it all come to? A change in the question. The frightened question is *how do I get more time?* — which has no good answer, which is why it makes people frantic and easy to sell to. The examined question is Seneca's and Aristotle's at once: *what would make this life — this one, the actual one, with the shifts and the distance and the finite years — whole rather than merely long?* A whole life is not one in which everything was achieved, but one in which, in the parts that were ever up to you, the choices passed through you — Chapter 3's *I considered this, I understood the cost, and this is my answer* — and the people you love heard the true sentence from you while there was still time to say it.

That last clause is the most practical sentence in this book, and your grandmother is why. The deadline is not only yours. Every unexamined "later" — the visit deferred, the conversation postponed, the apology waiting for the right festival — is a bet against other people's finitude, at odds no one is offered twice. Philosophy's oldest advertisement for itself, from Socrates onward, was as a preparation for death; :::glossary term="Memento mori"::: — *remember that you die* — was carved and painted not to darken life but to focus it, the way a frame focuses a picture.[^memento] Nothing in this book tells you what to write inside the frame; but the frame is real, and it is the reason the writing matters.

You arrived at this book on a video call, being told how your life should go. You leave it, I hope, with the tools these chapters have tried to hand you: to see the argument inside the demand, to weigh what you owe without erasing yourself, to aim at flourishing rather than mood, to know how you know, to argue with love and rigour at once — and now to let the fact of an ending do its proper work: not as a fear that shrinks your life, but as the frame that makes it, at last and unmistakably, yours.

[^williams]: Bernard Williams, "The Makropulos Case: Reflections on the Tedium of Immortality" (1973), argues that an endless life would exhaust the desires that give a person reason to go on living. The essay is contested; it is cited for its provocation, not as settled doctrine.
[^seneca]: Seneca, *On the Shortness of Life* (*De Brevitate Vitae*, 1st century CE): life is long enough if well invested, yet people who would never let anyone seize their property let anyone at all seize their time.
[^epicurus]: Epicurus, "Letter to Menoeceus" (4th–3rd century BCE), source of the argument that "death is nothing to us," and explicit that its purpose is to free life from a poisoning fear.
[^lucretius]: Lucretius, *De Rerum Natura* (1st century BCE), Book III, states the symmetry argument: the time after death mirrors the untroubling time before birth.
[^parfit]: Derek Parfit, *Reasons and Persons* (1984), argues that personal identity over time is less deep than commonly assumed; the "letter to a relative" framing is an application of his view, not his metaphor.
[^macintyre]: Alasdair MacIntyre, *After Virtue* (1981): human actions are intelligible only within narratives, and the unity of a human life is the unity of a narrative quest.
[^strawson]: Galen Strawson, "Against Narrativity" (2004), is the best-known counterstatement: many people are genuinely and non-defectively "episodic," and the demand to experience life as a narrative is an overgeneralisation.
[^memento]: The characterisation of philosophy as a preparation for death traces to Plato's *Phaedo*; the *memento mori* tradition runs through Stoic practice into centuries of European art. Cited as intellectual history, not endorsement.
