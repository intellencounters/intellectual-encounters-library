# 5. Living in Two Languages: Meaning, Untranslatables, and the Multilingual Self

There is a moment many multilinguals know and few talk about. You are on the phone with your mother, in Tamil or Hindi or Telugu, being the person that language makes easy: deferential, affectionate, indirect, quick to laugh. The call ends. Thirty seconds later you answer a colleague in German — flat, precise, direct to a degree that would horrify your mother — and in those thirty seconds something shifted, like a gearbox engaging. Same brain, same day, arguably not quite the same person.

Then there are the failures: the word you need does not exist in the other language — not "you don't know it"; it *is not there*. You try to tell a German friend what jugaad means and watch three sentences land where one word should have gone; you try to convey what Feierabend protects, and "free evening" says nothing.

This chapter is about what is going on: whether language shapes thought, what untranslatables do and do not prove, how the grammar of respect differs between your worlds, and why switching languages is a competence — and tiring anyway.

## 5.1 Does your language shape your thought? Carefully, now

Start with the big question, where this book must be more careful than most of what you will read online. The idea that your language shapes or even determines how you think is called :::glossary term="Linguistic relativity":::, popularly attached to Edward Sapir and Benjamin Lee Whorf.[^whorf] It comes in two strengths, and the difference is the whole game. The **strong version** — language *determines* thought; no word, no thought — makes wonderful dinner conversation and is rejected by essentially the whole field of modern linguistics and cognitive science. The decisive evidence is available to you personally: you translate every day — imperfectly, with losses, but you do it, which the strong version says should be impossible. People routinely think what their language has no word for; that is why languages borrow and coin at all.[^pinker]

The **weak version** is a different creature: language *influences* thought — nudges attention, memory, habits of categorisation — without imprisoning anyone. Here the science is genuinely contested: intriguing experimental results (colour discrimination, spatial reasoning in languages using compass directions instead of left/right, grammatical gender colouring associations) alongside failed replications, disputed interpretations, and a running argument over whether the effects are deep or trivial.[^deutscher]

:::uncertainty title="Where the Sapir-Whorf debate actually stands"
The strong deterministic thesis is dead by scholarly consensus. The weak thesis — real but modest effects on habitual thought — is live, contested terrain: individual findings (colour, space, gender, number) each carry their own replication record and critics, and the field has not converged on effect sizes. Any claim of the form "speakers of language X demonstrably think Y" is one this book refuses to make on its own authority: citation needed — verify before publishing. What survives every version of the debate no one disputes: languages differ in what they make *easy, obligatory, or socially expected* to say — and living between two such systems is a real experience, whatever its cognitive depth.
:::

:::counter title="Objection: don't let the debunking swing too far" to="the confident dismissal of linguistic relativity"
There is a fashionable counter-error: having learned that strong Whorfianism is wrong, people conclude language is a neutral pipe and all talk of language shaping experience is romantic nonsense. That overshoots. Even without deep cognitive effects, languages differ enormously in what they *oblige* you to encode (German forces a du/Sie decision in every address; many Indian languages a fine-grained respect decision; English lets you duck both), and obligatory distinctions train habits of attention as any daily practice does. The lived reality of multilinguals — certain feelings coming more easily in one language, some sentences sayable to your mother in only one — does not need strong Whorf to be true; social meaning, memory, and habit suffice. Rejecting the myth should not mean dismissing the experience.
:::

## 5.2 Untranslatables: windows, not walls

If untranslatable words do not prove that speakers live in different mental universes, what are they? Windows into what a community has *needed to say often enough to compress into a word* — evidence not of what people *can* think, but of what their shared life kept requiring them to say.

Hold four up to the light. **Jugaad** — the improvised fix, the frugal workaround — compresses a whole ecology: scarcity, ingenuity, flexible rules, pride in resourcefulness. That Hindi keeps it in daily circulation says something true about the life it grew in; that German has no equivalent (its nearest neighbours lean disapproving) says something equally true about a world of norms, warranties, and proper procedure — the world where your jugaad fix at work earns a stare instead of applause. **Gemütlichkeit** — the warm, unhurried cosiness of a well-kept interior with people you trust — compresses the opposite ecology: long winters, guarded privacy, the sanctity of the small circle. **Heimat** is heavier: not quite "home" or "homeland" but the felt landscape of first belonging — so loaded that Germans argue over who may use it.[^heimat] And from outside our two cultures, Portuguese **saudade** — the tender ache of longing for something absent, perhaps permanently — names in one word the emotion this book's readers may know better than either of their languages can say.

Two lessons. First: each took a phrase to gloss, and the gloss worked — you understood. That is 5.1 in action: the concept crosses; only the compression is lost. Second: when a word refuses to translate, you have found a place where the two webs of meaning from Chapter 1 are woven differently. Treat each untranslatable as free fieldwork, and vocabulary lessons quietly become the anthropology this book has been teaching all along.

## 5.3 The grammar of respect: du and Sie, aap and tum

Some differences are not in the dictionary but in the grammar of relationships — and none bites more often than the machinery of address. Linguists call the European version the :::glossary term="T–V distinction"::: — the choice between informal and formal "you," German **du** and **Sie**.[^politeness] Getting it wrong stings both ways: an uninvited *du* can read as presumptuous; a stubborn *Sie* among peers as cold. The transition is itself a small rite of passage — sometimes explicitly offered ("Wir können uns duzen") — and its timing varies by region, workplace, and generation; even Germans sometimes hover.

Your Indian languages run a system at least as rich. Hindi grades the second person three ways — **aap**, **tum**, **tu** — layering honorifics and kin terms on top; Tamil, Telugu and others have their own architectures of respect. You arrived already fluent in the *kind* of system du/Sie is — arguably a more demanding one. The trap is not the machinery; it is the **mapping**. Aap-type respect tracks age and kinship: an elder is aap almost regardless of context. Sie-type formality tracks distance and setting more than age: the twenty-five-year-old bank clerk is Sie, while your sixty-year-old Verein teammate may insist on du from day one — the club's levelling communitas made grammatical. Transferring home rules wholesale misfires both ways: you Sie people the situation has made du, seeming distant; or you expect age to command formality and read its absence as disrespect. Neither system is finer — both encode respect obligatorily, unlike English — but they encode different *maps* of it. Learn the map, not just the words.

:::check reread="5.3"
question: A student addresses his sixty-year-old German neighbour with Sie, and continues even after the neighbour cheerfully offers "du." He feels du would disrespect an elder. What is the most accurate reading?
- He is right: du to an elder is disrespectful in any culture.
- The neighbour is testing him and secretly expects continued Sie.
- He is mapping his home system (respect tracks age above all, as with aap) onto German, where formality tracks distance and setting; after an explicit offer of du, persisting with Sie can itself create unwanted distance.
- German has abolished formality, so Sie is always wrong.
answer: 3
explanation: Both languages encode respect grammatically, but along different maps: aap-type systems weight age and kinship; the T–V system weights distance and setting, and the offer of du is a small rite that redraws the relationship. Refusing it out of transferred age-deference is a mapping error, not extra politeness — the continued Sie now signals the very distance the neighbour tried to close.
:::

## 5.4 Switching is the skill; the accent is not the person

Chapter 3 introduced :::glossary term="Code-switching"::: for behaviour generally. In its original, linguistic sense it means switching between languages or varieties — sometimes mid-sentence — and a folk prejudice needs demolishing here: that mixing languages ("Hinglish," Tamil sentences with German nouns for bureaucratic objects) is lazy, broken speech betraying mastery of neither.

Linguists who study multilingual communities have found the opposite: fluent code-switching follows grammatical patterns, is deployed with social precision — this word for that listener, this language for intimacy, that one for authority — and requires command of both systems at once.[^switching] You switch *into* the language that holds the right word (of course the Termin and the Bescheid stay German — that is where those objects live) and *toward* the identity the moment calls for. Not two half-languages: one larger repertoire under skilled management.

:::argument title="The repertoire argument: multilingualism as expanded competence"
conclusion: Living in two (or three) languages should be counted an expansion of competence and self, not a dilution of either.
premise: Fluent code-switching demands simultaneous command of multiple grammatical and social systems, and observably follows rules — structured skill, not breakdown.
premise: Each language adds expressive range: registers of respect, untranslatable compressions, relationships that can only be fully conducted in it.
premise: A capacity that adds range and skill without removing prior abilities is an expansion by any reasonable accounting.
:::

:::counter title="Objection: the costs are real, and not everyone lets you win" to="The repertoire argument: multilingualism as expanded competence"
Steel-manned, the objection has three prongs. First, the ledger is not all gain: languages compete for maintenance, and words in your mother tongue genuinely recede with disuse — a real loss, sharpest when you imagine children who may not share your first language. Second, the expanded self is expensive to run: operating all day in a later-learned language, monitoring register and du/Sie and idiom, is fatiguing in a way monolinguals rarely see — some of the exhaustion you feel at night is exactly this invisible labour. Third, and least fair: competence is socially rated, not just possessed. An accent can be heard as lack of intelligence rather than evidence of a second system mastered; mixed speech is judged "broken" by monolingual listeners; no private reframing fully cancels the gatekeeping. The repertoire argument is right about the linguistics — but a fair accounting records maintenance costs, running costs, and a market that often refuses to price the asset at its value.
:::

Hold one asymmetry from that exchange: your accent in German marks you as someone who *added* a language in adulthood — an achievement your monolingual listener has, by definition, not matched. That will not stop every unkind ear. It is still the accurate description, and you are entitled to it.

## 5.5 A different person in each language?

Back, finally, to the gearbox feeling — being someone slightly different in each language. Is it real? Many multilinguals report it: bolder here, softer there, funnier in one, more formal in another. Psychologists have studied related phenomena — biculturals shifting interpretive frames with language cues, bilinguals answering questionnaires differently by language — but the strength, universality and mechanism of such shifts remain debated; this book will not pretend the matter is settled. What needs no settling is the mechanism already in your hands from 5.1 and 5.3: each language is welded to different relationships, registers, and obligatory choices. Tamil or Hindi carries your childhood, kin terms, an architecture of deference; German carries your working adulthood, du/Sie, the blunt registers of Chapter 2. Speaking each activates its whole social world. You need not decide whether language changes your cognition or merely cues different practised selves — either way, the feeling is not a malfunction. It is the sound of a repertoire large enough to hold more than one way of being.

:::reflect
Design one small experiment on yourself. Choose a thing you find hard to say — an apology, a refusal of a family expectation, a plain statement of what you want — and write it in both of your main languages. Notice which version came easier, which felt truer, which felt sayable to its intended hearer, and what each language's obligatory machinery forced you to decide that the other did not. Then the harder question: where a sentence is sayable in one language only, is that a fact about the language — or about the relationship it is welded to? Keep both drafts; they are data about the self the final chapter asks you to assemble deliberately.
:::

None of this makes Wednesday's switching less tiring. The honest summary is double: the folk fears are false — mixing is not brokenness, an accent is not a deficit of mind, no language has locked you out of any thought — and the folk costs are real: maintenance, fatigue, listeners who rate the accent instead of the achievement. You are not caught between two languages. You own a repertoire that includes both, plus the switching skill neither monolingual world sees. The final chapter takes this competence to the table where cultures meet most concretely — the meal, the gift, and the money you send home.

[^whorf]: Benjamin Lee Whorf's papers are collected in *Language, Thought, and Reality* (ed. John B. Carroll, 1956); Edward Sapir's related writings predate them. The "Sapir-Whorf hypothesis" label and its strong/weak division are largely later constructions of the field's debate.

[^pinker]: A forceful case against strong linguistic determinism is Steven Pinker, *The Language Instinct* (1994). Pinker's own strongly universalist position is itself contested — the debate has critics on both flanks.

[^deutscher]: A balanced survey of the weak-relativity evidence is Guy Deutscher, *Through the Language Glass* (2010), which rejects strong Whorfianism while defending modest, real effects of language on habit.

[^heimat]: The contested career of "Heimat" — Romantic belonging, political appropriation, current ambivalence — is a standing topic in German cultural history; the word's difficulty is part of its meaning.

[^politeness]: The T–V terminology derives from Latin *tu/vos* via the classic sociolinguistic literature on pronouns of "power and solidarity" (Roger Brown and colleagues); the broader machinery of linguistic respect is treated in Penelope Brown and Stephen C. Levinson, *Politeness* (1987), itself much debated for claiming universality.

[^switching]: That code-switching is rule-governed skill rather than deficit is a mainstream finding of sociolinguistics, associated with Shana Poplack and Carol Myers-Scotton, whose competing grammatical models both demonstrate systematic structure in mixed speech.
