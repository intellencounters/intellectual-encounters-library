# Citations and sources

This page lists the works referred to in the footnotes of this book, with a one-line note on what each contributes. These are canonical, widely available texts in sociology and social theory; the book cites them for their concepts, not for specific statistics, and where a precise empirical figure would be needed the text uses an `:::uncertainty` block rather than a citation.

## Chapter 1 — The Invisible Rules

- **Émile Durkheim, *The Rules of Sociological Method* (1895).** The founding statement of the *social fact* — a way of acting, thinking, or feeling external to the individual and endowed with a constraining power over them. The source for Section 1.2.
- **Émile Durkheim, *The Division of Labour in Society* (1893)** and ***Suicide* (1897).** Where Durkheim develops *anomie* — the disorientation of normlessness that arises when binding expectations loosen faster than new ones form. Used in Section 1.2.
- **C. Wright Mills, *The Sociological Imagination* (1959).** The source of the distinction between *personal troubles of milieu* and *public issues of social structure*, and of the central method of this book. Used throughout Section 1.3.
- **Peter L. Berger and Thomas Luckmann, *The Social Construction of Reality* (1966).** The classic account of how the taken-for-granted "reality" of everyday life is socially built. Underpins the treatment of *success as a social construction* in Section 1.4.
- **Max Weber, *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (1905).** For the argument that a particular idea of worldly success as a mark of worth has specific historical and cultural roots rather than being natural or universal. Referenced in Section 1.4.

## Chapter 2 — Family, Gender, and the Scripts We Inherit

- **Erving Goffman, *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* (1959)** and ***Stigma: Notes on the Management of Spoiled Identity* (1963).** For reputation understood as something continuously performed before an audience, and for how a "spoiled" identity is managed and policed. Referenced in Section 2.4 on honour and shame.

## Chapter 3 — Belonging in the In-Between

- **Georg Simmel, "The Stranger" (*Exkurs über den Fremden*, 1908).** The brief excursus, part of Simmel's *Soziologie*, on the figure who "comes today and stays tomorrow" — near and far at once. The source for Section 3.1.
- **Abdelmalek Sayad, *The Suffering of the Immigrant* (English translation 2004; French original *La double absence*, 1999).** The origin of the *double absence* — being absent from the society of origin and not fully present in the society of arrival. Central to Section 3.2.
- **Pierre Bourdieu, *Outline of a Theory of Practice* (French 1972; English 1977)** and ***Distinction* (French 1979; English 1984).** Where the concept of *habitus* is developed. Used in Section 3.3.
- **Benedict Anderson, *Imagined Communities* (1983).** For the idea that even large communities are held together by shared stories and symbols among people who will never meet — extended here to diaspora. Used in Section 3.4.
- **Stuart Hall, "Cultural Identity and Diaspora" (1990),** in J. Rutherford (ed.), *Identity: Community, Culture, Difference*. For diasporic identity as continually *produced* and negotiated rather than a fixed essence preserved or lost. Used in Section 3.4.

## Chapter 4 — The Dignity of Work: Class, Status, and the Shift-Work Self

- **Max Weber, "Class, Status, Party,"** in *Economy and Society* (published posthumously, 1922). The source of the distinction between *class* (market position) and *status* (social honour) that structures Section 4.1.
- **Arlie Russell Hochschild, *The Managed Heart: Commercialization of Human Feeling* (1983).** The founding study of *emotional labour*, based on fieldwork with flight attendants; the source of the surface-acting/deep-acting distinction. Central to Section 4.3.

## Chapter 5 — The Strength of Your Ties: Networks, Capital, and Community

- **Mark S. Granovetter, "The Strength of Weak Ties,"** *American Journal of Sociology* (1973), and ***Getting a Job: A Study of Contacts and Careers* (1974).** The classic argument that new information tends to travel along acquaintance ties bridging otherwise disconnected clusters. Cited cautiously in Section 5.1, with its scope conditions noted.
- **Robert D. Putnam, *Bowling Alone: The Collapse and Revival of American Community* (2000).** The source of the *bonding*/*bridging* social-capital distinction used throughout Sections 5.2 and 5.4.
- **Ronald S. Burt, *Structural Holes: The Social Structure of Competition* (1992).** The analysis of *brokerage* — the advantages accruing to those who span gaps between disconnected groups. Used in Section 5.3.

## Chapter 6 — How Norms Die: Social Change and the Generations

- **Karl Mannheim, "The Problem of Generations"** ("Das Problem der Generationen," 1928), in English in *Essays on the Sociology of Knowledge* (1952). The classic account of generations as formed by shared historical location in youth. Central to Section 6.3.
- **Mancur Olson, *The Logic of Collective Action: Public Goods and the Theory of Groups* (1965).** The classic analysis of why large groups sharing a common interest often fail to act on it while small groups succeed. The source for Section 6.4.

## A note on verification

These references are given from well-established editions and the concepts are attributed as accurately as the author can vouch for. **Before quoting, teaching from, or reprinting any of them, verify the exact bibliographic details** — edition, translator, year, and above all any page numbers — against the physical or library-catalogue record. Publication years for translated works in particular vary by edition. Where this book makes a specific empirical or statistical claim, it does *not* rest on these works: such claims are flagged in `:::uncertainty` blocks with the phrase "citation needed — verify before publishing," and must be sourced separately before publication.
