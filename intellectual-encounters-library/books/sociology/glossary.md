# Glossary

## Norm

A shared, largely unspoken expectation about how people ought to behave in a particular situation, enforced not usually by law but by ordinary social approval and disapproval — a nod, an awkward silence, gossip, pride, shame. Norms are learned so early and held so deeply that they are often mistaken for instinct or plain common sense. To name a norm as a norm is not to condemn it; it is to make it visible, and therefore open to examination.

## Role

A bundle of expectations attached not to a person but to a *position* they occupy — daughter, son, elder sibling, student, employee, the-one-who-went-abroad. Stepping into a role means being expected to carry whatever that role is supposed to carry. Much of the strain of a stretched life comes from holding several roles at once whose expectations conflict.

## Social fact

Émile Durkheim's term for a way of acting, thinking, or feeling that exists *outside* any single individual yet exerts a real, constraining force *over* them. Language, money, and marriage are social facts: you did not invent them, they preceded you, and they push back when you try to resist them. Durkheim's test is that friction — the resistance you meet is the social fact making itself felt.

## Sociological imagination

C. Wright Mills's name for the capacity to see the connection between *personal troubles* (what happens in one biography) and *public issues* (the larger structures and history one lives inside). Its central discipline is refusing to explain a public issue purely in the private language of individual character — refusing, that is, to blame yourself alone for a pattern shared by thousands in the same position.

## Social construction

The idea that a thing which feels like a fixed fact of nature — such as what counts as a "successful life" — is in fact assembled by a society, in a particular time and place, and could have been assembled otherwise. To call something socially constructed is *not* to call it fake or unimportant (money is constructed and will still get you evicted); it is to say it is made by people and therefore open to being questioned rather than simply obeyed.

## Anomie

Durkheim's term for a condition of normlessness or disorientation that arises not from too many rules but from too few that still hold — when old expectations dissolve faster than new ones can form. Migrants often know this territory: leaving a world of dense expectation for one that shrugs and says *do as you like* brings a real freedom that can also feel strangely unanchored.

## Institution

In sociology, not a building but a durable, widely recognised pattern of social relationships organised around a central human need — the family (care and reproduction), the market (exchange), religion (meaning), the state (governance). Institutions outlast the individuals who pass through them, carry their own roles and norms, and reproduce themselves by shaping each new generation. Calling the family an institution does not deny that it is also love; it adds that love, there, does structured social work.

## Socialization

The lifelong process by which a society's norms, values, and roles are transmitted to a person until they feel like the person's own nature. The most effective socialisation is invisible: not the explicit lecture but the thousand small corrections and rewards through which a child learns what is expected of someone like them.

## Gender script

The detailed, mostly unspoken set of expectations a society attaches to being a man or a woman — what one should want, how one should behave, what counts as success and what as shame for one's kind. The word *script* is deliberate: a script is not destiny (actors improvise and refuse lines) but it is not nothing either (it assigns your part before you speak). These scripts fall differently, and unequally, on men and women.

## Honour and shame

A system, found across many societies, in which a family's standing is a shared, collective possession: honour is social credit held by the family as a whole, and the actions of one member can raise or lower the standing of all. Analysed as a mechanism, it enforces norms without police or courts by making each person a guardian and a hostage of everyone else's reputation — doing real cooperative work under scarcity, while making personal freedom costly and, very often, loading shame unequally onto women.

## Double absence

Abdelmalek Sayad's term for the migrant's condition of being absent from two worlds at once: no longer fully present in the society of origin (missing from its daily life, reduced to remittances and a voice on a call) and not yet fully present in the society of arrival (received as a worker or a legal case rather than a whole person). It is not homesickness alone but the structural experience of being incompletely seen in both places simultaneously.

## Habitus

Pierre Bourdieu's term for the set of dispositions — tastes, reflexes, bodily habits, gut senses of what is comfortable, polite, funny, or shameful — that a society writes into a person in childhood, below conscious thought. The habitus is durable: laid down in one's first world, it is carried into the second, where the mismatch is felt not as an opinion but as bodily unease. It is why belonging cannot be willed in an afternoon.

## Diaspora

Not merely a scattered people but the *community that scattered people create across distance* — held together by shared origin, memory, ritual, food, language, and the constant traffic of the group chat. Sociologically a diaspora is a form of belonging built in the in-between, imagined across borders yet as real in its effects as any village, and continually produced rather than simply preserved.

## Assimilation

The process by which a minority or migrant group dissolves into the host society, taking on its norms and shedding the distinctiveness of origin. Full assimilation is rarely fully available (the habitus and the host society's own gaze see to that) and, where pursued, often costs more than it returns. It stands as one pure pole — total preservation being the other — between which most migrants, in practice, negotiate.

## Status

In Max Weber's sense, the social honour or prestige attached to a position, as distinct from class (one's market position and income). Status is granted in small, constant currency — forms of address, assumptions about intelligence, whose time counts as valuable, who is looked at and who is looked through — and it correlates with money without being the same thing. Status hierarchies are socially constructed, which is why the same occupation can carry very different honour in different societies.

## Status inconsistency

The condition, named by Gerhard Lenski, of holding visibly different ranks on different social hierarchies at once — for instance, high on the education ladder and low on the occupation ladder, as with a master's student working a fast-food night shift. Its characteristic strain comes from being read by whichever rank is visible (usually the uniform) while knowing the other, and migration tends to widen the gap, since the two ladders may sit in two different societies with two different audiences.

## Emotional labour

Arlie Hochschild's term for the work of producing, in oneself, the feelings an employer requires one to display — the warmth behind the service smile, the calm absorption of a customer's anger, or the suppression of fear and fatigue. It is real labour that tires like lifting, appears on no payslip, and is distributed along gender scripts: warmth-production is demanded more often of women, strain-suppression more often of men. Sustained for years, it risks estrangement from one's own feelings.

## Dirty work

Everett C. Hughes's term for the necessary work a society designates as physically, socially, or morally tainted — cleaning, waste, slaughter, intimate care, night logistics — and then delegates, looks away from, and quietly looks down upon. The disrespect is part of the arrangement, not an accident of it. Workers assigned dirty work characteristically build sturdy moral defences: reframing the job as infrastructure, drawing boundaries between what they do and what they are, and holding craft standards invisible to outsiders.

## Social capital

The resources — information, help, vouching, trust, opportunity — that a person or group can access through their web of relationships. Like money or skills it can be accumulated, invested, and lost; unlike them it is stored in other people and cannot be transferred at will. Its two broad forms, bonding and bridging, do different work, and most lives need both.

## Weak ties

Acquaintance-level relationships — low in invested time, emotional intensity, and obligation — as contrasted with the strong ties of family and close friendship. In Mark Granovetter's argument, weak ties matter out of proportion to their warmth because they often bridge between otherwise disconnected clusters: close contacts largely share one another's information, while an acquaintance connects to circles otherwise out of reach. The claim concerns the flow of new information, not which ties matter most for care and survival.

## Bonding capital

Robert Putnam's term for the social capital of dense, closed, similar groups — family, close friends, people sharing origin, language, and situation. It is the capital of *getting by*: emotional survival, mutual aid, small loans, understanding that needs no translation. Deep, load-bearing, and inward-facing; on its own it recycles the information its members already share.

## Bridging capital

Putnam's term for the social capital of connections *across* social distance — to people unlike oneself, in other communities, occupations, and worlds. It is the capital of *getting ahead*: new information, new opportunities, entry into circles where unfamiliar jobs and unwritten rules live. Not a superior form but a complementary one; a life with only bridges is well-connected and unheld.

## Network closure

The property of a dense network in which everyone knows everyone, analysed by James Coleman as the structural basis of trust and enforcement: no one can cheat and vanish, reputations are collective, and help is obligatory. The same closure that supports also polices — it enforces all of a group's norms, not only the helpful ones — and it makes a closed circle information-rich internally but redundant, since members mostly know what one another know.

## Brokerage

Ronald Burt's term for the position of a person who spans a *structural hole* — a gap between two groups not otherwise connected. The broker sees both sides' information first, can translate between them, and captures opportunities neither group knows the other holds. Closure gives safety; brokerage gives vision and options; a well-built network keeps closure at its core and brokers at its edges.

## Pluralistic ignorance

The state in which a norm's members privately doubt it while publicly complying — and each reads everyone else's public compliance as private conviction, so all believe themselves the only doubter. Norms in this state are hollow but standing, and they are the prime candidates for sudden collapse, since the first visible defections need not persuade anyone, only reveal to the silent doubters that they were never alone.

## Norm cascade

A pattern of rapid norm change in which early visible defections lower the perceived cost of further defections, triggering a self-reinforcing chain until the majority tips and the new behaviour hardens into a norm of its own. Cascades are possible because much of a norm's force rests on beliefs about others' beliefs, which can flip in a chain reaction once private doubt becomes publicly visible.

## Cohort effect

A durable mark left on the people who came of age in a particular historical period — scarcity, war, liberalisation, the arrival of the internet — and carried by that cohort across life like an accent. Contrasted with a life-cycle effect; the two are easy to confuse and opposite in implication, since a cohort difference between parent and child will not close as the child ages.

## Life-cycle effect

A pattern of attitude or behaviour that belongs to a *stage of life* rather than to a generation: whatever one's cohort, being twenty-five tends toward risk and mobility, being fifty-five toward caution and consolidation. "You will think like me at my age" is a life-cycle claim; "your caution is training from a vanished world" is a cohort claim; which is true in a given case decides whether a generational difference is a phase or a permanent formation.

## Collective action problem

Mancur Olson's analysis of situations in which a change would benefit everyone in a group, but the costs of initiating it fall entirely on whoever moves first — so each member's rational move is to wait, and even a change everyone wants may never happen. Small dense groups can breach the wall of waiting: they make contributions visible and reward first movers with solidarity instead of leaving them to pay alone.
