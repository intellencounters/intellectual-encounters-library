# 5. The Strength of Your Ties: Networks, Capital, and Community

Think back to how you actually got your current job. Not how one is supposed to get a job — the polished CV, the portal, the interview — but how it really happened. For a striking number of people the honest answer runs through a person: a friend already working there said they were hiring; someone's flatmate knew the shift manager; a message appeared in a group chat — *they need people, send me your details tonight*. The same is true, quite often, of the room you first slept in, the city you chose, the tip about which office processes paperwork faster, and the warning about which employer to avoid. Ask around your circle and count. The economy you actually live in is not the one in the textbook, where isolated individuals meet a labour market. It is a web of people, and information, help, obligation, and constraint all travel along its threads.

This chapter gives you the tools to *see* the web — your own, specifically. It will end reflexively, with a sociological reading of something you are inside at this very moment: a small WhatsApp circle of friends far from home. But first, the instruments.

## 5.1 Strong ties, weak ties, and where new information lives

A network, for a sociologist, is just people (nodes) and relationships (ties) — but the ties differ in kind, and the difference turns out to matter enormously. *Strong ties* are the close ones: family, best friends, the people you call at 2 a.m., relationships thick with time, emotion, and mutual obligation. :::glossary term="Weak ties"::: are the acquaintances: the former colleague, the classmate you occasionally message, the friend of a friend from another city, the person you know from one season of one job.

Intuition says the strong ties are the valuable ones, and for survival — care, loans, comfort, the 2 a.m. call — intuition is right. But in a famous 1973 paper, "The Strength of Weak Ties," the sociologist Mark Granovetter argued that for *new information*, the intuition inverts.[^granovetter] In his study of professional men in a Boston suburb who had recently changed jobs, many of those who found work through personal contacts had found it not through close friends but through acquaintances — people seen rarely, sometimes barely remembered. Granovetter's explanation was structural, and it is the part worth keeping even if one holds the specific findings cautiously (his sample was small, local, male, and white-collar, and later studies across other countries and labour markets have found the pattern in some settings and not others). Your strong ties mostly know each other and mostly know *what you already know* — they swim in the same information as you. A weak tie is your bridge to a different cluster: different workplaces, different gossip, different vacancies. Novelty enters a life from its edges, not its centre.

:::definition term="Weak ties"
Acquaintance-level relationships — low in time invested, emotional intensity, and mutual obligation — as contrasted with the strong ties of family and close friendship. In Granovetter's network argument, weak ties matter out of proportion to their warmth because they often serve as *bridges* between otherwise disconnected clusters: your close contacts largely share one another's information, while an acquaintance is connected to circles you cannot otherwise reach. The claim is about the flow of new information, not about which ties matter most for support, trust, or care.
:::

- Because close friends mostly share your own circles and therefore your own information, while an acquaintance bridges to clusters you are not otherwise connected to — so genuinely new information tends to arrive along weak ties.

answer: 2
explanation: The argument is structural, not moral: novelty lives at the edges of a network because strong ties cluster together and recycle shared information, while weak ties bridge between clusters. Strong ties remain essential for support, trust, and survival — and the original evidence came from one small, particular sample, so the pattern should be held as a well-theorised tendency that varies across settings, not a universal law.

## 5.2 Social capital: what a network is worth

If ties carry real resources — information, help, vouching, trust — then a person's web of relationships is a kind of asset, and sociologists call it :::glossary term="Social capital":::. The word *capital* is deliberately provocative: like money or skills, relationships can be accumulated, invested, drawn upon, and lost; unlike money, they are stored in other people and mostly cannot be transferred or spent down deliberately without destroying them.

The political scientist Robert Putnam, in *Bowling Alone*, drew a distinction inside social capital that this chapter will lean on heavily.[^putnam] :::glossary term="Bonding capital"::: is the capital of dense, closed, similar groups — family, close friends, people who share your origin, language, and situation. It is the capital of *getting by*: emotional survival, small loans, meals, someone who understands the joke and the grief without translation. :::glossary term="Bridging capital"::: is the capital of connections *across* social distance — to people unlike you, in other communities, occupations, and worlds. It is the capital of *getting ahead*: new information, new opportunities, entry into circles where the better jobs and the unwritten rules live. The distinction is not a ranking. A life with only bridging capital is well-networked and unheld — plenty of contacts, no one to call at 2 a.m. A life with only bonding capital is warm and walled. The finding worth underlining is that the two are not substitutes; they do different work, and most people need both.

## 5.3 The migrant's network: rope and net

Nowhere is the doubleness of social capital sharper than in migration, because almost no one migrates alone in the sociological sense. Long before the visa, the network was at work: the cousin who explained which course to apply for, the friend of the family in Frankfurt, the senior from college who picked you up from the airport and lent you a mattress and a SIM card. Migration scholars call this *chain migration* — people follow the links of those who went before — and its logic is generous: each arrival lowers the cost and terror of the next.

But watch what else the same rope does. The network that found you your first job found it *where the network works* — which is how entire friend-groups end up in the same warehouse, the same fast-food chains, the same delivery fleets. The help was real, and it was also a channel: the network delivers you efficiently into the niches it already occupies, and the longer you stay inside its comfort, the fewer bridges you build to anywhere else. The rope that pulled you across can quietly become a net that holds you where you landed.

Network analysts have a vocabulary for this trade-off. A dense network where everyone knows everyone — what the sociologist James Coleman analysed as :::glossary term="Network closure"::: — is superb at trust, enforcement, and mutual aid: nobody can cheat you and vanish, reputations are collective, help is obligatory. That is its gift, and it is also its price, because closure enforces *all* the norms, not only the helpful ones — the very machinery of reputation and obligation that Chapter 2 called :::glossary term="Honour and shame"::: runs on network closure, and the group chat can carry surveillance as efficiently as it carries solidarity. The structural alternative was analysed by the sociologist Ronald Burt: :::glossary term="Brokerage":::, the position of the person who spans a *structural hole* — a gap between two groups that are not otherwise connected.[^burt] The broker sees both sides' information first, can translate between them, and captures opportunities neither group knows the other has. Closure gives safety; brokerage gives vision and options. A well-built life, Burt's tradition suggests, wants closure at its core and brokerage at its edges.

:::uncertainty title="How much migrant employment actually flows through networks?"
It would be natural to quantify this section — for instance, what share of migrants find their first job through personal contacts, or how strongly ethnic-niche employment concentrates specific groups in specific sectors in Germany. A substantial literature in migration studies and labour economics addresses exactly these questions, and the qualitative pattern described here — networks as both entry-ramp and channel — is well attested in it. But the specific proportions differ sharply by origin group, destination, sector, legal status, and decade, and no single figure can be responsibly asserted from memory. Citation needed — verify before publishing. Read this section as structural analysis of a mechanism, not as measurement of its size.
:::

:::check reread="5.3"
question: A tight circle of friends from home all work at the same warehouse, found each other the jobs, lend each other money, and know everything about each other's lives. Which analysis best fits?
- The circle has failed at social capital, since none of them has a prestigious job.
- The circle is rich in bonding capital and network closure — which delivers real trust, aid, and enforcement — but poor in bridges, so the same structure that supports its members also channels them into the same niches and recycles the same information.
- The circle has maximum bridging capital, because its members bridge each other to the warehouse.
- Closure of this kind has costs only, and the group would be better off dissolving.
answer: 2
explanation: This is closure and bonding capital working exactly as theory describes: dense mutual ties produce trust, obligatory help, and collective reputation — genuine goods — while the absence of bridges means new information rarely enters and the network reproduces its own employment niche. Ties among similar, already-connected people are bonding, not bridging; and the remedy for missing bridges is to add them, not to dissolve a core that provides real support.
:::

## 5.4 The sociology of a WhatsApp circle

Now the reflexive turn. Take a small WhatsApp group of close friends — people from home, scattered across a foreign country, in constant conversation: memes, shift complaints, exam panic, festival photographs, the occasional 2 a.m. crisis handled together. Read it with this chapter's instruments, and it stops being just a chat and becomes a recognisable sociological object.

It is, first, a concentrated store of bonding capital — arguably the single most valuable asset its members hold in Germany. It is where the :::glossary term="Habitus"::: can relax: the jokes need no footnotes, the code-switching can stop, the grief about home needs no explanation. It is a mutual-insurance pool (money, couches, contacts, cover for shifts) and a piece of the :::glossary term="Diaspora"::: in Chapter 3's sense — belonging, actively produced across distance, several evenings a week. It is also, structurally, a *closed* network: high closure, everyone tied to everyone, reputation collective, information instantly shared — and therefore instantly redundant. When one member hears of a vacancy, all know within the hour; but they are usually vacancies from the same few workplaces the group already inhabits. And closure's other face operates here too: the group's norms — about spending, ambition, marriage, how much to send home, what may be joked about and what may not — are enforced by the gentlest and most effective of mechanisms, the wish not to disappoint the only people nearby who fully know you.

None of this is a flaw to be fixed by dissolving the group; the bonding is load-bearing. The design question is different: can a bonding circle *also* become a bridging structure, without losing its warmth? Structurally, yes — because a group's reach is not its own ties but the sum of its members' outside ties. Every member has weak ties the others do not: a German classmate, a colleague on another contract, a professor, a gym acquaintance, a Verein, an alumni network. Unshared, these are private edges; brought back into the group — the vacancy at *her* office, the internship *his* classmate mentioned, the bureaucratic shortcut someone's German neighbour explained — they become bridges the whole circle can walk across. A circle of six people with six directions of outside contact is, structurally, a brokerage engine; a circle of six whose outside worlds all overlap is a warm room with one window. The practical differences are small and concrete: treating members' "outside" acquaintances as shared infrastructure rather than private property; occasionally bringing an outsider in — to the Diwali gathering, the birthday, the football — so that a weak tie thickens; and each member keeping one foot in one structure where they are the stranger, on the understanding that what they learn there flows back.

:::argument title="A bonding circle serves its members best when it deliberately builds bridges"
conclusion: A close-knit migrant friend circle should deliberately invest in bridging ties — pooling members' outside contacts and cultivating connections beyond its boundary — rather than relying on bonding alone.
premise: The circle's bonding capital, however deep, mostly recycles information its members already share, while the opportunities they migrated for — better jobs, entry into professional worlds — live in clusters to which the circle is not yet connected.
premise: New information reliably enters a network through bridges and weak ties at its edges, and a group's potential bridges are exactly its members' individual outside acquaintances, which cost little to share.
premise: Bridging investments of this kind do not require weakening the bonding core — the same circle can hold its closure at the centre and add bridges at its edges, and gains from one member's bridge flow to all.
:::

:::counter title="Objection: bridges are not free, and not equally available" to="A bonding circle serves its members best when it deliberately builds bridges"
Three honest replies. First, the instrumental frame can corrode what it touches: a friendship audited for its network value is already less of a friendship, and people can smell being treated as a bridge — the advice "cultivate weak ties" curdles quickly into the networking culture everyone rightly finds hollow. Second, bridging is not equally available to everyone: weak ties deliver less to those whom the host society discounts, and research traditions after Granovetter have noted that the returns to informal contacts vary with race, class, gender, and accent — a bridge you walk across only to be quietly turned away at the far end is not the asset the theory promised, and telling a structurally excluded person to "just network more" mistakes a demand-side wall for a supply-side failure. Third, closure is not a design error; under precarity it is rational. The tight circle exists *because* its members cannot yet afford the risks that loosely-networked natives absorb easily — a failed bridge costs an evening for the secure and a month's rent for the precarious. The argument for bridging stands, but only held together with these limits: build bridges without instrumentalising people, expect unequal returns through no fault of your own, and never dismantle the raft to build the pier.
:::

## 5.5 Seeing your own web

What this chapter offers is a change of vision. The job you got, the room you found, the niche your friends share, the warmth of the group chat and its gentle surveillance, the sense that opportunity lives somewhere just beyond the people you know — none of these are accidents of personality. They are the ordinary workings of network structure: strong and weak ties, bonding and bridging capital, closure and brokerage, ropes that pull and nets that hold. You cannot stand outside your web — no one can — but you can learn to see it: to notice which of your ties are bridges and which are mirrors, to value the closed core for what it genuinely provides, and to tend, deliberately and without cynicism, the edges where the new comes in.

One thread remains, and it leads to the final chapter. Networks do not only carry jobs and information; they carry *norms* — and they are also where norms weaken, where a small circle quietly stops enforcing an expectation the wider world still holds. How the invisible rules of Chapter 1 loosen, shift, and die; why generations formed in different worlds collide inside one family; and what small groups like yours have to do with large social change — that is where this book ends.

Then classify your recent gains — job leads, housing, advice worth having — by which kind of tie actually delivered them. Now the harder part: identify the biggest *structural hole* in your web — a world you need (a profession, an institution, a German-speaking circle) to which you currently have no bridge at all — and name one specific, non-cynical step toward a single tie into it. Finally, steel-man your own closure: write two sentences defending the tightness of your inner circle against this chapter's bridging advice, and then say which consideration wins for you this year, and why.
