# 1. Attention Is a Budget

It is 23:40. The shift ended an hour ago; your feet still remember the floor. You open the textbook, read a paragraph on database normalisation, and reach the full stop having absorbed nothing. You read it again. Your phone lights up — a message in the family group — and when you look back at the page you have lost the thread entirely.

This chapter is not about willpower. It is about a resource. Your capacity to hold and work with information in the moment is small, strictly limited, and easily spent — and it is spent faster when you are tired. Treating attention as a *budget* rather than a *virtue* changes what you do at 23:40. You stop blaming yourself for running out and start deciding, deliberately, what to spend the little you have on.

## 1.1 The narrow doorway

Almost everything you consciously think about has to pass through a small, temporary workspace. Psychologists call it :::glossary term="Working memory":::. It is where a phone number lives while you dial it, where the first half of a sentence waits while you read the second, where a running sub-total sits while you add the next line. It is not long-term storage; it is a workbench, and the workbench is tiny.

:::definition term="Working memory"
The brain system that holds a small amount of information in an active, readily available state while you use it — reasoning, reading, calculating, following an instruction. It is limited in both capacity and duration: contents fade within seconds unless refreshed, and only a handful of items can be held at once. The influential model of Alan Baddeley and Graham Hitch describes it not as a single store but as a set of specialised parts — a verbal loop, a visual sketchpad, and a central executive that allocates attention among them.[^baddeley]
:::

How small is "a handful"? The most famous answer is George Miller's — that we can hold about seven items, give or take two. Later work suggested the true figure, once you stop people from silently rehearsing, is smaller still. The exact number matters less than the shape of the fact: it is astonishingly few, and this narrowness is the ceiling on everything that follows.

:::uncertainty title="How many items, exactly?"
The specific capacity figures — "seven plus or minus two" from Miller (1956) and the later revision to roughly four "chunks" associated with Nelson Cowan's work — are widely cited, but the precise number depends on the task, the material, and how rehearsal is controlled. Do not repeat any single figure as a settled constant. Citation needed — verify before publishing. What is safe to say, and all this chapter relies on, is that the capacity is very small.
:::

There is one legitimate way to smuggle more through the doorway: *chunking*. To a novice, `C-A-P-G-E-M-I-N-I` is nine items and hopeless to hold. To someone who knows the company, it is one — "Capgemini". Expertise is, in large part, the slow building of chunks, so that what costs a beginner ten slots of working memory costs an expert one. This is why the same lecture exhausts you and barely taxes the person beside you who has seen the material before. They are not smarter tonight; they are carrying it in bigger boxes.

## 1.2 The multitasking myth

The tired mind reaches instinctively for multitasking: revise the SQL notes *and* keep half an eye on the WhatsApp group *and* have the football on. It feels efficient. It is the opposite.

What actually happens is that :::glossary term="Attention"::: does not split; it *switches*. You are not doing two things at once — you are doing one thing, then the other, then back, paying a small toll each time you cross. That toll is a :::glossary term="Task-switching"::: cost: after each switch, your mind spends a moment reloading the rules and contents of the new task before it can work well again.

:::argument title="The case against studying while switching"
conclusion: For anything that demands understanding, doing one task at a time is faster and more accurate than interleaving it with others, even though switching feels more productive.
premise: Working memory can hold only a few items, so each task keeps its own contents loaded there.
premise: Every switch forces the mind to clear one task's contents and reload another's, which takes time and is error-prone.
premise: The felt sense of "getting more done" comes from constant activity, not from measured output.
premise: Therefore the switching tax is paid repeatedly and invisibly, and total real progress falls.
:::

:::counter title="Steel-manning the multitasker" to="The case against studying while switching"
The honest objection is that not all pairings are equal. Well-practised, automatic activities — walking, folding laundry, a familiar commute — draw little on working memory and can genuinely overlap with something more demanding; people revise vocabulary while on the exercise bike to real effect. The claim is not that the mind can never do two things, but that it cannot do two *effortful, novel* things at once. If one task has become automatic, pairing it with study may cost little. The skill is telling which of your tasks is truly automatic — and being honest that reading unfamiliar material never is.
:::

The trap is that the switch itself is invisible. You do not feel the reload; you feel busy. This is why people routinely believe they multitask well while performing measurably worse — the feedback that would correct the belief is exactly the feedback that switching destroys.

:::check reread="1.2"
question: You revise unfamiliar statistics notes while a group chat pings every few minutes. Each glance at the phone lasts only a couple of seconds. Why is the real cost far larger than those seconds?
- It is not larger; a two-second glance costs two seconds, so the notes lose almost no time.
- After each switch, working memory has to reload the statistics you were holding before you can work again, so the true cost is the glance plus the reload, paid every time.
- Phones emit light that physically tires the eyes, which is the main cost.
- The cost is purely emotional guilt, not anything to do with attention.
answer: 2
explanation: The visible glance is the small part. The expensive part is the reconstruction afterwards — reloading the half-built understanding you had dropped. Because that reload happens on every return, a task that is glanced away from many times can take far longer than the same task done in one unbroken block.
:::

## 1.3 Cognitive load

Working memory's narrowness has a name when you are learning: :::glossary term="Cognitive load":::. At any moment, some of your limited capacity is spent on the actual thing to be understood, and some is wasted on how the material happens to be presented — a badly laid-out slide, an untranslated idiom, a noisy room. Learning stalls when the total demand exceeds the workbench.

:::definition term="Cognitive load"
The total demand a task places on working memory at a given moment. Cognitive load theory, developed by John Sweller, distinguishes the load that is *intrinsic* to the material's difficulty from the *extraneous* load added by how it is presented or the conditions you study in. You cannot lower intrinsic load without changing what you are learning — but you can often cut extraneous load, freeing capacity for the part that matters.
:::

This gives you a lever. Fatigue shrinks the budget; you cannot fix that at midnight. But you *can* stop wasting the budget you have. Silencing the phone, closing the fifteen browser tabs, working from one clean page instead of five scattered ones, studying material in a language you are fluent in when you have the choice — each removes extraneous load. None makes you cleverer. Each hands back a slot of the workbench for the work itself.

## 1.4 Studying after a shift

Put the pieces together and the practical shape of a tired study session emerges — not as motivation, but as budgeting.

- **Protect single-tasking ruthlessly.** One task, phone in another room, in a genuine block. The most valuable thing you can do for a tired brain is not add caffeine but remove switches.
- **Cut extraneous load before you start.** A tidy desk and a closed door are not fussiness; they are working-memory you get to keep.
- **Spend your best capacity on your hardest material.** If you have one clear hour, give it to the concept that must be *understood*, not to re-typing notes — a low-load task you could do half-asleep.
- **Use short blocks and accept them.** A tired workbench is smaller, so twenty honest minutes on one idea beats two distracted hours. This is a real constraint, not a failure of character.

:::case title="Two Tuesdays"
Consider a composite of a pattern many readers will recognise. On the first Tuesday, Priya finishes a late retail shift and studies with the television on and the phone face-up, moving between three subjects "to cover more". After ninety minutes she has turned many pages and remembers little. On the second Tuesday she picks one topic, leaves the phone charging in the kitchen, and works in three twenty-minute blocks with a short walk between them. She covers less on paper and understands more. Nothing about her intelligence changed between the Tuesdays — only how she spent a fixed and tired budget.
:::

:::reflect
Think about your last three study sessions honestly. Where did your attention actually go — not where you meant it to go? Identify the single biggest source of switching or extraneous load in your real setup (the phone, a noisy flat, jumping between subjects, studying in a second language when a first was available). Commit now to removing exactly one of them for your next session, and write down what you expect will change so you can check whether it did.
:::

:::check reread="1.3"
question: You have one alert hour before you fade. Which use of it best respects a limited, tired attention budget?
- Re-type your lecture notes into neat colour-coded files, because a tidy record feels productive.
- Keep three subjects open and rotate between them every ten minutes so nothing is neglected.
- Spend the hour, single-tasked and phone-free, on the one concept you genuinely do not yet understand.
- Leave the hardest concept for when you are less tired, and do easy revision now.
answer: 3
explanation: Your scarcest resource is high-quality working memory, and fatigue shrinks it further. The hardest, must-be-understood material is exactly what needs that capacity; low-load tasks like re-typing or easy revision can be done when depleted. Rotating subjects only adds switching costs. Spend your best capacity on what most demands it.
:::

## 1.5 An honest close

None of this abolishes tiredness. A budget is still a budget; a small one is still small. What the framing buys you is not more capacity but fewer wasted slots and less self-blame. When you cannot absorb the paragraph at 23:40, the useful conclusion is rarely "I am not disciplined enough". It is more often "the workbench is full, and half of it is going on things that are not the paragraph." One of those conclusions leads somewhere. The next chapter turns from spending attention to the harder question of what actually survives it: how the little you take in tonight becomes something you still know at the exam.

[^baddeley]: The multi-component model of working memory originates with A. D. Baddeley and G. Hitch, "Working Memory" (1974), and is developed across Baddeley's later writing, including his overview *Working Memory, Thought, and Action* (2007). Treat the model as an influential framework, not the only one.
