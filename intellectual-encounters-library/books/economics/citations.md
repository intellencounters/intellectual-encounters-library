# Citations and further reading

This page lists the works referred to in the footnotes, with a one-line note on
what each contributes. These are canonical, widely available books and papers by
their named authors; they were chosen because they are genuinely well known, not
because any specific page, figure, or study result is quoted from them. Where the
text needed a specific statistic it could not verify, that claim was placed in an
`:::uncertainty` block rather than asserted here.

## Chapter 1 — Scarcity Is the Whole Game

- **Lionel Robbins, *An Essay on the Nature and Significance of Economic Science* (1932).** Source of the enduring definition of economics as the study of "ends and scarce means which have alternative uses," which frames scarcity and opportunity cost.
- **Sendhil Mullainathan and Eldar Shafir, *Scarcity: Why Having Too Little Means So Much* (2013).** Source for the "bandwidth tax," tunnelling, and the argument that scarcity itself degrades decision-making. The book's specific experimental magnitudes are flagged in the chapter's uncertainty block rather than quoted as settled figures.

## Chapter 2 — The Mind Is a Bad Accountant

- **Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, "Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk," *Econometrica* (1979); and Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011).** Sources for loss aversion, reference points, and framing effects.
- **Richard Thaler, *Misbehaving: The Making of Behavioural Economics* (2015); and Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein, *Nudge: Improving Decisions About Health, Wealth, and Happiness* (2008).** Sources for mental accounting, present bias / hyperbolic discounting, and the "choice architecture" that lets the present self be bound in advance.

## Chapter 3 — Foundations, Not Advice

- **Herbert A. Simon** (originator of *satisficing*, developed across his work on bounded rationality, e.g. *Administrative Behavior*, 1947, and later writings). Source for the idea that "good enough, and decided" beats "optimal, and deferred" under limited attention.
- **Amartya Sen, *Development as Freedom* (1999).** Source for the "capability approach" — the view that income matters as a means to real freedoms (capabilities), not as an end in itself — which frames the discussion of remittances.

## Chapter 4 — What a Wage Is

- **Gary Becker, *Human Capital: A Theoretical and Empirical Analysis* (1964).** Foundational treatment of skills, training, and health as investments that raise productivity and earnings — the "human capital" side of the degree debate.
- **Michael Spence, "Job Market Signaling," *Quarterly Journal of Economics* (1973).** The canonical model of education as a costly, credible signal of unobservable ability — the "signalling" side of the same debate.
- **Bryan Caplan, *The Case Against Education* (2018).** The strongest recent book-length statement of the signalling view; cited as one side of a live argument, not as a verdict.
- **David Card and Alan Krueger, *Myth and Measurement: The New Economics of the Minimum Wage* (1995).** Source of the border-comparison studies that challenged the consensus that minimum wages must reduce employment; the wider empirical debate is flagged as unsettled in the chapter's uncertainty block.

## Chapter 5 — Why Nations Differ

- **Douglass North, *Institutions, Institutional Change and Economic Performance* (1990).** Foundational statement of institutions as the "rules of the game" shaping incentives and long-run economic performance.
- **Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson, *Why Nations Fail: The Origins of Power, Prosperity, and Poverty* (2012).** Source of the inclusive-versus-extractive institutions framework and the historical natural experiments discussed in the chapter.
- **Jared Diamond, *Guns, Germs, and Steel* (1997).** The best-known geographic explanation of long-run development differences, cited as the strongest counterweight to purely institutional accounts.
- **Abhijit Banerjee and Esther Duflo, *Poor Economics* (2011).** The experimental approach to development — testing specific interventions rather than debating grand causes.

## Chapter 6 — Risk, Time, and the Long Game

- **Daniel Bernoulli, "Exposition of a New Theory on the Measurement of Risk" (1738; English translation in *Econometrica*, 1954).** Analysis of the St. Petersburg paradox and the origin of the idea that money's usefulness diminishes with wealth — the founding document of expected-utility thinking.
- **Peter L. Bernstein, *Against the Gods: The Remarkable Story of Risk* (1996).** An accessible history of risk and probability, including Bernoulli's contribution.

## A note on verification

Titles, dates, and attributions above are given from general knowledge and should
be checked against the works themselves before being reused or cited elsewhere.
No page numbers, quotations, or specific empirical results are asserted here; any
figure a reader wishes to rely on should be traced to, and confirmed in, a primary
source. Nothing in this book is financial advice, and nothing on this page
