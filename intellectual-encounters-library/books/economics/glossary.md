# Glossary

## Anchoring

The tendency for a judgement or estimate to be pulled toward a reference number encountered beforehand — a "was €80, now €50" tag, an opening salary offer, a suggested tip percentage — even when that number carries no genuine information about the thing's value. Anchoring works even on people who know about it, so the reliable defence is to arrive with your own number rather than to resist in the moment.

## Brain drain

The loss to a country of its trained and talented people through emigration — doctors, nurses, engineers whose education the origin country often subsidised. The charge is real, especially for small countries losing scarce medical staff, but it is contested by "brain gain" mechanisms: the prospect of migration raises education incentives, and migrants send back money, ideas, business networks, and sometimes themselves. The net balance is unsettled and context-dependent, and the metaphor itself is loaded — people are not national property that can be drained.

## Collective bargaining

Negotiation of wages and working conditions conducted by workers as a single body — typically through a union — rather than one employee at a time. Its economic logic is bargaining power: a lone worker who cannot afford a missed paycheque takes what is offered, while a workforce that can credibly walk away together captures a larger share of the value it creates. Whether unions on net help workers overall or protect insiders at outsiders' expense is a live debate; the mechanism itself is not.

## Compensating differential

The portion of a wage that offsets a job's non-monetary characteristics — night hours, danger, monotony, insecurity — rather than paying for skill. Where workers can choose between jobs, unpleasant conditions must offer extra pay to attract anyone, which is why the night shift carries a premium. The idea goes back to Adam Smith; its dark corollary is that badly paid *and* unpleasant jobs persisting together usually signals workers who lack real alternatives.

## Compound interest

Interest calculated not only on the original sum but also on the interest already added, so the amount grows on itself and accelerates rather than rising in a straight line. The same mechanism works for a patient saver (on savings and investments) and against a borrower (on unpaid debt). Because time in the market to compound matters even more than the amount, starting small and early can beat starting large and late.

## Convergence

The prediction that poorer economies should grow faster than rich ones — because they can adopt existing technology rather than invent it — until incomes draw level. The record is mixed: the East Asian catch-ups and the post-reform growth of China and India confirm convergence can happen at historic scale, but it appears to be *conditional* on institutions and policies that permit accumulation, and many regions have stagnated for decades. The Kochi–Cologne gap is neither a law of nature nor closing on a schedule a career can wait for.

## Diversification

Spreading exposure across multiple things that are unlikely to fail together, so that no single event is ruinous. In finance, not concentrating wealth in one asset; as a life principle, applying the same logic to skills, income sources, relationships, and even documents. Its permanent price is that a diversified position never matches the single best pick in the best case; its payoff is surviving the worst one.

## Emergency buffer

A modest amount of easily accessible money set aside to absorb unexpected shocks — a lost shift, a broken laptop, an urgent flight home — without resorting to high-interest debt. Its purpose is protection, not return: it keeps one bad week from triggering a borrowing spiral and the scarcity mindset that comes with it. The right size is a personal, country-specific judgement, not a universal figure.

## Ergodicity

A property a process has when the average across many possible futures equals the average along one lifetime lived in sequence. Many real gambles lack it: a run of losses you do not survive is not redeemed by the branches where you would have won, so averaging across futures can flatter bets that can end you. The modest, safe lesson is old — protect survival first, optimise second. How far the modern "ergodicity economics" critique overturns standard decision theory is genuinely contested.

## Expected value

The probability-weighted average of an uncertain prospect's outcomes: each possible result multiplied by its chance, summed. The standard first tool for comparing gambles — it explains why lotteries are bad purchases and long-shot applications can be good ones — but it misleads when outcomes are extreme, because a small chance of ruin is not offset by a pleasant average. Bernoulli's fix: what matters is an outcome's usefulness to *you*, which depends on your buffer and obligations, not its raw euro amount.

## Human capital

The stock of skills, knowledge, health, and languages a person carries that makes their labour productive — treated, in Gary Becker's framing, like any other capital: costly to build, value-raising, and investable. Every evening spent on a language course or a certification is capital formation. Distinct from the *credential* that certifies it, which is a separate asset whose value is jurisdiction-specific.

## Informal economy

Economic activity that happens outside state registration, taxation, and regulation — street vending, cash-paid labour, unregistered workshops — which employs the majority of workers in many developing countries. Informality is a rational shelter where registering invites cost and predation, but it carries a steep price: no contract enforcement, no scale, no credit, no pension. It is one of the main channels through which weak institutions become low productivity.

## Institutions

The humanly devised rules of the game in a society — laws, property rights, courts, and the informal norms and enforcement that make them real. In the tradition of Douglass North, and of Acemoglu and Robinson's contrast between inclusive and extractive rule-systems, institutions shape whether effort is rewarded or expropriated, and are the leading (though contested) candidate for the deep cause of why nations differ in prosperity.

## Jugaad

An Indian term for the frugal, improvised workaround — the machine kept running with wire and wit, the solution assembled from whatever is at hand. It names real ingenuity flourishing without institutional support, and deserves both its celebration and an honest accounting: an economy that *must* improvise everything is an economy whose formal rules have failed it. The symptom of the constraint as much as the triumph over it.

## Loss aversion

The tendency to feel the pain of a loss more intensely than the pleasure of an equivalent gain, so that avoiding a loss motivates us more strongly than acquiring the same-sized gain. Because whether an outcome reads as a "loss" or a "gain" depends on the reference point, framing can flip the same choice from attractive to unbearable. Identified in the work of Kahneman and Tversky.

## Marginal thinking

Evaluating a decision in terms of the next unit — the next hour, the next shift, the next euro — rather than as an all-or-nothing whole. Most real choices are marginal: not "should I work while I study?" but "is *this* additional shift worth what it costs right now?" The value of the next unit, not the average, is what should drive the decision.

## Mental accounting

The habit of sorting money into mental categories — "rent money," "fun money," "the tax refund," "money for home" — and treating those categories as if they obeyed different rules, even though every euro is interchangeable with every other. Mental accounting can mislead (holding idle cash while carrying costly debt) but can also be harnessed deliberately, by building useful jars such as a separate savings account. Named by Richard Thaler.

## Opportunity cost

The value of the best alternative you did not choose. Every choice has one, because doing a thing means not doing everything else you could have done with the same time, money, or attention. The opportunity cost of an hour worked is the most valuable other use of that hour — which is why the true cost of a shift is the study, rest, or relationship it displaced, not zero.

## Optionality

The state of holding options you are not obliged to exercise: choices kept open, capacity in reserve, decisions kept reversible. Under uncertainty the right to decide later — after more is known — has real value, so a reversible path can beat a somewhat better irreversible one. But options expire and cost rent, and some central goods exist only inside irreversible commitments, so the discipline is to spend options deliberately, not hoard them indefinitely.

## Present bias

The tendency to weight immediate rewards far more heavily than future ones, and to discount the near future much more steeply than the distant future (also called hyperbolic discounting). It is the gap between the plans of the rested "future self" and the choices of the tired "present self," and the reason willpower-based saving so often fails. The robust fix binds the decision in advance rather than fighting it each day.

## Purchasing power parity

A way of comparing incomes across countries using what money actually buys locally, rather than raw exchange rates. A euro wage looks enormous converted to rupees but shrinks when spent on German rent; PPP adjustment corrects for such price differences. Even after the correction, large real wage gaps between countries remain — gaps that attach to places and their institutions, not to the people.

## Remittance

Money sent by someone working abroad back to family in their home country. Remittances can buy enormous real freedom — schooling, medicine, a repaired home — and are often among the most valuable uses of a wage. They also carry powerful mental accounting and loss aversion, and expectations that fall differently on men and women, which makes the amount and schedule a decision worth making deliberately rather than by reflex.

## Risk pooling

The arrangement by which many people each pay a small, certain amount into a common pool, from which the few who suffer a large loss are paid. It works because misfortunes are unpredictable one by one but stable in aggregate, converting a small chance of ruin for each into a small certain cost for all. The founding logic of insurance and, writ large, of the welfare state — and the reason years of paying in without claiming are the product working, not waste.

## Satisficing

Choosing an option that is good enough to meet your real needs instead of exhaustively searching for the theoretically optimal one. Coined by Herbert Simon, it reflects the fact that under limited time and attention, "good enough, and decided" reliably beats "perfect, and endlessly deferred." A budget you will actually keep beats an ideal one you abandon.

## Scarcity

The condition in which the means available — time, money, attention, energy — are limited relative to the ends they could serve. Scarcity, not money, is what makes something an economic problem: spending a resource one way forecloses every other use. It is distinct from poverty; anyone with finite time faces scarcity, though scarcity of both time and money bites hardest.

## Scarcity mindset

The cognitive load imposed by being short of something important, which captures attention and degrades the very judgement needed to manage the shortage well — a "bandwidth tax," in the phrase of Mullainathan and Shafir. It causes tunnelling on the urgent while the important sits unseen, and explains why decisions made under strain look short-sighted from outside. It is a predictable load, not a character flaw.

## Signalling

The theory, formalised by Michael Spence, that education pays partly by credibly *certifying* pre-existing ability and persistence to employers who cannot observe them directly — credible because degrees are much harder to finish for those who lack the traits. Rival (and complement) to the human-capital story in which education creates productivity; the real mixture between the two is empirically unsettled. A signal is only worth what its receiver can read, which is why credentials often lose value when they cross borders.

## Sunk cost

A cost already incurred that cannot be recovered whatever you now choose. Because it is identical under every option, a sunk cost is irrelevant to a forward-looking decision — yet the pull to "not waste" what was already spent leads people to throw good money, or scarce hours, after bad. "I've already put so much in" describes the past; it is not a reason about the future.

## Trade-off

The exchange forced by scarcity: because means are limited, more of one thing means less of another, and no one can maximise sleep, grades, income, and a social life in the same week. Naming a trade-off honestly ends the fantasy of having everything and reframes the task as choosing the mix deliberately rather than escaping the constraint.
