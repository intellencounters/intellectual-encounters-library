# Glossary

## Power

The ability to bring about an outcome one wants, especially the ability to make others act in ways they otherwise would not, even against their resistance. Following Weber, power in this broad sense covers everything from a mugger's threat to a tax authority's demand; the more useful political questions concern which power is recognised as rightful (authority) and why (legitimacy).

## Authority

Power that is recognised as rightful. Authority is the difference between "do this or I hurt you" and "do this because the rule is proper and applies." A command backed only by force is power; a command widely accepted as entitled to obedience is authority. Authority rests on legitimacy.

## Legitimacy

The widely shared belief that a particular power is entitled to be obeyed — that its commands are valid and proper, not merely enforceable. Legitimacy turns raw power into authority. Weber distinguished three ideal bases for it: tradition, charisma (the personal pull of a leader), and legal-rational rules. Legitimacy can be strong or thin, and it can be lost.

## The state

The human community that successfully claims, within a given territory, the monopoly on the legitimate use of physical force. A state is defined not by what it does — states do many different things — but by this claim to be the sole rightful wielder or authoriser of force in its territory. Where the claim holds, private violence becomes an offence and disputes go to courts rather than to feuds.

## Monopoly on the legitimate use of force

Weber's defining feature of the state: the claim to be the only body whose use of force, within a territory, is treated as rightful. It does not mean the state is the only one physically capable of violence — criminals exist — but the only one whose violence is regarded as authorised. This is why officials may do what private persons may not, and why "taking the law into your own hands" is itself punishable.

## Bureaucracy

Administration through written rules, files, defined offices, and qualified officials, each handling a case by procedure rather than by personal favour. For Weber it is the characteristic machinery of legal-rational authority — a moral achievement because its impersonality guards against favouritism, and a potential "iron cage" because the same rule-boundness can leave no one able to accommodate a reasonable but unforeseen need. Power that often works through delay rather than refusal.

## Natural rights

The view that persons possess certain moral rights simply by virtue of being human — prior to, and independent of, any government — which governments exist to protect and cannot legitimately abolish. On this view a law that violates such rights is unjust rather than valid, and appeals to a standard above the law itself. The tradition behind the idea of human rights; associated with Locke and, in critique, with Bentham.

## Social contract

The idea that political authority is grounded in an agreement — actual or hypothetical — among people to form and submit to a government, typically in exchange for protection of interests or rights they held beforehand. Used by thinkers such as Hobbes and Locke to explain why the state's power over us might be legitimate, and criticised (e.g. by Hume) where the "agreement" cannot realistically be declined.

## Legal positivism

The view that a right or a law is whatever a legal system actually recognises and enforces, and that the existence of a law is one question while its moral merit is another: a rule can be genuinely law even if unjust. Positivists insist on clarity — calling something a "right" that no institution will honour breeds false hope — without necessarily denying that unjust laws should be changed. Associated with Bentham and H. L. A. Hart.

## Rule of law

The principle that governmental power must be exercised through general, publicly known, prospective laws applied equally to all — including the powerful and the government itself — and enforced by independent courts, rather than through the arbitrary or secret will of officials. It constrains *how* power may be used but does not by itself guarantee that the laws are just.

## Due process

The requirement that before the state does something serious to a person — denying an application, ending a status, imposing a penalty — it must follow a fair, knowable procedure: notice of the case, a chance to respond, an impartial decision-maker, reasons, and usually a route of appeal. Due process converts power from a private, unaccountable act into one that must be justified and can be reviewed. Its specific form varies greatly by country.

## Democracy

A system in which political power ultimately derives from and answers to those it governs — typically through the vote, competitive elections, and freedoms of speech, association, and dissent. Its moral core is the claim that those who must live under a decision have a rightful say in making it. Democracies differ over where to draw the boundary of the voting community.

## Civil society

The sphere of voluntary associations and organised activity between the individual family and the state — unions, community and faith groups, campaigns, professional and tenants' bodies, mutual-aid networks, independent media — formed freely by people rather than directed by government. A principal arena of political participation between elections, and generally open regardless of citizenship.

## Enfranchisement

The condition of holding a formal political voice, especially the right to vote, within a political community; disenfranchisement is its absence. A person can be fully governed by a country's laws yet not enfranchised in it — the characteristic position of many long-term non-citizen residents — and can still participate substantially through civil society.

## Institution

A humanly devised rule that structures how people interact — "the rules of the game," in Douglass North's phrase, as distinct from the players (parties, firms, families) who move within them. Institutions include formal rules (constitutions, statutes, procedures) and informal ones (customs, norms, expectations); together they let millions of strangers coordinate without personal trust, by trusting the rules instead.

## Constitution

A set of rules about rules: the framework that establishes who may make ordinary law, how, and within what limits, deliberately made harder to change than the laws beneath it. Constitutions typically entrench basic rights and the machinery of government through supermajorities, special procedures, and sometimes unamendable clauses — a democracy's way of binding today's majority to commitments made in calmer deliberation.

## Precommitment

Binding yourself in advance, while calm, against what you predict you will want in a moment of pressure — Ulysses tying himself to the mast to hear the sirens safely. Applied to politics, it is the standard justification for constitutional entrenchment: a people restricting its own future majorities against panic, rage, and demagoguery. Its standing objection is the "dead hand" problem — later generations are bound by knots they never tied.

## Separation of powers

The design principle, classically formulated by Montesquieu, that the powers to make laws, to execute them, and to judge disputes under them should not be gathered in the same hands, but assigned to distinct branches — legislature, executive, judiciary — set to check one another. Madison's addition: the arrangement runs on harnessed self-interest, each office defending its own turf, not on hoped-for virtue.

## Judicial review

The power of courts to measure laws and governmental acts against the constitution and to strike down those that fail the test. Judicial review turns written constitutional limits into enforceable ones — and hands significant power to unelected judges, which is why every constitutional democracy argues persistently about how actively courts should use it. Its form and scope differ by country.

## Federalism

The vertical division of power in which a constitution assigns some subjects to a central government and others to constituent units — Länder, states, provinces — each holding constitutionally protected authority of its own rather than revocable delegations. Federalism trades uniformity for local fit, dispersed power, and room to experiment, at the price of complexity, unevenness between regions, and blurred accountability.

## Path dependence

The tendency of systems to persist because the costs of switching mount with every year of investment in the current path — trained professions, adapted contracts, settled expectations — until alternatives that might once have been better are no longer worth the wreckage of transition. It explains why institutional reform is rare, slow, and usually born of crisis, and why change runs along the grain of what exists.

## Agenda-setting

The capacity of media, simply by choosing what to cover and how prominently, to shape which issues the public regards as important. In the classic formulation, the press may not tell people what to think, but it is strikingly successful at telling them what to think *about* — a power that operates through selection and emphasis, before any question of truth or falsehood arises.

## Framing

Presenting facts within a structure that makes one interpretation natural: selecting aspects of a situation and making them salient so as to suggest what the problem is, who caused it, and what would count as fixing it. Because a frame can distort proportion and implication without asserting anything false, framing is often more powerful than lying — there is nothing to fact-check.

## Misinformation

False or misleading content spread with or without intent to deceive (deliberate deception is often distinguished as disinformation). Misinformation holds structural advantages over its corrections: it is selected for vividness, alarm, and moral charge; repetition breeds familiarity that is mistaken for truth; and a vivid story, once it organises someone's understanding, tends to keep influencing their reasoning even after they accept a correction.

## Filter bubble

The hypothesised state in which algorithmic personalisation quietly removes from a person's feed what they would disagree with, leaving each user in an information environment tailored to confirm them. The mechanism is plausible; how strongly it operates across whole populations is empirically contested, with much research finding most people's news diets more varied than the image suggests.

## Echo chamber

A social or media environment in which one's views meet mainly agreement and are thereby hardened — whether built by algorithms, by self-selection, or both. Related to but distinct from the filter bubble: a bubble is imposed by unseen filtering, while an echo chamber can be actively chosen and maintained by its members. The strength and prevalence of both effects remain debated in the research literature.

## Distributive justice

The branch of political philosophy concerned with how the benefits and burdens of a shared world — income, wealth, opportunity, risk, places in the queue — should be allocated among persons, and, in its global extension, among peoples. The great modern rivals are patterned end-state theories (Rawls), historical entitlement theories (Nozick), and capability-based approaches (Sen).

## Veil of ignorance

Rawls's thought device: principles of justice are those that rational persons would choose for society while knowing all general facts but nothing particular about themselves — not their talents, sex, caste, religion, wealth, or nationality. The veil deletes exactly the information that lets people bend rules toward their own case, so agreement behind it is meant to model fairness itself.

## Difference principle

The second part of Rawls's second principle of justice: social and economic inequalities are acceptable only if they work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged members of society. It permits incentive-based inequality when that inequality raises the position of those at the bottom, and forbids justifying an arrangement by averages or totals while the floor sinks.

## Entitlement theory

Nozick's historical account of justice in holdings: a distribution is just if it arose through just original acquisition and voluntary transfers, whatever pattern it displays, and unjust if it arose through force or fraud — with a third principle, rectification, for correcting past injustice. Its core claim against patterned theories is that liberty upsets patterns, so any pattern can be preserved only by continuous interference with free exchange.

## Capability approach

Sen's framework for evaluating social arrangements by the substantive freedoms people have to do and be what they value — to be nourished, educated, mobile, heard — rather than by resources or utility alone. Its central observation is that people convert resources into living at very different rates, so equal incomes or equal formal rights need not amount to equal real freedom.

## Luck egalitarianism

The view that inequalities flowing from unchosen luck — birth, genes, accident — are unjust and call for compensation, while inequalities flowing from genuine choice may stand. Its best-known critique, from Elizabeth Anderson, holds that it treats the imprudent harshly, humiliates the unlucky by conditioning aid on proven incompetence, and mistakes the point of equality, which is a relationship of equal standing rather than a corrected lottery.
