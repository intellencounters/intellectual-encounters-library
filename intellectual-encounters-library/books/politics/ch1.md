# 1. What Power Is

You have felt power long before you could define it. You felt it the first time you sat in a waiting room at the Ausländerbehörde with a number on a slip of paper, watching a clock, unable to leave, unable to speed anything up, entirely dependent on whether a stranger behind a glass panel would stamp a form. You felt it on a night shift when a supervisor changed the rota and there was no one to appeal to. You felt it on a video call home when a parent said, quietly, "We are only thinking of your future" — and the sentence landed with a weight that had nothing to do with law and everything to do with obligation.

These are three very different situations, and the aim of this chapter is to give you language precise enough to tell them apart. Because "power" is one of those words that seems obvious until you try to use it carefully, and then it splits into pieces.

## 1.1 The word that hides three words

At its plainest, :::glossary term="Power"::: is the ability to get an outcome you want, especially the ability to make others act in ways they otherwise would not. The sociologist Max Weber put it in almost exactly those terms: power is the chance of imposing your will within a social relationship, even against resistance.[^weber-power]

But notice how much that definition leaves unsaid. A mugger with a knife has power over you in that moment. So does a tax office. So does an employer. So, in a softer register, does a family. If we stop at "the ability to impose your will," we cannot distinguish the mugger from the magistrate — and we sense that they are not the same. The difference is not in *how much* they can make you do. It is in something else.

That something else is :::glossary term="Authority":::. Authority is power that is *recognised as rightful*. When the tax office demands money, it does not merely threaten you; it claims that it is *entitled* to demand, and — crucially — most people around you accept that claim. The mugger makes no such claim, or if he does, no one believes it. Authority is the difference between "do this or I hurt you" and "do this because it is the rule and the rule is proper."

And underneath authority sits a third word: :::glossary term="Legitimacy":::. Legitimacy is the belief, widely held, that a particular power *deserves* to be obeyed — that its commands are valid, not just enforceable. Legitimacy is what turns raw power into authority. A regime can have enormous power and thin legitimacy (people obey only from fear), or modest power and deep legitimacy (people obey because they think they should). The most stable arrangements have both.

:::definition term="Legitimacy"
The widely shared belief that a particular power is entitled to be obeyed — that its commands are valid and proper, not merely backed by force. Legitimacy is a claim made by those who hold power and a judgement made by those subject to it; where the two meet, power becomes authority. Legitimacy can rest on tradition, on law, on results, or on the personal pull of a leader, and it can be lost.
:::

## 1.2 Weber's three pure types

Where does legitimacy come from? Weber's most famous answer sorts it into three "pure types" — ideal categories that rarely appear alone in the real world but let us see what is mixed in any actual case.[^weber-types]

The first is **traditional** authority: obeyed because "it has always been so." The elder speaks and is heeded; the custom is followed because it is the custom. Much of the pressure a family exerts is traditional in exactly this sense — its force comes not from a written rule but from the felt authority of how things have always been done, of what a dutiful son or daughter owes.

The second is **charismatic** authority: obeyed because of the extraordinary personal qualities attributed to a particular individual — a prophet, a revolutionary, a founder, a magnetic boss. Charismatic authority is intense and unstable; it lives and dies with the person and their aura.

The third — the one that governs most of your official life in Germany — is **legal-rational** authority: obeyed because of impersonal rules, correctly applied. You obey the clerk not because of who she is (charisma) or because clerks have always been obeyed (tradition), but because she occupies an office defined by written procedure, and the procedure is what commands. The person is, in principle, interchangeable. This is the authority of the form, the statute, the regulation.

:::check reread="1.2"
question: A newcomer says, "I obeyed the housing office worker because she seemed like the kind of person you don't argue with." A friend replies, "No — you obeyed because the rules give her office the power to decide, whoever sits in the chair." Which of Weber's types is the friend naming?
- Traditional authority — obedience because it has always been so.
- Charismatic authority — obedience because of the official's extraordinary personal qualities.
- Legal-rational authority — obedience to an impersonal office defined by rules, not to the person in it.
- None of them; the friend is describing ordinary fear.
answer: 3
explanation: The friend relocates the source of authority from the individual to the office and its rules. That is precisely Weber's legal-rational type, in which the person is interchangeable and the procedure commands. The newcomer's first account leaned toward charisma; the correction is the whole point of the distinction.
:::

## 1.3 The state and its monopoly

Now put these pieces around the largest holder of power you deal with: :::glossary term="The state":::. What is a state, exactly? Weber again gives the sharpest working answer. A state is not defined by *what* it does — states do wildly different things — but by a particular claim it makes: the state is the human community that successfully claims the :::glossary term="Monopoly on the legitimate use of force"::: within a given territory.[^weber-state]

Read that slowly, because every word carries weight. *Monopoly*: the state claims to be the only body allowed to use violence, or to authorise it. *Legitimate*: not that it is the only one physically able to hurt people — criminals exist — but the only one whose use of force is treated as rightful. *Within a territory*: authority stops at borders, which is exactly why crossing one changes your standing so completely.

This is why a police officer may do what a private person may not, and why "taking the law into your own hands" is itself an offence. The state has, in effect, asked everyone to hand in their private capacity for force and trust it to a central authority that will use it under rules. When that bargain holds, you get order without every dispute becoming a fight. When it fails — where the state cannot make its monopoly stick — you get warlords, feuds, private security as the real law. Much of what feels reassuringly boring about life in a stable country is this monopoly quietly working.

## 1.4 Why we obey

So why do people actually comply? Not, mostly, because a soldier is standing over them. Standing armies are far too small to watch everyone. The honest answer is that legitimacy does the heavy lifting: most people, most of the time, obey because they broadly accept that they should, or because everyone around them does and defection feels both wrong and pointless.[^hume]

This raises a genuine puzzle worth sitting inside, because it is contested and there is no settled answer.

:::argument title="The consent-based account of political obligation"
conclusion: A state's authority over you is grounded, at least in part, in a kind of consent you give by participating in the society it governs.
premise: When you accept the benefits of a legal order — its roads, its courts, its protection, its currency — you take part in a shared scheme of cooperation.
premise: Accepting the benefits of a cooperative scheme, while others bear its burdens, generates an obligation to bear your share of those burdens, including obeying its rules.
premise: Continued residence and participation, when you could in principle leave, signal a tacit acceptance of the arrangement.
:::

:::counter title="Objection: tacit consent is a fiction for those who cannot really leave" to="The consent-based account of political obligation"
The strongest reply is that "consent" of this kind is barely consent at all. For most people — and especially for a migrant on a work visa, a student tied to enrolment, a person whose family and income are anchored in one place — leaving is not a real option, and an agreement you cannot decline is not an agreement. Hume made the point sharply: to say a poor peasant "consents" to a government by staying is like saying a man consents to the ship's master by remaining aboard when he was carried onto the vessel asleep and would drown if he jumped off.[^hume-consent] Critics therefore ground obligation elsewhere — in fairness, in the goods the state provides, or in there simply being no general duty to obey at all, only particular duties (not to harm, to keep promises) that the law happens to track. The debate is unresolved; be suspicious of anyone who tells you it is obvious.
:::

## 1.5 Bureaucracy: power in slow motion

There is one more form of power you will meet more often than any policeman, and it rarely raises its voice. Weber saw :::glossary term="Bureaucracy"::: as the characteristic machinery of legal-rational authority — administration by written rules, files, defined offices, and qualified officials, each handling their piece of a case according to procedure.[^weber-bureaucracy]

He admired it and feared it in the same breath. Admired, because bureaucracy is a genuine moral achievement: it replaces "who do you know" and "who does the official like" with "what do the rules say." At its best, the *Amt* treats the well-connected and the stranger by the same standard. That impersonality — the thing that feels so cold at the counter — is the very thing protecting you from favouritism.

Feared, because the same machinery can become an "iron cage": a system so rule-bound that no single person inside it can say yes to your obvious, reasonable need, because the form does not have a box for it. No one is cruel; everyone is following procedure; and you wait. This is power in slow motion — power exercised not by a decision but by a delay, not by a "no" but by a "not yet, not like this, come back with document 7b." Understanding it as *a form of power* — legitimate, rule-bound, often genuinely fair, and still capable of grinding you down — is more useful than experiencing it only as bad luck.

:::case title="The counter that cannot say yes"
Consider a composite, fictional scene familiar to many newcomers. You need a residence document to start a job; the job requires the document; the document requires proof of the job. Each official is polite. Each is, by their own rules, correct. No individual has wronged you, and yet the outcome is that a qualified person cannot work for six weeks. This is not corruption and not malice — it is legal-rational authority meeting a case its categories were not shaped for. Naming it precisely is the first step to navigating it without taking it personally.
:::

:::check reread="1.5"
question: Weber calls bureaucracy both a moral achievement and a potential "iron cage." What is the single feature that makes it *both*?
- Its secrecy — bureaucracies hide their rules, which is efficient but oppressive.
- Its impersonality — treating every case by the same rule protects against favouritism, yet can leave no one able to accommodate a genuine exception.
- Its charisma — officials inspire loyalty, which is uplifting but dangerous.
- Its cheapness — bureaucracies save money but cut corners.
answer: 2
explanation: The impersonal application of rules is the same trait seen from two sides: it is what shields the stranger from favouritism, and it is what can prevent any individual from saying yes to a reasonable but unforeseen need. The achievement and the cage are one feature, not two.
:::

## 1.6 What to carry forward

Strip the chapter to its spine. Power is the ability to make outcomes happen against resistance. Authority is power recognised as rightful. Legitimacy is the belief that grounds that recognition, and it comes in traditional, charismatic, and legal-rational flavours, usually mixed. The state is the body that successfully claims a monopoly on legitimate force in a territory, and most obedience runs on legitimacy rather than fear. Bureaucracy is that legitimacy in daily working form — impersonal, often fair, and capable of exhausting you precisely because it is following the rules.

None of this tells you what power *should* be used for. That is the next problem, and it has a name: rights.

:::reflect

Which of Weber's three sources was that power actually drawing on: tradition, charisma, or impersonal rules? Was the power *legitimate* in your eyes — did you think it was entitled to be obeyed — or merely effective? Write down your answer, and one sentence on what would have had to change for your judgement of its legitimacy to flip.
:::

[^weber-power]: Max Weber, *Economy and Society* (posthumous, 1922), defines *Macht* (power) as the probability that an actor will be able to carry out their own will despite resistance. The formulation is deliberately broad, which is why Weber immediately moves on to the narrower and more useful notions of authority and domination.

[^weber-types]: Weber's three pure types of legitimate authority — traditional, charismatic, and legal-rational — are set out in *Economy and Society* and in his lecture *Politics as a Vocation* (1919). "Pure type" (*Idealtypus*) means an analytical construct; real regimes blend all three.

[^weber-state]: The definition of the state as the human community claiming "the monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force within a given territory" comes from Weber's lecture *Politics as a Vocation* (1919).

[^hume]: David Hume argued that governments rest ultimately on opinion — that even despots command through the assent of their officials and soldiers — in his essay *Of the First Principles of Government* (1741).

[^hume-consent]: The ship analogy is from David Hume's essay *Of the Original Contract* (1748), his classic critique of the idea that mere residence amounts to consent to be governed.

[^weber-bureaucracy]: Weber's analysis of bureaucracy — hierarchy, written files, defined competences, appointment by qualification, and impersonal rule-following — is in *Economy and Society*. The "iron cage" (*stahlhartes Gehäuse*) image is from his earlier *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (1905) and is often applied to the bureaucratic order.
