# 4. Practice: What the Traditions Ask You to Actually Do

The first three chapters were about ideas, and ideas are where this book must be honest about its own limits. You can agree with every page so far — nod at the :::glossary term="Dichotomy of control":::, see the truth of :::glossary term="Impermanence":::, admire Camus' revolt — and change nothing. Reading about deadlifts builds no muscle; watching German lessons on a phone at 1 a.m. conjugates no verbs. Understanding is necessary and it is not sufficient.

The traditions knew this before we did. Every one comes packaged not just with doctrines but with a *regimen* — things to do daily, weekly, seasonally, with your body and your attention, whether or not you feel like it. This chapter looks at that regimen: meditation, prayer, journaling, fasting and voluntary discomfort, and their modern secular adaptations. As before, nothing here asks you to adopt any of it, and nothing here is medical or religious advice. We are examining practices the way earlier chapters examined arguments: laid out in their strongest form, weaknesses shown, verdict left with you.

## 4.1 Why the traditions insist on practice

Start with the claim itself, stated as the traditions would state it.

:::argument title="The case that practice, not assent, is the unit of change"
conclusion: If you want a tradition's insight to alter how you actually live, some regular practice is required; intellectual agreement alone rarely changes a person.
premise: The dispositions the traditions target — craving, reactivity, self-deception, fear of loss — are habits of attention and response, not merely mistaken opinions.
premise: Habits of attention and response are changed by repetition under mild difficulty, the way physical capacities are, not by a single act of understanding.
premise: Each tradition, independently, built daily and seasonal disciplines rather than relying on doctrine alone — a convergence that suggests their teachers all discovered the same gap between assent and transformation.
:::

The first premise is the deep one. If your problem were only a false belief — "outcomes are up to me" — a good argument would fix it. But nobody's problem is only that. You can *know* the exam result is out of your hands and still refresh the portal nine times an hour. The knowing sits in one part of you; the refreshing lives in another. Practice, on the traditions' account, is how the knowing is carried from the first part to the second — slowly, by repetition, the way an accent is acquired. Hold the objection for now; it arrives in section 4.6 with real force. First, the practices themselves.

## 4.2 Meditation: two jobs, often confused

"Meditation" in English flattens a distinction the Buddhist traditions keep sharp, and it is the most useful thing this section can give you.

The first job is settling. :::glossary term="Samatha"::: — often translated "calm abiding" — rests attention on one thing, classically the breath, returning to it each time the mind wanders. That is the whole instruction. Its aim is not to have no thoughts (a misreading that convinces beginners they are failing) but to train the *returning*: noticing you have drifted and coming back, hundreds of times, without commentary. What it cultivates is a steadier, less hijackable attention.

The second job is looking. :::glossary term="Vipassana"::: — "insight" — uses that steadied attention to *observe* experience closely: sensations, moods, thoughts, arising and passing. Its aim is not calm but seeing — in particular, seeing for yourself that experiences are impermanent, that clinging to them chafes, and that they arrive and dissolve without a little manager inside directing them. Where samatha resembles training a muscle, vipassana resembles running an experiment: Chapter 2 made claims about :::glossary term="Dukkha"::: and craving, and insight practice is the tradition's laboratory for checking them against your own mind.

The order matters, on the traditional account: settle first, then look, because an unsettled mind observes about as well as a shaken camera. Most classical lineages braid the two rather than treating them as rivals.[^satip]

:::check reread="4.2"
question: A friend tries a meditation app, has thoughts the entire time, and concludes: "I can't meditate — my mind won't go blank." Which reply best reflects the samatha/vipassana distinction?
- She is right; a wandering mind means meditation has failed.
- Blankness was never the goal: samatha trains the *return* of attention after each wander, and vipassana uses attention to *observe* experience — so noticing her thoughts, far from being failure, is the raw material of both practices.
- She should switch immediately to vipassana, which requires no attention training at all.
- Thoughts during meditation are only acceptable after ten years of practice.
answer: 2
explanation: Samatha is the practice of resting attention on an object and returning each time the mind drifts — the returning is the training, so wandering is expected, not disqualifying. Vipassana then uses that steadied attention to observe experience, thoughts included. "Empty your mind" is a caricature that sets beginners up to conclude, wrongly, that they are constitutionally unable to practise.
:::

## 4.3 Prayer, viewed philosophically

To a reader without religious belief, prayer can look like the one practice with nothing to offer — surely it is *just* asking, and if no one is listening, the line is dead. Looked at more carefully, prayer across traditions is a bundle of quite different acts. Petition — asking for help — is the piece the skeptic fixes on. But notice what a person does when they formulate a petition honestly: they are forced to say, in words, what they actually want and cannot control — the Stoic sorting exercise of Chapter 1, performed in the second person. Gratitude prayers, before meals or at day's end, deliberately redirect attention from what is missing to what is present — a discipline the Stoics ran under their own flag. Confession is a structured self-audit with the excuses stripped out. And contemplative prayer in the Christian and Sufi traditions — silent, wordless, attention resting on the divine — is, described from the outside, close kin to samatha, which is why comparative scholars keep placing them side by side.[^varieties4]

Two honesty notes, one in each direction. To the non-believer: describing prayer's psychological structure shows neither that it is *merely* psychological nor that anyone is listening; that question stands where it stood, and Chapter 5 takes it up properly. To the believer: translating prayer into "practices" may feel like the strip-mining Chapter 3 warned about — for the person praying, the point is the relationship, not the technique, and a prayer kept only for its calming effects would be, on their own terms, no longer prayer. Both cautions are fair, and we let them stand.

## 4.4 Journaling as askesis: the emperor's private notebook

The Greek philosophical schools had a word, :::glossary term="Askesis":::, that simply meant training — the same word an athlete would use. Only later did "asceticism" curdle into meaning hair shirts. In its original sense, askesis is any structured exercise done on your own character, and the Stoics' favourite instrument was writing.

The proof is sitting on the shelf. The *Meditations* of Marcus Aurelius — quoted everywhere as polished wisdom — was not a book at all. It was a private notebook, almost certainly never meant for publication, in which the most powerful man in the Roman world talked *to himself*: rehearsing the morning's principles before facing the court, scolding himself for irritation, returning again and again to :::glossary term="Memento mori"::: and the smallness of fame.[^med4] Its later Greek title just means "to himself." What survives is not Stoic doctrine but Stoic *practice* — one man's repetitions, caught on paper. Seneca describes the companion exercise: an evening review, the day replayed — where did I go wrong, what did I do, what was left undone — conducted as a fair hearing rather than a self-flogging.[^sen]

The modern translation is nearly frictionless — journaling is the practice this book's own reflection prompts have been quietly running since Chapter 1. A notes app on a night bus works. The form matters more than the medium: not a diary of events but a dialogue with yourself about your own responses — morning rehearsal (what will test me today, and what would my best judgement look like?), evening review (what actually happened, and what does it teach?). The discipline is in the honesty, and honesty is easier in writing, because a written excuse looks like what it is.

:::case title="Two notebooks"
A composite, fictional pair. Priya, between a warehouse shift and a statistics lecture, keeps a nightly note on her phone: three lines, never more — where she lost her temper or her nerve, where she held it, one thing to try tomorrow. Her flatmate Arun keeps the journal the internet suggested: affirmations, gratitude lists from a template, five pages when motivation strikes, nothing for weeks after. After six months, Priya can point to two patterns she found and changed — she snaps when ashamed of not knowing something; she avoids her supervisor's emails exactly as long as they stay unread. Arun has a warm feeling about journaling. The difference is not effort or sincerity. Priya's notebook interrogates her; Arun's flatters him. Askesis is the first kind.
:::

## 4.5 Fasting and voluntary discomfort

The strangest family of practices, to a modern eye, is deliberate hardship — and it is also the most universal. Muslims fast through Ramadan's daylight hours; many Hindus keep vrat fasts; Christians have Lent; Jews, Yom Kippur; many Buddhist monastics do not eat after midday; and the Stoics, with no god requiring it, practised voluntary poverty anyway — Seneca advises setting aside days to eat little, dress roughly, and ask, of the condition you fear, *is this what I was afraid of?*[^sen] To be clear about register: we are examining the philosophical logic of these practices, not their health effects, which are outside this book's competence and none of its business.

Why would traditions that disagree about everything else all institutionalise discomfort? Three shared logics are visible. First, *rehearsal*: fear of loss shrinks when you have visited the loss on purpose and returned. Second, *decoupling*: hunger arrives and, held inside a fast, is observed rather than obeyed — the direct experience that a desire can be felt without being acted on, which is Chapter 2's :::glossary term="Non-attachment"::: taught through the body instead of the mind. Third, *solidarity and memory*: communal fasts bind a community and point it at something — the poor, the dead, the divine — beyond appetite. The third logic is unavailable to a purely private adaptation — a loss we will meet again shortly.

:::reflect
Without adopting any practice, run the audit. Which of this chapter's four families — attention training, structured address (prayer or its secular analogues), written self-examination, voluntary discomfort — does your life already contain *in any form*, even unlabelled (a long run is voluntary discomfort; a Sunday call home can be an examination of the week)? Which is entirely absent? For the absent one, write down what claim about your own mind you would be testing if you tried it for two weeks, and what result you predict. You are designing the experiment, not committing to it — but write the prediction down, because a prediction you can be wrong about is what separates self-examination from self-flattery.
:::

## 4.6 Lost in translation: the secular adaptations

All four families now circulate in secular form: app-based mindfulness, gratitude journal prompts, Stoic-flavoured productivity writing, cold showers and fasting regimens. This book cannot consistently object; it has treated the traditions as philosophies to borrow from. But Chapter 3 flagged the cost of borrowing, and here the flag has a name. Critics call the corporate, app-mediated version :::glossary term="McMindfulness":::, and their argument deserves its strongest form.

:::counter title="Objection: extraction changes what the practice is" to="The case that practice, not assent, is the unit of change"
The critique runs: traditional practice came embedded in three things the secular versions quietly drop — an ethical framework (in Buddhism, meditation is one limb of a path that includes right conduct and livelihood), a community that holds you accountable, and a diagnosis that points at *craving itself*, not at stress. Strip those away and the same techniques change function: attention training becomes a performance enhancer; acceptance, marketed to an exhausted workforce, becomes a way to metabolise conditions — understaffing, precarity, unreasonable targets — that ought to be contested rather than breathed through. That last step is Chapter 1's "tolerating the intolerable" worry, now with a business model: the apps do not deliver a smaller dose of the tradition but a different product in the tradition's packaging. The reply — that a stressed worker's relief is real, that secular access reaches people the tradition never would, and that nothing stops a practitioner adding the ethics back — is also serious. But the critics are right that "add the ethics back" is precisely what the subscription does not prompt, and a borrowed practice should be borrowed with its eyes open.
:::

:::check reread="4.6"
question: What is the fairest statement of the "McMindfulness" critique of secular mindfulness?
- That meditation techniques have no effects at all outside a monastery.
- That extracting the techniques from their ethical framework, community, and diagnosis of craving changes their function — risking a tool that helps individuals absorb conditions that ought instead to be contested — even though the relief it offers is real.
- That anyone who uses a meditation app is acting immorally.
- That secular people are incapable of learning attention-training practices.
answer: 2
explanation: The steel-manned critique is not that the techniques are fake or the users foolish — the relief is granted as real. It is that the traditional context (ethics, community, a diagnosis aimed at craving rather than stress) did load-bearing work, and that without it the same practice can slide into helping people accommodate what should be challenged. It is an argument about function and framing, not a blanket condemnation.
:::

One more honesty is owed, about the science — and why this chapter has claimed only what the traditions claim.

:::uncertainty title="What contemplative science can and cannot yet say"
A large research literature on meditation now exists, and readers will meet confident summaries in both directions — "proven benefits" and "all hype." Neither confidence is warranted. The field's own methodologists note real difficulties: many studies are small, a convincing placebo for meditation is hard to design, effects often shrink against active control groups, long-term and adverse effects are understudied, and "meditation" in the literature covers many practices at many doses. Whether specific programmes produce specific durable benefits, of what size, for whom, is genuinely contested among researchers. Citation needed — verify before publishing; this book therefore asserts no clinical or performance claims for any practice in this chapter, and a reader should treat the enthusiastic and the debunking headline with the same raised eyebrow. The attribution of the coinage "McMindfulness" also varies across sources and should be checked before naming a coiner in print.
:::

Where does that leave practice? Roughly here: the claim that repetition, not assent, changes a person is as old as every tradition in this book, and testable in the only laboratory you fully control. The next chapter turns to the question that has been waiting politely since Chapter 1 — not what the traditions ask you to do, but whether what they *believe* can be believed.

[^satip]: Classical Buddhist discourses — for example the *Satipatthana Sutta* ("The Foundations of Mindfulness") in the Pali canon — treat calm and insight as cooperating aspects of one training. Textual details and translations vary; verify against a named edition before quoting.

[^med4]: Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* — a private notebook of self-addressed exercises, not a treatise; the traditional Greek title, *Ta eis heauton*, is usually rendered "to himself." Numbering varies by edition; verify any passage before quoting.

[^varieties4]: William James, *The Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902), the classic comparative study of prayer, mysticism, and contemplative states, examined psychologically without settling their metaphysics. Verify passages before quoting.

[^sen]: Seneca, *Letters to Lucilius* (*Epistulae Morales*), commends practising poverty — set days of plain food and rough dress, asking "is this the condition I feared?"; Seneca depicts a nightly self-examination in *On Anger*. Numbering and wording vary by edition; verify before quoting.
