# Glossary

## Primary source

A trace of the past made *within* the period being studied, by someone in a position to know — a letter, ledger, photograph, treaty, wage book, diary, or physical object. Raw material for history, not truth in itself: a primary source records what someone wrote, drew, or made at the time, which still has to be cross-examined for bias, error, and interest.

## Secondary source

A later account that *interprets* primary sources — a historian's book or article, a documentary. It is a colleague's argument to be weighed against the evidence, not a verdict to be copied. Confusing a secondary source's confidence for direct knowledge of the past is a common error.

## Historiography

The study of how history itself has been written: which questions were asked, whose records survived and whose were lost or destroyed, and how interpretations have changed over time. Attending to historiography is part of reading evidence honestly, because every account is shaped by the standpoint and concerns of the person who wrote it.

## Presentism

The error of judging the past by the assumptions, categories, and vocabulary of the present without noticing you are doing so — reading today's concepts back onto people who did not possess them. Presentism can make us either condescend to the past or surrender to it; both mistakes treat the past as a single settled thing rather than as contested and changing.

## Anachronism

A detail placed in the wrong time — a concept, object, word, or attitude that did not exist in the period where it is being used. A close cousin of presentism; the wristwatch in a film about an ancient court is the classic example.

## Causation

One thing genuinely bringing another about. Establishing causation is harder than noticing that two things occur together, and in history — where events cannot be re-run — it usually requires disciplined counterfactual reasoning about what would plausibly have happened otherwise.

## Correlation

Two things moving or occurring together. Correlation may reflect one thing causing the other, both being caused by a third factor, or mere coincidence. Mistaking correlation for causation is as dangerous when reasoning about the past as when reading a spreadsheet.

## Counterfactual

A disciplined thought experiment about what would plausibly have happened if some cause had been absent or different. Used to test whether a supposed cause actually mattered; it must be tethered to what was really possible at the time, or it becomes idle speculation.

## Modernity

Historians' shorthand for the connected cluster of changes — industrial production, cities, wage labour, mass literacy, the nation-state, science as authority, and the self as an individual project — that reshaped much of the world over recent centuries, unevenly and at different times. A useful description, not a magic threshold or a value judgement; told as a single story of progress, it risks becoming a grand narrative.

## Periodisation

The act of dividing the past into named periods or eras — "medieval," "modern," and so on — and deciding where one stops and the next begins. Periodisation is a choice made by historians for particular purposes, not a natural feature of time, and reasonable scholars draw the boundaries differently.

## Time-discipline

The trained, internalised sense that time is a measurable resource to be spent, saved, or wasted, and that punctuality is a moral duty. E. P. Thompson argued this was cultivated on a mass scale by industrial wage labour, where time rather than the task became the unit of value, enforced by bells, clocks, and fines.

## Grand narrative

A single, sweeping, tidy story claimed to explain the broad course of history — often a story of progress. Grand narratives are useful for orientation but dangerous when they centre one group's path, cast others as merely "behind," or smuggle a value judgement in as if it were a neutral chronology.

## Diaspora

A population settled far from an ancestral homeland while retaining ties — cultural, familial, economic, or imagined — to it. One of the oldest social forms in human history, encompassing movement that was freely chosen as well as movement that was coerced, forced, or driven by famine and war.

## Orientalism

Originally the Western academic study of Asian languages and cultures; sharpened by Edward Said into a critique of the West's long habit of studying and representing "the East" in ways entangled with power over it. The knowledge produced was often real, but framed by the interests and fantasies of the studier. Germany's colony-less Indology is a much-debated test case for how far the critique reaches.

## Indology

The academic discipline devoted to the languages, texts, history, and cultures of the Indian subcontinent, built into a national specialty in nineteenth-century German-speaking universities. Its achievements in editing and translation were real; so were the Romantic projections and "golden age, degraded present" storylines woven through them.

## Comparative philology

The nineteenth-century discipline of reconstructing the family relationships of languages by systematically comparing their grammars, roots, and sound changes. It established the kinship of Sanskrit, Greek, Latin, and their relatives (the Indo-European family) — a solid finding later abused when others leapt from shared language to imagined shared race.

## Longue durée

Fernand Braudel's term for the "long duration": the slow, deep layer of historical time — climate, geography, crops, diseases, the settled habits of daily life — that changes over centuries and constrains the faster layers of economic cycles and political events above it. A reminder that the load-bearing parts of history move far more slowly than the news.

## History from below

The approach, associated above all with E. P. Thompson, that treats ordinary people — workers, peasants, the defeated and obscure — as historical actors with their own reasoning, rather than as raw material processed by great events. It widens what counts as a source: court records, wage books, songs, and ledgers read against the grain.

## Guild

An association of masters, journeymen, and apprentices that organised a skilled urban trade in medieval and early modern Europe, regulating entry, training, quality, and price. Guilds made work a lifelong communal identity, but were also closed, hierarchical, and exclusionary — including toward women and outsiders. Their ghost survives in the German apprenticeship system.

## Separate spheres

The nineteenth-century ideology holding that men belong to the public world of work and politics and women to the private world of home and virtue. Preached most confidently by and about the middle classes, contradicted throughout by the waged work of poorer women, and the datable source of the belief that a "traditional" household has a sole male breadwinner.

## Collective memory

Maurice Halbwachs's term for the shared representation of the past that a group — family, faith, class, nation — maintains, and that maintains the group. Memory in this sense is functional: it selects, simplifies, and moralises the past to bind a community, which makes it a different instrument from evidence-bound history, not a rival copy of it.

## Sites of memory

Pierre Nora's term (lieux de mémoire) for the memorials, anniversaries, archives, anthems, and symbols in which a modern nation deliberately deposits its past. On Nora's account, a society builds such sites precisely when the unselfconscious, lived transmission of tradition has weakened — it no longer inhabits its past, but curates it.

## Vergangenheitsbewältigung

German for "working through" or "coming to terms with" the past: the decades-long, contested process by which German society confronted the crimes of the Nazi period through trials, scholarship, education, political symbolism, and memorials. Not a finished achievement but an ongoing public argument — and the world's most discussed case study in deliberately remembering a nation's crimes.

## Invented tradition

Eric Hobsbawm and Terence Ranger's term for a practice of recent, datable origin that claims — and ritually implies — deep continuity with the past. Many national ceremonies and "ancient" customs were manufactured in the nineteenth century. An invented tradition may still be valuable; what it forfeits is the authority of age, which is exactly what it borrows.
