# 1. How We Know What Happened

At two in the morning, somewhere between the fryer and the drive-through window, a colleague says the thing people say to end an argument: "That's just how it's always been." Maybe it is about who cooks at home, or how much respect a manager is owed, or whether a woman should move cities for a job. The phrase is meant to close the subject. It sounds like a fact about the past. It is almost never a fact about the past. It is a claim — usually a false one — and learning to test it is the first and most practical thing history teaches.

This chapter is not about kings and dates. It is about how anyone can *know* something happened, and how much confidence that knowledge deserves. If you can see how a historian builds a claim about the past out of fragments, you gain something you can use on a Tuesday: a way to loosen the grip of "always" and "everyone" and "naturally," which are the words expectation hides behind.

## 1.1 What history is, and what it isn't

History is not the past. The past is gone; it does not sit in an archive waiting to be looked at. History is what we can responsibly reconstruct of the past from the traces it left — documents, objects, buildings, bones, songs, account books, court records — plus the reasoning that connects those traces to conclusions. This is why two honest historians, looking at the same evidence, can disagree: they are not disagreeing about a videotape they have both watched, but about the best explanation of an incomplete set of clues.

That makes history sound weaker than it is. It is not weaker; it is a different kind of strength. A verdict reached from evidence, argued in public, and open to revision when new evidence appears is exactly what we mean by knowledge in a court, a laboratory, or a newsroom. The historian Marc Bloch, who wrote about the craft of his trade while in hiding during the Second World War, compared the historian to an examining magistrate: the witnesses cannot be trusted just because they are witnesses; every testimony has to be cross-examined.[^bloch]

[^bloch]: Marc Bloch, *The Historian's Craft* (written 1941–43, published posthumously in French in 1949 as *Apologie pour l'histoire*). Bloch, a founder of the *Annales* school, was executed by the Gestapo in 1944 for his work in the French Resistance; the book was unfinished at his death.

## 1.2 Two kinds of source

The magistrate's first question is: who is telling me this, and how would they know? Historians divide their evidence into two broad kinds.

A :::glossary term="Primary source"::: is a trace made *within* the period being studied, by someone in a position to know — a letter, a photograph, a ledger, a treaty, a factory's wage book, a migrant's diary. It is raw material, not truth: a wage book tells you what a firm recorded paying, which is not always what it paid.

A :::glossary term="Secondary source"::: is a later account that *interprets* primary sources — a historian's book, an article, a documentary. It is a colleague's argument, to be weighed, not a verdict to be copied.

The distinction matters the moment someone quotes "history" at you. When your uncle says dowry, or the joint family, or a particular idea of the man's role "goes back thousands of years," ask the magistrate's question: on the strength of what source? Often the honest answer is that the claim rests on a secondary account that itself rests on very thin primary evidence, or on no evidence at all beyond the fact that the speaker heard it as a child.

:::check reread="1.2"
question: A friend forwards a message claiming that "for 3,000 years our people have celebrated this festival in exactly this way." What is the single most useful first question to ask?
- Is the person forwarding it trustworthy?
- What primary source — from anywhere near that period — records the practice, and does it actually describe "exactly this way"?
- Does the festival feel meaningful to me personally?
- Do most people in the group chat already agree with it?
answer: 2
explanation: Trust in the messenger and personal meaning are real, but they are not evidence about the past. The historian's move is to ask what trace from the period itself supports the claim, and whether that trace really says what the claim says — continuity over millennia is exactly the sort of assertion that usually dissolves when you look for a primary source.
:::

## 1.3 Causation is not correlation — even backwards in time

Suppose you notice that in the years a country grew richer, its birth rate fell. Did wealth *cause* the smaller families? Maybe. Or maybe both were caused by something else — girls staying in school longer, cities pulling people off the land, cheaper contraception. Or maybe the causal arrow runs the other way, and smaller families freed up money. The pattern alone cannot tell you.

This is the difference between :::glossary term="Correlation"::: — two things moving together — and :::glossary term="Causation"::: — one thing bringing another about. The trap is exactly as dangerous in history as in a spreadsheet at work. Because the events are in the past and cannot be re-run, historians reach for a disciplined thought experiment: the :::glossary term="Counterfactual":::. To claim that a cause *mattered*, you have to be able to say, at least roughly, what would plausibly have happened without it. If the outcome would have arrived anyway, the "cause" was a passenger, not a driver.

Counterfactual reasoning is easy to abuse — you can imagine any alternative you like — so historians keep it tethered to what was actually possible at the time, given the tools, ideas, and constraints people really had. Used carefully, it is a guard against the laziest form of historical storytelling: the one that treats whatever happened as the only thing that *could* have happened, and therefore as natural, and therefore as your fate.

## 1.4 The historian in the picture: historiography and bias

There is no view from nowhere. Every account is written by someone, from somewhere, with questions shaped by their own time. The study of how history has been written — which questions got asked, whose records survived, whose were destroyed or never made — is called :::glossary term="Historiography":::. It is not a distraction from "the facts." It is part of reading the facts honestly.

Consider whose voices reach us. For most of the past, writing was the privilege of a small elite — rulers, priests, merchants, tax collectors. The people who cooked, carried, farmed, and were bought and sold left far fewer written traces, and those traces were often recorded *by* the powerful, in the language of the powerful. So an archive is not a neutral mirror of the past; it is a survivor, and it survived for reasons. When a record says nothing about ordinary women's work, or about a caste's own account of itself, that silence is data too — evidence about who held the pen, not proof that nothing happened.

:::argument title="The case for treating the past as knowable"
conclusion: We can have genuine, if provisional, knowledge of the past, and the honest response to bias and gaps in the record is better method, not despair.
premise: Independent kinds of evidence — documents, material objects, physical remains, later corroborating accounts — can converge on the same conclusion, and convergence from independent sources is hard to explain except by its being roughly true.
premise: The biases in the record are themselves partly knowable: we can often say who made a source, for whom, and what they had reason to hide or exaggerate, and correct for it.
premise: A method that is public and open to revision lets errors be caught and fixed over time, which is what distinguishes knowledge from mere assertion.
:::

:::counter title="Objection: the gaps go deeper than method can reach" to="The case for treating the past as knowable"
The strongest reply is not that history is worthless but that its silences are not randomly distributed — they are structured by the very power the historian is trying to see past. If the enslaved, the illiterate, the colonised, and the poor left few records *because* they were denied the pen, then "better method" still works only on what survived, and can quietly reproduce the winners' version by mistaking the loudest evidence for the whole. Some historians of subaltern and colonised peoples argue that recovering these lives sometimes requires reading sources *against the grain* — using a tax record or a criminal trial to glimpse the person it was meant to control — and that even then, honesty means admitting how much is simply lost. This does not defeat the argument in 1.4, but it sets a real limit on it: convergence can only converge on what was written down.
:::

## 1.5 Presentism: the mirror we mistake for a window

The subtlest error is to judge the past by the assumptions of the present without noticing you are doing it — reading today's categories back into people who did not have them. Historians call it :::glossary term="Presentism":::. Its cousin is the :::glossary term="Anachronism":::, the stray detail from the wrong time: a wristwatch in a film about the Mughal court.

Presentism cuts both ways, which is why it is worth naming. It makes us congratulate ourselves too easily ("people back then were simply ignorant") and it makes us surrender too easily to tradition ("they did it this way, so it must have deep reasons we've forgotten"). Both mistakes assume the past was a single, settled thing. It rarely was. The people you are told "always" lived a certain way were themselves arguing, changing, borrowing from neighbours, and complaining that *their* grandparents had done it differently.

:::reflect
Pick one expectation you feel most sharply right now — about marriage, money sent home, career, caregiving, or "success" — that is defended to you with the words "this is how it has always been." Write down, as specifically as you can: (1) how far back "always" is actually being claimed to reach, and (2) what single primary source, if it existed, would make you believe the claim. Then note whether you have ever seen anything close to that source. Keep this; Chapter 2 will give you reason to suspect the answer is "much more recent than you were told."
:::

## 1.6 Why "it's always been this way" is usually false

Put the tools together and a pattern appears. Claims of timeless continuity almost always fail at least one test: no primary source reaches as far back as claimed; a correlation is being sold as a cause; the "tradition" turns out to have a datable beginning; or a present-day category is being smuggled onto people who never held it. This is not a licence to believe *nothing* is old. Some things genuinely are. It is a licence to ask, every single time, *how do you know?* — and to notice how often the honest answer is "I don't; I was simply told."

:::uncertainty title="How often is 'timeless tradition' actually recent?"
It is tempting to assert a figure — "most traditions people call ancient are less than two centuries old." I cannot responsibly put a number on that; the claim in this section is a qualitative pattern that historians of "invented tradition" have documented in many specific cases, not a measured proportion across all cultures. Citation needed — verify before publishing. Treat Section 1.6 as a well-supported *habit of suspicion*, not a quantified law.
:::

That habit of suspicion is the gift. It does not tell you what to choose about your own marriage, money, or move to another country. It clears the ground so that the choice can be *yours* — made in the open, for reasons you could state, rather than surrendered in advance to a past that, on inspection, turns out to be far younger and far more argued-over than anyone told you.

:::check reread="1.5"
question: A documentary shows a medieval village and, in voiceover, praises the peasants for "bravely resisting the patriarchy." Setting aside whether their society was unequal, what methodological error is the voiceover most clearly committing?
- Correlation mistaken for causation.
- Presentism — applying a modern concept and vocabulary the people themselves did not possess, and judging them by it.
- Relying on a secondary rather than a primary source.
- Building an untethered counterfactual.
answer: 2
explanation: The villagers could not have "resisted the patriarchy" as an idea, because the concept and word belong to a much later moral vocabulary. Reading present-day categories back onto past people — and grading them against those categories — is presentism. The society may well have been unequal; the error is in the anachronistic framing, not in noticing inequality.
:::
