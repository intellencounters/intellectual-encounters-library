# Glossary

## Stress response

The body's coordinated physiological reaction to a perceived demand or threat — activation of the sympathetic nervous system and the release of hormones such as adrenaline and cortisol, producing raised heart rate, heightened alertness, and the temporary shutdown of non-urgent systems like digestion and immune repair. A mobilisation for action, well suited to a brief physical emergency and poorly suited to a chronic psychological one.

## Appraisal

In the transactional model of stress (Lazarus and Folkman), the mind's rapid evaluation of a situation that largely determines the stress response: a *primary* appraisal of whether the situation threatens something one values, and a *secondary* appraisal of whether one has the resources to cope. Because appraisals are judgements, they can sometimes be examined and revised where the underlying event cannot.

## Allostatic load

The cumulative cost to the body of adapting to repeated or chronic stress — the wear and tear that accrues when the stress response is triggered too often, stays on too long, or fails to switch off after a demand has passed. It locates the danger of stress not in any single episode but in the absence of recovery between episodes.

## Burnout

A syndrome arising from chronic workplace stress, characterised (following Maslach) by three dimensions: emotional exhaustion, cynicism or detachment from the work, and a reduced sense of accomplishment. It is driven less by sheer workload than by persistent mismatch between a person and their work around control, fairness, reward, community, or meaning. Not a formal clinical diagnosis, and better understood as an injury with nameable causes than as a personal weakness.

## Intrinsic motivation

The drive to do an activity because it is inherently interesting, satisfying, or expressive of who one is trying to become — the activity is its own reward. In self-determination theory it is nourished by autonomy, competence, and relatedness, and it is more durable and less tiring than motivation that depends on external pressure.

## Extrinsic motivation

The drive to do an activity for a consequence separable from the activity itself — pay, grades, approval, avoidance of punishment. It is effective but costly, requiring continual reinforcement and tending to wear thin under fatigue. Extrinsic and intrinsic motivation can coexist, and an extrinsically driven task can often be shifted partway toward intrinsic motivation by changing its conditions.

## Self-determination theory

Deci and Ryan's account of human motivation, holding that people flourish and become intrinsically motivated when three basic psychological needs are met: autonomy (a sense of ownership over one's actions), competence (a sense of effective progress), and relatedness (a sense of connection to others). Where these needs are frustrated, motivation and well-being suffer even when external rewards are present.

## Emotion regulation

The processes by which people influence which emotions they have, when they have them, and how they experience and express them. Because avoidance genuinely relieves the discomfort a task provokes, procrastination is best understood as a strategy of emotion regulation — short-term mood repair at the expense of longer-term goals — rather than as a failure of time management.

## Habit loop

The three-part cycle underlying habitual behaviour: a *cue* (a trigger such as a time, place, preceding action, or emotional state), a *routine* (the behaviour itself), and a *reward* (whatever the brain registers as worth repeating). Once established, the cue summons the routine automatically, without fresh motivation — which is why redesigning cues and frictions is a more reliable way to change behaviour than trying to want it more.

## Self-control

The capacity to align behaviour with longer-term goals in the face of competing impulses. Rather than a fixed quantity of willpower to be spent in the moment, it is more usefully treated as a design problem: people who appear highly self-controlled tend to structure their environments to avoid temptation rather than to resist it repeatedly. Some influential willpower findings, including "ego depletion," have faced replication difficulties.

## Self-concept

The organised, relatively stable set of beliefs a person holds about themselves — traits, abilities, values, social roles, and the narrative that links them into a continuous identity. It is not simply discovered by introspection but substantially constructed through experience and through how one is seen and treated by others, which is why major changes of context can disrupt it.

## Attachment style

A person's characteristic working model of closeness, learned from early relationships with caregivers and carried into adult relationships. Broad patterns — secure, anxious, avoidant — describe how one seeks and responds to intimacy under stress. Best treated as continuous dimensions that can shift with experience, not as fixed and permanent types.

## Belongingness

The proposal (Baumeister and Leary) that the drive to form and maintain a few stable, caring relationships is a fundamental human motivation, comparable to basic drives like hunger, with wide effects on emotion, cognition, and physical health when it is unmet. Belonging is thus a precondition for functioning, not merely a reward for having already succeeded.

## Loneliness

The distressing feeling that arises from a gap between the social connection a person wants and the connection they actually have. Defined by quality rather than headcount, it can be understood as a signal — the social equivalent of hunger — that points toward an unmet need rather than a personal flaw. Chronic loneliness can bias perception toward rejection, complicating its own relief.

## Emotional granularity

The ability to distinguish and label emotional states with precision — knowing the difference, in oneself, between anger and hurt, anxiety and excitement, envy and admiration. Associated in research (notably by Lisa Feldman Barrett and colleagues) with more flexible and effective emotion regulation, plausibly because a precisely named feeling points toward a specific situation and response, where an undifferentiated "bad" points nowhere.

## Cognitive reappraisal

An emotion-regulation strategy that acts early in the emotional episode by changing the interpretation that is generating the feeling — finding a truer or less catastrophic reading of the situation before the emotion fully unfolds. In James Gross's research it tends to reduce the felt emotion at little physiological cost, though reinterpreting a genuinely unjust situation into acceptability is anaesthesia, not regulation.

## Expressive suppression

An emotion-regulation strategy that acts late in the emotional episode by inhibiting the outward display of a feeling that has already occurred — the composed face over the tight chest. It tends to leave the feeling and its physiology intact while consuming attention and subtly cooling social interactions. Sometimes exactly right as a temporary postponement; costly as a way of life.

## Guilt

The moral emotion that says *I did a bad thing* — focused on a specific act rather than the whole self. In June Price Tangney's research its natural motion is repair: apology, compensation, doing differently. In proportion, and correctly addressed, it is a functional signal; guilt felt for choices that wronged no one is misaddressed and can be declined.

## Shame

The moral emotion that says *I am a bad thing* — focused on the self rather than any specific act. Its natural motions are hiding, withdrawal, and counter-attack rather than repair, which is why it is generally less functional than guilt. Distinguishing the two determines whether a painful feeling points toward something repairable or merely away from the people it concerns.

## Envy

The pang produced by an unfavourable comparison between one's own situation and another's — endemic on social media, where curated peaks are compared against one's own unedited life. Read carefully, envy maps what a person actually wants, often more honestly than their official answers; what it is not is evidence about the other person's real life.

## Repair attempt

Any small move during conflict — humour, touch, apology, a softened restart, an explicit pause — whose function is to de-escalate and signal that the relationship matters more than the current exchange. In observational research on couples, whether repair attempts are offered and, above all, received distinguishes stable from distressed relationships better than the frequency of conflict itself.

## Self-disclosure

The gradual, reciprocal sharing of personal information through which intimacy is built — in Altman and Taylor's social penetration account, a widening and deepening from safe outer layers toward what is guarded, with each disclosure a small risk that invites a matching one. Trust grows as a staircase of held disclosures, not as a switch.

## Boundary

A stated condition under which one can stay in good connection with another person — what one will and will not do, can and cannot absorb. A well-made boundary regulates the maker's own conduct rather than attempting to control the other person's, and functions as relationship maintenance: it converts private, building resentment into a public, negotiable expectation.

## Big Five

The five broad trait dimensions along which personality research commonly describes people: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and emotional stability (versus neuroticism). Longitudinal work shows both considerable rank-order stability on these dimensions and genuine mean-level change across adulthood.

## Self-efficacy

In Albert Bandura's theory, a person's belief in their capacity to execute a specific course of action — domain-specific rather than a single dial of confidence. It shapes whether one attempts, how long one persists, and how setbacks are read, and it is built most strongly by graduated mastery experiences: actually succeeding, at manageable scale, before feeling confident.

## Growth mindset

Carol Dweck's term for the belief that abilities can be developed through effort and strategy, contrasted with a fixed mindset. Influential and subsequently contested: replication work has shrunk the claimed effects substantially, while large trials retain evidence of small, real benefits concentrated among those who currently believe their ability is fixed. Oversold as a universal key; not empty.

## Resilience

The capacity to withstand or recover from adversity. In developmental research (Ann Masten's "ordinary magic") it is the usual outcome of ordinary adaptive systems doing their work — at least one solid relationship, functioning self-regulation, a sense of agency, communities that hold — rather than a rare inner steel, and it fails mainly when adversity knocks out those systems.

## Post-traumatic growth

Reported positive change following serious adversity — deepened relationships, shifted priorities, a stronger sense of capability — described by Tedeschi and Calhoun. Genuinely contested: perceived growth and demonstrated before-to-after change are only loosely related in comparative studies, and reported growth often coexists with continuing distress. Growth after adversity happens; it is neither guaranteed nor owed.
