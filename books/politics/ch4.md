# 4. The Machinery: How Institutions Shape an Ordinary Tuesday

Take an ordinary Tuesday. Your shift starts at six; the tram runs on a timetable set by the city. Your payslip arrives with deductions at rates set by a parliament you may not vote for. Your cousin in India messages that his state has changed its school-leaving exam again, while a friend in another Land has school holidays in entirely different weeks from yours. The shop under your flat is shut, by law, on Sunday; the shop under your cousin's flat in Chennai is open every day of the year.

None of this is anyone's personality. No individual decided your Tuesday. It was shaped by machinery — rules that were there before you arrived and will be there after you leave. This chapter is about that machinery: what institutions are, why constitutions tie the hands of the very majorities that could change them, why power gets split into pieces, why the same question gets different answers in different Länder or Indian states, and why systems, once running, are so hard to redirect.

## 4.1 The rules of the game

Political scientists use the word :::glossary term="Institution"::: in a way broader than "a building with a logo." An institution, in this sense, is a humanly devised rule structuring how people interact — the rules of the game, as the economist Douglass North famously put it, as distinct from the players.[^north] A parliament, a constitution, a tax code: institutions. A particular party, firm, or family: players, moving within the rules.

Two features of this definition earn their keep. First, institutions include the *informal* as well as the formal: "arrive on time" is written in no statute, and "the eldest son provides" in no Indian law, yet you know the force of both — and much of the friction of migration is formal rules from one system meeting informal rules from another. Second, the definition explains how millions of strangers coordinate: you and the pension office do not trust each other personally; you both trust the rules. Institutions are frozen agreements — argued out by people now dead, left standing so each generation need not fight every fight again. That is their gift, and, as the chapter's end will show, their danger.

## 4.2 Constitutions: the promise you make to your future self

A :::glossary term="Constitution"::: is the strangest institution of all: a set of rules *about* rules — who may make ordinary law, how, within what limits — deliberately made harder to change than the laws beneath it. Notice how odd that is: in a democracy the people rule, yet a constitution exists to tell today's majority that there are things it may not do.

The classical image for why comes from Homer. Ulysses, wanting to hear the sirens' song without steering into the rocks, had himself tied to the mast *in advance*, ordering his crew to ignore his later commands. Philosophers call this :::glossary term="Precommitment"::: — binding yourself now, while calm, against what you predict you will want in the storm.[^elster] A constitution is a society tied to the mast: written in a cool hour to hold in hot ones — the hour of panic after an attack, of rage at an unpopular minority, of a leader promising to fix everything if the limits are suspended once. Entrenchment — supermajorities, special procedures, sometimes unamendable clauses — is the rope.

:::argument title="The precommitment case for constitutional limits"
conclusion: A democratic people has good reason to entrench certain rules beyond the reach of ordinary majorities, even though this constrains democracy itself.
premise: Majorities, like individuals, are predictably tempted in moments of fear or anger toward decisions they would condemn in calm reflection — especially decisions targeting unpopular minorities.
premise: Rules adopted deliberately, in advance, behind procedures that force delay and broad agreement, are more likely to reflect a people's considered judgement than rules adopted in a crisis.
premise: Some harms — to basic rights, to the electoral machinery itself — are irreversible or self-entrenching, so waiting for the majority to regret them is not a workable remedy.
:::

:::counter title="Objection: the dead hand of the past" to="The precommitment case for constitutional limits"
The strongest reply asks a blunt question: *who* tied the knots? Ulysses bound himself; a constitution binds people who never agreed to it — every later generation, and every newcomer arriving under it. Jefferson pressed exactly this point: "the earth belongs to the living," and no generation has a right to bind the next.[^jefferson] The framers were particular people, with blind spots and interests, and entrenchment freezes the blind spots along with the wisdom — sometimes the *entrenched* rules are the panic, adopted in a founding crisis, and the later majority the sober one. Defenders answer that amendment procedures let the living rework the rope, and that the real alternative to imperfect entrenchment is not perfect flexibility but vulnerability to the worst hour. The debate is unresolved; both sides share the recognition that "the majority wants it right now" cannot be the whole of legitimacy.
:::

## 4.3 Cutting power into pieces

A constitution's second great move is division. Montesquieu gave the idea its classic form: liberty is endangered whenever the power to make laws, to execute them, and to judge disputes under them is gathered in the same hands.[^montesquieu] The :::glossary term="Separation of powers"::: parcels these functions out to different bodies — legislature, executive, judiciary — and sets them watching each other.

You cannot staff a government with angels, James Madison argued, so arrange it so that "ambition must be made to counteract ambition" — each office-holder's self-interest in defending their turf becomes the energy that checks the others.[^federalist] It assumes ambitious rulers rather than hoping for virtuous ones, harnessing them against each other as an engineer uses opposing forces to hold up a bridge.

The sharpest tooth in the arrangement is :::glossary term="Judicial review":::: the power of courts to measure laws and government acts against the constitution and strike down those that fail. It turns the parchment limits of Section 4.2 into something with consequences — and it hands great power to unelected judges, which is why every constitutional democracy argues permanently, and by design, about how actively courts should use it.

:::uncertainty title="Named cases, courts, and doctrines — verify the specifics"
Textbook accounts trace judicial review to famous landmarks — an early American case establishing the practice, and in India a doctrine that even constitutional amendments cannot destroy the constitution's "basic structure" — and Germany's constitutional court is often called among the world's most influential. But case names, dates, holdings, and current doctrinal scope are specific legal detail this book should not assert from memory; they differ by jurisdiction and change over time. Citation needed — verify before publishing. Treat this chapter's account of judicial review as conceptual only.

## 4.4 Federalism, or why the answer depends on where you ask

So far power has been divided *horizontally*, among branches. :::glossary term="Federalism"::: divides it *vertically*: a constitution assigns some subjects to the centre and others to constituent units — Länder in Germany, states in India — each with real, constitutionally protected authority of its own, not merely delegated favours the centre can revoke.

This is why school holidays differ between Länder, why much of education and policing is a matter for each Land, and why your cousin's state runs its own language policy, alcohol rules, and land laws. It is not untidiness but a deliberate answer to a hard question: how can very different regions, with different histories, languages, and majorities, live under one roof without either splitting apart or being flattened into uniformity?

The case for federalism has several strands. Local decisions can fit local conditions and are made by people who must live with them. Divided power is harder to capture — a would-be authoritarian must seize not one government but many. And units can experiment: one state tries a policy, others watch, the federation learns — the famous image of states as laboratories. The case against is just as honest: federalism multiplies bureaucracy, lets entitlements change when you move house, lets regional majorities mistreat regional minorities behind the shield of "local matters," and blurs accountability — when everything is shared, every level can blame the other.

Hold Germany and India side by side, without ranking them: the two great democratic federations solve the puzzle differently. Germany's federalism is often described as *cooperative*: the Länder participate in federal law-making through a second chamber and then largely administer federal laws themselves, so the levels are woven together. India's was built for staggering diversity under a founding fear of fragmentation, and its constitution gives the centre unusually strong tools alongside genuinely powerful states — a balance its courts and politics have contested ever since. Same problem — unity without uniformity; different machines, each answering its own history.[^federal-compare]

## 4.5 A polity with layers: the European Union, sketched

Living in Germany, you are inside one more layer that fits none of the classical boxes. The European Union is not a state — its members remain sovereign states — yet it is far more than a treaty club, because they have pooled real law-making power, and some rules made at that level reach directly into daily life within member countries without each national parliament re-enacting them. Political scientists call it "a polity of layers" precisely because the old vocabulary — federation, alliance, empire, league — keeps not quite fitting.

Two conceptual points matter. First, the EU shows that this chapter's machinery is not a fixed menu: humans keep inventing institutional forms, and Chapter 1's questions of legitimacy — who authorised this power, answerable to whom? — apply to the new forms with full force; hence the permanent European debate about a "democratic deficit." Second, layering means your situation is governed from several levels at once — one more reason not to assume the office in front of you chose the rule it applies.

## 4.6 Why the machinery persists: path dependence

One puzzle remains: much of this machinery is visibly imperfect — everyone can name a rule that has outlived its reason — so why does so little change? Part of the answer is :::glossary term="Path dependence":::: once a system runs, the costs of switching mount with every year of investment in the current path, until alternatives that might have been better at the start are no longer worth the wreckage of transition.[^path]

The homely example is the keyboard: its layout was shaped by nineteenth-century mechanical constraints, yet replacing it would mean retraining hundreds of millions of hands, so it persists. Institutions are keyboards at scale: whole professions are trained in the current rules, contracts and careers are built atop them, and every actor who might coordinate a switch has adapted to the path as it is. This is why serious reform is rare, slow, and usually born of crisis — and why "why don't they just change it?" almost always underestimates the problem. Read kindly, it is also stability: no one could plan a life inside a system any passing majority could redesign from scratch.

Path dependence is not fate — federations rebalance, constitutions get amended, unions deepen or fray — but change runs along the grain of what exists. Knowing that is the difference between the frustrated customer's question, "why is this stupid?", and the mechanic's: "what old problem was this part built to solve, and what holds it in place?"

## 4.7 What to carry forward

Institutions are the rules of the game, formal and informal, that let strangers coordinate. Constitutions are precommitment — the calm self binding the panicked self — and the dead-hand objection is the price of the rope. Power is cut horizontally into branches that check each other, and vertically into layers — Land, federation, and in Europe a novel layer above both — so the same question honestly gets different answers in different places. And the machine persists because switching paths costs more each year the path is walked. None of this tells you whether any particular rule is *good*; it tells you why the rule is there, who could change it, and why they mostly don't. Which leaves the next chapter's question: who shapes what you *believe* about all of it?

:::reflect
Choose one rule that concretely shaped your week — a shop's Sunday closing, an office deadline, a rule from home your family still applies across the distance. Trace it: which layer does it come from (informal custom, city, Land or state, federal, or above), what old problem might it have been built to solve, and what holds it in place? Then answer honestly: knowing the mechanism, would you still change it if you could — and who would bear the switching costs?

[^elster]: Jon Elster, *Ulysses and the Sirens* (1979), develops precommitment as rational self-binding, with constitutional entrenchment as a central application. The Ulysses episode is from Homer's *Odyssey*, Book 12.

[^jefferson]: Thomas Jefferson, letter to James Madison, 6 September 1789: "the earth belongs in usufruct to the living." Madison's cautious reply is the classic first round of the debate.

[^montesquieu]: Montesquieu, *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748), Book XI, argues that liberty requires that legislative, executive, and judicial power not be united in the same hands.

[^federalist]: James Madison, *The Federalist* No. 51 (1788): "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition." With No. 10 and the broader *Federalist Papers* (Hamilton, Madison, Jay, 1787–88), the classic defence of the design.

[^federal-compare]: These characterisations of German and Indian federalism follow standard comparative-politics accounts, painted with a broad conceptual brush; current constitutional detail in each country should be checked against up-to-date sources.

[^path]: Path dependence entered institutional analysis largely through North (1990) and Paul Pierson's *Politics in Time* (2004); the QWERTY example was made famous by the economist Paul A. David.
