# 6. What Would Be Fair? Justice Between People and Peoples

Payday. The number lands, and before you have slept off the shift you are dividing it: rent, insurance, the exam fee, savings, the transfer home. Perhaps that last line is the largest; perhaps it is contested — a partner who thinks it too much, a parent who hoped for more, your own future asking its share. Around the payslip, the same question at scale: deductions funding strangers' pensions, wages differing sharply between the country you work in and the one you were born in, the queue for citizenship, the queue at the border.

All of these are one question in different clothes: *what would be fair?* This final chapter faces the question every earlier tool serves: how should the good and bad things of a shared world — income, opportunity, burdens, queue places — be distributed among people, and among peoples? Philosophers call this :::glossary term="Distributive justice":::, and the twentieth century produced three great rival answers worth owning. The chapter ends the book, so it will end honestly: tools handed over, no verdict imposed.

## 6.1 The machine behind a curtain: Rawls

The modern debate begins with John Rawls, who rebuilt political philosophy around a single device.[^rawls] Suppose society's rules had to be chosen behind a :::glossary term="Veil of ignorance"::: — knowing everything general about economics and institutions, but nothing particular about oneself: not one's talents, sex, caste, religion, wealth, nationality, or conception of the good life. Whatever rules such choosers would agree to, Rawls argues, are fair, because the veil deletes precisely the information that lets people bend rules toward their own case: fairness is what you choose when you do not know who you will be.

Rawls argues two principles would be chosen. First, the most extensive basic liberties for each, compatible with the same for all — with strict priority: liberty is not for sale against economic gains. Second, social and economic inequalities are acceptable only if attached to positions open to all under fair equality of opportunity, *and* if they work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged — the famous :::glossary term="Difference principle":::.

Steel-man that clause: it is not envy and not levelling-down. Rawls permits inequality — a surgeon may earn more than a cleaner — *when* the incentives producing it leave the cleaner better off than flatter alternatives would. What it forbids is defending an inequality by pointing to the *average* while the people at the bottom sink. The test of a society is taken at its floor.

:::argument title="The case for choosing justice behind the veil"
conclusion: Principles of justice are those that rational persons would choose behind a veil of ignorance, deprived of all knowledge of their own position in society.
premise: Principles of justice should not be tilted by morally arbitrary facts — no one earns their birthplace, parents, caste, sex, or native talents, yet these dominate life outcomes.
premise: An agreement is fair only if not struck under bargaining advantages drawn from those arbitrary facts.
premise: The veil of ignorance deletes exactly that information and nothing else, so agreement behind it models what a fair agreement would be.
premise: Not knowing which position you will occupy, with a whole life at stake, it is rational to secure basic liberties absolutely and judge economic arrangements by the worst position you might land in.
:::

## 6.2 "But it's mine": Nozick's reply

Robert Nozick's :::glossary term="Entitlement theory"::: is the strongest counter to the Rawlsian project; it deserves its full force.[^nozick]

:::counter title="Objection: justice is history, not a pattern" to="The case for choosing justice behind the veil"
Nozick's entitlement theory holds that a distribution is just not because it matches some pattern — equality, merit, the difference principle — but because of *how it came about*: if holdings were justly acquired and every later transfer voluntary, the result is just *whatever it looks like*; what arose through force or fraud is unjust likewise. His famous thought experiment: start from any distribution you consider just, then let a million fans each pay a small sum to watch a star athlete. The star is now vastly rich and the pattern broken, yet every step was voluntary, made with shares the pattern called rightful. Preserving any pattern therefore requires "continuous interference" with what free adults do with their own. Taxing earnings to fund a pattern is, in Nozick's deliberately shocking phrase, "on a par with forced labour" — treating people's time and talents as a common resource. One honest complication cuts the other way: his third principle — rectification of past injustice — concedes that where histories are steeped in conquest and exclusion (that is, everywhere), justice may demand substantial correction before "hands off" applies; Nozick never worked out how much. Pattern versus history remains a great unsettled argument of political philosophy.
:::

:::check reread="6.2"
question: A founder's fortune came entirely from voluntary purchases of a product she invented. A Rawlsian and a Nozickian assess whether the resulting inequality is just. What does each ask?
- Both simply ask whether the fortune is large.
- The Rawlsian asks whether the inequality works to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged under fair institutions; the Nozickian whether the fortune arose through just acquisition and voluntary transfers — if so, its size and the pattern it breaks are irrelevant.
- The Rawlsian asks whether the founder deserved her talents; the Nozickian whether the fortune matches the difference principle.
- Both agree that all inequality is unjust.
answer: 2
explanation: This is the pattern-versus-history distinction. Rawls evaluates distributions by how the arrangement treats the least advantaged; Nozick by the justice of their history, holding that free exchange will break any pattern. The same fortune can be just for Nozick and unjust for Rawls: the disagreement is foundational, not a detail.
:::

## 6.3 What can you actually do and be? Sen's third way

Amartya Sen changed the question itself. Rawls measures shares of "primary goods"; Nozick guards holdings; Sen asks what a person is *able to do and be* — people convert resources into living at wildly different rates.[^sen] The same income buys different lives for a healthy single man and a woman caring for a disabled parent; the same formal right of Chapter 2 is worth different amounts depending on language, health, and standing. You know this personally: your salary and a native colleague's may be identical while your *capabilities* — to change jobs on a restricted visa, to have a degree recognised, to argue with a landlord in a third language — are not.

Sen's :::glossary term="Capability approach"::: evaluates arrangements by the substantive freedoms they give people — to be nourished, educated, mobile, heard, to appear in public without shame — rather than by resources alone. Two consequences matter: equal resources are not yet equal justice, if conversion differs. And justice is done or undone in the small machinery of Chapters 1 and 4 — the recognition procedure, the office queue, the language course — not only in income distribution. Sen drew a methodological moral too: rather than perfecting a theory of the ideally just society, compare the feasible alternatives and remove the manifest injustices we can already identify — a philosophy for people with shifts to work.[^sen-idea]

One related idea, briefly. :::glossary term="Luck egalitarianism"::: holds that inequalities flowing from unchosen luck — birth, genes, accident — are unjust and call for compensation, while those flowing from genuine choice can stand. Its pull is obvious; its sharpest critic, Elizabeth Anderson, replied that it abandons the imprudent, humiliates the unlucky by making aid depend on proving incompetence, and mistakes justice's point — which is securing relationships of equal standing, not refereeing the cosmic lottery.[^anderson] Both the position and the critique are live.

## 6.4 Do duties stop at borders?

Now the question this book cannot politely avoid, because you live it: every framework above assumed a closed society sharing one scheme, and you stand across two. The transfer you send home outperforms, in human terms, almost anything the money could do locally. So: do duties of justice stop at borders?

The cosmopolitan answer says no. Peter Singer's famous argument opens with a child drowning in a shallow pond: you would ruin your clothes to save her, and — he argues — distance and nationality do not change the moral mathematics; if you can prevent something very bad at modest cost, you ought to, wherever the child is.[^singer] The Rawlsian veil, globalised, points the same way: you did not choose your country of birth, yet it predicts your life prospects more powerfully than almost any other fact — the *most* morally arbitrary fact of all, taken as the limit of justice.

The statist answer — held by serious philosophers, notably Rawls himself — says duties differ in kind across borders.[^rawls-lop] Strong distributive justice, on this view, arises *within* a scheme of shared coercive institutions: fellow citizens coerce one another through a common state and jointly produce a common order, so they owe one another fair shares in a way separate-scheme strangers do not. Between peoples there are real but different duties — fair dealing, human rights, assistance to burdened societies — not a global difference principle. Cosmopolitans reply that the global economy and border regimes already coerce outsiders — ask anyone refused a visa — so the "shared institutions" test may capture far more of the world than statists admit. On immigration, the same honest split: one side argues borders arbitrarily imprison people in their birth lottery; the other that even just communities may control entry, within humanitarian limits, for the sake of the institutions newcomers seek to join. This book takes no side. It notes only that you, sending money across one border after crossing another, are no onlooker here: you are the subject matter, and both sides claim you as evidence.

:::check reread="6.4"
question: What is the core of the statist position on global justice, as presented here?
- Foreigners' suffering does not matter morally.
- Strong distributive justice arises among people bound under shared coercive institutions: fellow citizens owe each other fair shares in a way they do not owe outsiders — toward whom different duties hold, such as human rights and assistance.
- Duties of justice apply identically to everyone on Earth.
- Borders exist, therefore they are just.
answer: 2
explanation: The statist view is not indifference to outsiders — it affirms duties of human rights, fair dealing, and assistance. Its claim is that the *strongest* distributive duties come from shared participation in a coercive institutional scheme, which currently exists within states, not across them. The cosmopolitan reply — that global markets and border regimes already coerce outsiders — attacks that very premise, which is why the pond and the payslip remain contested ground.
:::

## 6.5 The people who are not here yet

One frontier remains, through time rather than space. Decisions made now — what a society saves or borrows, builds or exhausts — will be inherited by people who cannot vote, bargain, or object: they do not yet exist. Contract theories strain here: future people bring nothing to the table, cannot reciprocate, and their existence and identity depend on what we choose. Rawls extended his device: behind the veil you do not know which *generation* is yours, so you would endorse a fair savings principle — each generation maintaining just institutions, passing on no poisoned inheritance.[^rawls] Derek Parfit showed the water is deeper: our policies determine not just how future people fare but *which* people exist, scrambling the usual sense of "harming" them — a puzzle that remains, fittingly for this book's last section, genuinely open.[^parfit] You need no philosophy degree to feel the weight: anyone who has sacrificed money and sleep for a sibling's education, or for a child not yet born, has already taken a position on duties to the future; the theories try to say precisely what you already practise.

## 6.6 Handing over the tools

This is the end of the book, so the opening promise holds: tools, not a verdict.

Of any arrangement — a payslip, a queue, a border, a family's expectations — you can ask this book's questions. Is this power mere force, or authority with legitimacy? Which link between paper right and lived right is broken? Whose voice counted, and what voice do the governed-but-unenfranchised keep? What old problem built these institutions, and what holds them there? Who drew this map in my head, and what did they frame? And, from this chapter: would the arrangement survive the veil; does its history of acquisition and transfer bear examination; what can the people in it actually do and be — and do my answers change at the border, should they?

Thoughtful people — including the friends you will argue with — will weigh Rawls's floor, Nozick's consent, and Sen's capabilities differently, and draw the circle of justice at different distances, honestly. That is not philosophy failing; it is the condition philosophy exists to dignify. What you may demand — of them and of yourself — is not agreement but *reasons*: positions that have faced their strongest objections, held with proportioned confidence. You live between systems and countries, between the family you came from and the one you may build, between the person expectations scripted and the one you choose to become. That position is tiring — and also, as every chapter has tried to show, one of unusual clarity: you see the machinery because you cannot take it for granted. The rules of the ground you stand on are now, at least, visible. What you build on that ground — and what you argue it *ought* to be — is yours.

:::reflect
Take a real distributive question from your life — how much to send home this month, how a cost is split in your household, a queue you are inside. Run it through all three lenses: What would be agreed behind a veil, by people not knowing which position they would occupy? Was every step in its history of agreements and transfers genuinely voluntary? What can each person involved actually do and be under it? Write down where the lenses agree, where they collide, and which you trusted in the collision. That last answer is your working theory of justice — now state its strongest objection, from this chapter, in words its defender would accept.
:::

[^rawls]: John Rawls, *A Theory of Justice* (1971): the original position and veil of ignorance, the two principles including the difference principle, and the just savings principle for justice between generations.

[^nozick]: Robert Nozick, *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974): the entitlement theory — justice in acquisition, transfer, and rectification — the Wilt Chamberlain argument against patterned principles, and the "on a par with forced labour" claim about taxing earnings.

[^sen]: Amartya Sen's capability approach is developed accessibly in *Development as Freedom* (1999); its origin as a critique of resource- and utility-based metrics is his lecture "Equality of What?" (1979).

[^sen-idea]: Amartya Sen, *The Idea of Justice* (2009), argues for comparative judgements between feasible alternatives — removing manifest injustice — rather than a "transcendental" theory of the perfectly just society.

[^anderson]: Elizabeth Anderson, "What Is the Point of Equality?", *Ethics* (1999): the classic critique of luck egalitarianism, defending "democratic equality" — equality as a relationship of equal standing, not a correction of cosmic luck.

[^singer]: Peter Singer, "Famine, Affluence, and Morality," *Philosophy & Public Affairs* (1972): the drowning-child argument, and the principle that if we can prevent something very bad without comparable sacrifice, we ought to.

[^rawls-lop]: John Rawls, *The Law of Peoples* (1999), extends his framework internationally but declines to globalise the difference principle, defending a duty of assistance to "burdened societies" and fair dealing among peoples — to the disappointment of his cosmopolitan students.

[^parfit]: Derek Parfit, *Reasons and Persons* (1984), Part Four: the "non-identity problem" and related puzzles about duties to future people.
