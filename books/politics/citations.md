# Citations

This page lists the works referred to in the footnotes of this book, with a one-line note on what each contributes. All are canonical, widely available texts. Editions, dates, translations, and page references vary; **verify bibliographic details against a reliable edition before quoting or reusing them.** Nothing here is legal advice, and the law referred to in general terms differs by country and changes over time.

## Political power, authority, and the state

- **Max Weber, *Economy and Society* (posthumous, 1922).** Source of the broad definition of power (*Macht*), the three pure types of legitimate authority (traditional, charismatic, legal-rational), and the analysis of bureaucracy as the machinery of legal-rational rule.
- **Max Weber, *Politics as a Vocation* (lecture, 1919).** Source of the definition of the state as the community that successfully claims the monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force within a territory.
- **Max Weber, *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (1905).** Origin of the "iron cage" (*stahlhartes Gehäuse*) image often applied to the bureaucratic order.
- **David Hume, *Of the First Principles of Government* (essay, 1741).** Argues that all government rests ultimately on opinion — that even rulers govern through the assent of their agents.
- **David Hume, *Of the Original Contract* (essay, 1748).** Classic critique of tacit consent, including the analogy of the man carried aboard a ship who cannot be said to consent merely by not leaping into the sea.

## Rights, law, and procedure

- **John Locke, *Two Treatises of Government* (1689).** Foundational statement of pre-political natural rights to life, liberty, and property, and of government as a trust to protect them (the social-contract tradition).
- **Thomas Hobbes, *Leviathan* (1651).** The other great early social-contract text, grounding the sovereign's authority in a covenant to escape the insecurity of a stateless condition. (Referenced in the glossary entry on the social contract.)
- **H. L. A. Hart, *The Concept of Law* (1961).** The classic modern statement of legal positivism and of the separation between what the law is and what it ought to be.
- **Jeremy Bentham, *Anarchical Fallacies* (written c. 1795, published 1843).** Source of the dismissal of natural rights as "nonsense upon stilts."
- **A. V. Dicey, *Introduction to the Study of the Law of the Constitution* (1885).** Influential modern formulation of the "rule of law."

## Democracy, participation, and membership

- **Robert A. Dahl, *On Democracy* (1998) and *Democracy and Its Critics* (1989).** Statements of the core criteria of democracy and of democracy as rule responsive to the governed.
- **Alexis de Tocqueville, *Democracy in America* (1835–1840).** Classic account of voluntary associations and the vitality of civil society.
- **Hannah Arendt, *The Origins of Totalitarianism* (1951).** Source of "the right to have rights" and the analysis of the vulnerability of the stateless, in the chapter "The Decline of the Nation-State and the End of the Rights of Man."

## Institutions, constitutions, and the design of government

- **Douglass C. North, *Institutions, Institutional Change and Economic Performance* (1990).** Source of the definition of institutions as "the rules of the game," the distinction from organisations, and the role of path dependence in institutional persistence.
- **Jon Elster, *Ulysses and the Sirens* (1979).** Develops precommitment as rational self-binding, with constitutional entrenchment as a central political application; the Ulysses episode is from Homer's *Odyssey*, Book 12.
- **Thomas Jefferson, letter to James Madison, 6 September 1789.** Source of "the earth belongs in usufruct to the living" and the classic statement of the dead-hand objection to constitutional entrenchment.
- **Montesquieu, *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748).** Book XI contains the classic argument that liberty requires the separation of legislative, executive, and judicial power.
- **Alexander Hamilton, James Madison, and John Jay, *The Federalist Papers* (1787–88).** No. 51 is the source of "ambition must be made to counteract ambition"; No. 10, on faction, completes the classic defence of the design.
- **Paul Pierson, *Politics in Time* (2004).** Develops path dependence and increasing returns as tools of political analysis.

## Media, information, and public opinion

- **Walter Lippmann, *Public Opinion* (1922).** Source of "the world outside and the pictures in our heads" and the "pseudo-environment" — the founding statement of the citizen's dependence on mediated maps.
- **Bernard C. Cohen, *The Press and Foreign Policy* (1963).** Source of the formulation that the press tells people not what to think but "what to think about."
- **Maxwell McCombs and Donald Shaw, "The Agenda-Setting Function of Mass Media," *Public Opinion Quarterly* (1972).** The Chapel Hill study that founded empirical agenda-setting research.
- **Robert Entman, "Framing: Toward Clarification of a Fractured Paradigm," *Journal of Communication* (1993).** The standard definition of framing as selection and salience.
- **Edward S. Herman and Noam Chomsky, *Manufacturing Consent: The Political Economy of the Mass Media* (1988).** Source of the propaganda model and its filters, presented in Chapter 5 alongside its standard criticisms.
- **Stephan Lewandowsky, Ullrich Ecker, Colleen Seifert, Norbert Schwarz, and John Cook, "Misinformation and Its Correction," *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* (2012).** A widely cited survey of the continued influence of corrected misinformation.
- **John Stuart Mill, *On Liberty* (1859).** Chapter 2 for the defence of liberty of thought and discussion ("dead dogma, not a living truth"); chapter 1 for the harm principle.

## Justice, within and beyond borders

- **John Rawls, *A Theory of Justice* (1971).** Source of the original position, the veil of ignorance, the two principles including the difference principle, and the just savings principle.
- **John Rawls, *The Law of Peoples* (1999).** Rawls's international extension, declining to globalise the difference principle in favour of a duty of assistance and fair dealing among peoples.
- **Robert Nozick, *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974).** Source of the entitlement theory, the Wilt Chamberlain argument, and the "on a par with forced labour" claim about taxing earnings.
- **Amartya Sen, *Development as Freedom* (1999) and *The Idea of Justice* (2009).** Accessible statements of the capability approach and of comparative (rather than transcendental) judgements of justice; the approach originates in Sen's lecture "Equality of What?" (1979).
- **Elizabeth Anderson, "What Is the Point of Equality?", *Ethics* (1999).** The classic critique of luck egalitarianism and defence of democratic equality.
- **Peter Singer, "Famine, Affluence, and Morality," *Philosophy & Public Affairs* (1972).** Source of the drowning-child argument for duties to distant strangers.
- **Derek Parfit, *Reasons and Persons* (1984).** Part Four sets out the non-identity problem and related puzzles about duties to future people.

*A note on verification.* These references are given from well-known works to support conceptual points, not to vouch for specific pages, editions, or figures. Where the text makes a specific empirical, statistical, or legal claim, it is flagged in an `:::uncertainty` block with the phrase "citation needed — verify before publishing." Confirm every quotation and detail in a reliable edition, and check current, jurisdiction-specific sources for any point of law before relying on it.
