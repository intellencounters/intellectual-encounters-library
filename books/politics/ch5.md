# 5. Who Told You That? Media, Information, and the Attentive Citizen

It is two in the morning, you are on break, and your phone lights up. In the family WhatsApp group an uncle has forwarded a message: a breathless paragraph, a blurry image, a claim that something alarming is being done — to food, to money, to people like you — and the instruction *forward this to everyone you care about*. By the time your shift ends, three relatives believe it and your mother is asking whether you are safe.

Every previous chapter has assumed something quietly enormous: that you *know things* about politics. But almost nothing political is known to you directly — you have never seen a parliament vote or a border negotiated. Nearly everything you believe about the systems around you arrived through a channel — a broadcaster, a newspaper, an algorithm, an uncle. This chapter is about those channels — what they select, how they frame, when they mislead, why corrections limp behind falsehoods, and what a tired citizen can do. It is about the supply lines of your own mind.

## 5.1 The world outside and the pictures in our heads

The classic statement of the problem is a century old. Walter Lippmann observed that the real political world is "out of reach, out of sight, out of mind" — too vast and distant to experience directly — so each of us acts not on the world but on a simplified picture assembled in our heads from what the channels deliver.[^lippmann] Democracy's official story assumes citizens who know what is going on; Lippmann's uncomfortable point is that citizens actually possess a secondhand map, drawn by someone.

This is not a reason for despair but for method. You already accept the point elsewhere — you trust instruments, not eyesight, to know your blood pressure or bank balance. In politics the question is never "map or no map?" but "how was this map drawn, by whom, and where are its distortions?"

## 5.2 What to think about, and how: agenda-setting and framing

Begin with the subtlest power the channels have, one operating before any lie is told. :::glossary term="Agenda-setting"::: is the capacity of media, simply by choosing what to cover and how prominently, to shape which issues the public regards as important. The seminal study, by Maxwell McCombs and Donald Shaw, compared the issues voters in one American town rated most important with those their media had emphasised, and found a striking correspondence.[^mccombs] Bernard Cohen's formulation is quoted alongside: the press "may not be successful much of the time in telling people what to think, but it is stunningly successful in telling its readers what to think about."[^cohen]

Two channels can report only true things and still build different worlds in their audiences' heads, by volume and placement alone: one leads every bulletin with crime, another with the cost of living, and their viewers inhabit differently shaped realities — each accurate in detail, each distorted in proportion.

The second mechanism is :::glossary term="Framing":::: presenting the same facts within a structure that makes one interpretation natural. A frame selects aspects of a situation and makes them salient — defining the problem, who caused it, and what counts as fixing it.[^entman] You have seen this done to people like you: the same person — working nights, studying, sending money home — can be framed as "labour migration straining public services" or as "the dedicated newcomer keeping essential services running." Same facts; different problem-definition; different politics. Neither frame is a lie — which is exactly why frames are more powerful than lies: there is nothing to fact-check.

:::check reread="5.2"
question: Two channels report only accurate stories, yet their regular viewers end up with very different beliefs about the country's biggest problems. Which mechanism best explains this?
- One of the channels must secretly be fabricating stories.
- Agenda-setting — by choosing what to cover and how prominently, each channel shapes which issues its audience regards as important, without any falsehood required.
- The viewers must be unintelligent.
- Corrections always fail, so the beliefs cannot be changed.
answer: 2
explanation: Agenda-setting operates through selection and emphasis, not fabrication. Truthful outlets that allocate attention differently produce audiences with differently ranked pictures of "what matters" — which is why media power begins well before any question of lying, and why "but it was all true" does not settle whether coverage distorted.
:::

## 5.3 The strongest case that the news is manufactured

If selection and framing are this powerful, who sets the selectors? The most systematic sceptical answer is the "propaganda model" of Edward Herman and Noam Chomsky; it deserves its full strength before examination.[^herman]

:::argument title="The propaganda model of commercial media"
conclusion: In market societies, mainstream news systematically filters information in ways that serve concentrated economic and political power — without any conspiracy or conscious dishonesty among journalists.
premise: News organisations are typically large businesses, owned by larger ones and funded by advertisers — structural reasons not to alienate owners, advertisers, or the affluent audiences advertisers want.
premise: News production needs cheap, regular, authoritative-seeming information, which flows most reliably from governments and large institutions — making official sources structurally overrepresented.
premise: Such selection pressures, operating over time on hiring, promotion, and editorial instinct, can produce systematically slanted output from sincere individuals — a river needs no plan to sort its stones by size.
:::

:::counter title="Objection: a filter that explains everything explains too little" to="The propaganda model of commercial media"
The strongest reply grants the premises real force — media concentration, advertising dependence, and source dependence are documented — but presses three points. First, falsifiability: critical coverage is read as the exception that disciplines the system; its absence confirms the model — and a theory that wins either way is not doing empirical work. Second, famous investigations have brought down governments and exposed corporate and military wrongdoing — awkward for a strong reading — and systems with strong public broadcasters fit the model less well than the commercial system it described. Third, taken as a slogan rather than an analysis, the model licenses dismissing *any* inconvenient report as "manufactured" — indistinguishable in practice from the propaganda mindset it warns against. The defensible residue is a habit, not a verdict: ask who owns a channel, who funds it, whose statements it treats as automatically newsworthy — and then still judge each report on its evidence.
:::

## 5.4 Why corrections limp behind falsehoods

Now to outright falsehood. :::glossary term="Misinformation"::: — false or misleading content, spread with or without intent to deceive — has structural advantages over its corrections, best understood as mechanics rather than a morality tale about the gullible. False claims are engineered, or simply selected by sharing itself, for vividness, alarm, and moral charge; corrections are dutiful, dull, and late. A repeated claim feels familiar, and familiarity is easily mistaken for truth. And a vivid story, once it organises your understanding of an event, is not deleted by a correction the way a file is — researchers speak of misinformation continuing to influence reasoning even after people accept the correction.[^cie]

:::uncertainty title="How badly do corrections fail? The empirics are contested"
Early, widely publicised studies suggested corrections could even "backfire," entrenching false beliefs more deeply. Later and larger studies have often failed to reproduce this, finding instead that corrections usually help — partially, unevenly, with effects that decay. The honest picture: corrections are worthwhile but weaker and slower than the falsehoods they chase, and the size and conditions of these effects remain actively debated. Citation needed — verify before publishing. Treat this section's mechanisms as well-supported directions, not settled quantities.
:::

:::case title="The forward in the family group"
A composite, fictional case, built from patterns many readers will recognise: a health-scare message arrives in the family WhatsApp group, forwarded by a beloved aunt. It has every structural advantage above: vivid, frightening, morally urgent; it arrives through a *trusted person*, so the sender's credibility silently stands in for the absent source's; it names no author, so there is no one to check; and the platform is private and encrypted, so no editor or public correction can attach to it as it travels. The most important feature: the aunt is not malicious; she forwarded it *because she loves you* — the message is designed so forwarding feels like care. Any response treating her as a fool will fail and deserve to. The workable response addresses the message, not her character — "Where did this start? Who first said it?" — privately, gently, with the same warmth that motivated the forward.
:::

## 5.5 Bubbles and echoes: a worry, held to the evidence

You have heard the next worry as a slogan: the algorithms have sorted us into a :::glossary term="Filter bubble"::: — a personalised feed that quietly removes what we would disagree with — and the :::glossary term="Echo chamber":::, a social world where our views meet only agreement and harden. The worry is mechanically plausible: recommendation systems optimise for engagement, agreement engages, and a citizenry that never meets opposing arguments loses the shared ground democratic disagreement needs. But this book owes you the state of the evidence, not the slogan, and the evidence is genuinely mixed.

:::uncertainty title="The contested empirics of filter bubbles and echo chambers"
A substantial body of research complicates the strong version of the story. Studies of actual browsing and platform data often find most news diets more varied than the bubble image suggests, heavy online news users encountering *more* cross-cutting material than offline audiences, and severe insulation characterising a small, politically intense minority rather than the average user — while other studies find real algorithmic narrowing on particular platforms. Disentangling what algorithms cause from what people freely choose (we sorted ourselves by neighbourhood, newspaper, and friendship well before software) is methodologically hard, findings differ across platforms and countries, and platform data is only partially open to researchers. Citation needed — verify before publishing. The defensible position is vigilance without catastrophism: the mechanism is real, its population-wide strength unsettled — and the strongest documented chambers are often ones people enthusiastically build for themselves.
:::

That last clause deserves a beat: the deepest problem may be less that machines hide disagreement than that we prefer not to meet it — in which case the remedy cannot be purely technical.

:::check reread="5.5"
question: A friend says, "It's proven: the algorithms have locked everyone into bubbles where they never see the other side." What is the most accurate response?
- Correct — the research is settled and the bubble effect is universal.
- The opposite is proven — algorithms have no effect on anyone's information diet.
- The mechanism is real and worth vigilance, but the empirics are mixed — studies often find most diets more varied than the slogan claims, severe insulation concentrated in intense minorities, and self-sorting hard to disentangle from algorithmic sorting.
- Bubbles only exist on encrypted messaging apps.
answer: 3
explanation: The honest summary is neither the slogan nor its dismissal. The narrowing mechanism is documented in some contexts, but much research finds average news diets more diverse than the bubble image suggests, with strong chambers concentrated among the most politically engaged — and people's own choices do much of the sorting. Holding a worry and its unsettled evidence together is the skill this chapter practises.
:::

## 5.6 Epistemic hygiene for the tired citizen

What can you practically do — you, with a shift, a thesis, and forty minutes of attention a day? Not "research everything"; that is advice for people without jobs. The goal is hygiene: cheap habits that catch most contamination.

Six habits are worth the effort. **Separate sender from source** — the aunt is trustworthy; the anonymous paragraph she forwarded has no track record. **Read laterally**: leave the dubious page and see what independent sources say about the claim and the outlet — faster, and it works better. **Let strong emotion be a flag, not a verdict** — content that makes you furious or triumphant is exactly the content engineered to be shared before it is checked. **Distinguish reporting from opinion from advertising** — the labels exist and are routinely ignored. **Ask what would change your mind** — a belief nothing could dislodge is not knowledge; it is furniture. And **calibrate**: you are allowed to say "probably," "I don't know," and "I was wrong" — the vocabulary of people whose maps improve. None of this makes you invulnerable — just expensive to fool, which is usually enough, because manufactured content hunts cheap targets at scale.

## 5.7 The hard question underneath: speech and harm

Behind everything above sits a dilemma this book will not pretend to settle. One tradition, classically argued by John Stuart Mill, holds that even false and offensive speech must be met with counter-speech, not suppression: the silenced view might be true, might contain part of the truth, or, if wholly false, is the whetstone keeping true beliefs sharp instead of hardening into dead dogma — and the power to suppress, once granted, will not stay in wise hands.[^mill] The rival tradition replies that this is too serene: defamation wrecks the innocent, incitement precedes violence, coordinated harassment silences its targets (a *loss* of speech, not a gain), and industrial-scale falsehood can poison the shared ground debate stands on. Democracies split the difference differently — the United States protects speech unusually broadly; Germany, with its particular history, restricts categories such as incitement to hatred more readily — and each regards the other's balance as a cautionary tale. Both traditions are defended in good faith; where the line belongs is a live question in every democracy, not a solved problem this chapter can hand you.

You now know how the map in your head gets drawn: selection, framing, structural filters, viral falsehood, and sorting — by machines and by ourselves. The final chapter turns from how you know to what you owe: what would a *fair* world look like on an honest map?

Then the harder part: recall a claim you shared that turned out wrong or unverifiable. Which of this chapter's mechanisms got you — and which single habit from Section 5.6 would most cheaply have caught it?

[^lippmann]: Walter Lippmann, *Public Opinion* (1922), opens with "The World Outside and the Pictures in Our Heads" and develops the "pseudo-environment" — the simplified mental picture on which people actually act.

[^mccombs]: Maxwell McCombs and Donald Shaw, "The Agenda-Setting Function of Mass Media," *Public Opinion Quarterly* (1972) — the Chapel Hill study that founded agenda-setting research.

[^cohen]: Bernard C. Cohen, *The Press and Foreign Policy* (1963), source of the "what to think about" formulation.

[^entman]: Robert Entman, "Framing: Toward Clarification of a Fractured Paradigm," *Journal of Communication* (1993), defines framing as selecting aspects of reality and making them salient to promote a particular problem definition, causal story, evaluation, or remedy.

[^herman]: Edward S. Herman and Noam Chomsky, *Manufacturing Consent: The Political Economy of the Mass Media* (1988), sets out the propaganda model and its filters; the counterarguments in the text are drawn from the model's standard critics.

[^cie]: The persistence of corrected misinformation is studied as the "continued influence effect"; a widely cited survey is Lewandowsky, Ecker, Seifert, Schwarz and Cook, "Misinformation and Its Correction," *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* (2012). The debate over the "backfire effect" is flagged in the uncertainty block above.

[^mill]: John Stuart Mill, *On Liberty* (1859), chapter 2, "Of the Liberty of Thought and Discussion" — a true opinion shielded from challenge becomes "dead dogma, not a living truth" — and chapter 1 for the harm principle.
