# 2. Rights, and What They Can and Cannot Do

There is a particular feeling that comes when someone tells you, "You have the right to that." Relief, usually — the sense of solid ground under your feet. And then, sometimes, a second feeling when you try to use the right and find it doesn't work the way you expected: the appointment is booked out for months, the form is in a language you half-follow, the person who is supposed to help shrugs. The right was real. So why did it not simply *work*?

This chapter is about that gap, and about the deeper question hiding beneath it: what a right actually is, where it comes from, and what it can and cannot do for you. It is a conceptual chapter, not legal advice — laws differ profoundly from country to country and change over time, and nothing here is guidance for your particular situation.

## 2.1 Two honest stories about where rights come from

Ask where your rights come from and you will get, broadly, two families of answer that have argued with each other for centuries. Both are serious. It is worth steel-manning each before choosing a reaction.

The first is the tradition of :::glossary term="Natural rights":::. On this view, you have certain rights simply because you are a human being — before and independent of any government. A law that violates them does not thereby cancel them; it is an *unjust* law, a law that fails to protect what was already yours. John Locke argued that individuals possess rights to life, liberty, and property in a "state of nature," and that people form governments precisely to protect those pre-existing rights — a bargain later thinkers called the :::glossary term="Social contract":::.[^locke] This is the intuition behind the very idea of *human* rights: that a person stripped of citizenship, papers, and property still has *something* no official can rightfully take. When you feel that a rule is wrong even though it is legal, you are thinking like a natural-rights theorist.

:::argument title="The natural-rights case"
conclusion: Some rights are not created by governments and cannot be legitimately abolished by them; they belong to persons as such.
premise: We judge certain laws — those permitting slavery, say, or the arbitrary killing of the innocent — to be unjust even when they are validly enacted.
premise: To call a validly enacted law "unjust" is to appeal to a standard above the law itself, against which the law can be measured.
premise: The most economical explanation of that standard is that persons have moral claims — rights — that exist prior to, and constrain, what any government may rightfully do.
:::

The second family is :::glossary term="Legal positivism":::. On this view, a right is not a mysterious moral possession; it is whatever a legal system actually recognises and enforces. The law's existence is one thing, its merit another: a rule can be law even if it is unjust, and a rule you would love to have is not a right until some real institution backs it.[^hart] Positivists are not moral cynics — many care deeply about justice — but they insist on clarity. Calling something a "right" that no institution will honour, they argue, breeds confusion and false hope; better to say plainly "this ought to be a right, and is not yet," and then fight to make it one.

:::counter title="Objection: 'natural' rights may be wishes wearing a crown" to="The natural-rights case"
The strongest positivist reply is that the natural-rights argument mistakes a strong feeling for a discovered fact. That we *judge* certain laws unjust shows only that we hold moral views, not that rights exist "out there" the way rivers do. And the history of "self-evident" natural rights is uncomfortable: for centuries lists of them quietly excluded women, the enslaved, the colonised, and the propertyless — which suggests the lists tracked the intuitions of the powerful rather than a fixed moral order. The positivist does not deny that we should protect people; she denies that dressing our commitments as timeless "nature" makes them truer, and warns that it can just as easily be used to freeze one group's preferences into eternal law. Jeremy Bentham put the challenge at its bluntest, calling natural rights "nonsense upon stilts."[^bentham]

The natural-rights theorist has replies — that without some standard above the law, "unjust law" becomes literally meaningless, and reformers lose their footing. The dispute is genuinely unresolved, and a thoughtful person can hold either view.
:::

:::check reread="2.1"
question: A colleague says, "There's no such thing as a right until a court will actually enforce it — everything else is just a wish." Which position is she expressing?
- Natural rights — rights exist prior to government.
- Legal positivism — a right is what a legal system actually recognises and enforces.
- The social contract — government exists to protect pre-existing rights.
- The rule of law — all are equal before the law.
answer: 2
explanation: Tying the existence of a right to actual institutional recognition and enforcement, and separating that sharply from what *ought* to be, is the core of legal positivism. The natural-rights view would say the wish can already name a real right that the law is failing to honour.
:::

## 2.2 The rule of law

Whatever rights are, they are worth little unless power itself is bound. The idea that does the binding has a name: the :::glossary term="Rule of law":::. It means, at minimum, that everyone — including the government, the police, the wealthy, and the well-connected — is subject to the same publicly known laws, applied by independent courts, rather than to the arbitrary will of whoever holds power that day.[^dicey]

Contrast two worlds. In the first, an official can decide your case however he likes today and differently tomorrow, and there is no one above him to appeal to. In the second, the official is himself bound: there are rules he must follow, they were knowable in advance, and if he breaks them you can go to a court that does not answer to him. The second world is not paradise — the rules can be bad, the courts slow — but it is a categorically different place to be a small person in. The rule of law is what makes the impersonal bureaucracy of Chapter 1 a protection and not just a maze: because the clerk is *also* ruled by rules, those same rules can be held up in your defence.

:::definition term="Rule of law"
The principle that governmental power must be exercised through general, publicly known, prospective laws applied equally to all — including the powerful and the government itself — and enforced by independent courts, rather than through the arbitrary or secret will of officials. The rule of law constrains *how* power may be used; it does not guarantee that the laws themselves are just.
:::

That last sentence matters and cuts against comfort. The rule of law is a constraint on *arbitrariness*, not a guarantee of *justice*. A regime can have meticulous legal procedure and deeply unjust laws — apartheid South Africa had courts and statutes. So the rule of law is necessary but not sufficient. It answers "is power being exercised regularly and accountably?" — not "are the rules themselves good?" Keeping those two questions apart protects you from two opposite mistakes: assuming that because something is legal it is just, and assuming that because a law is unjust the whole apparatus of legality is worthless.

## 2.3 Rights on paper, rights in practice

Here is the gap you felt at the start of the chapter. A right written in a constitution or a directive is a *promise*. Whether it reaches you depends on a long chain: whether you know it exists, whether you can find and afford the procedure to claim it, whether the office is staffed, whether the official applies it correctly, whether you can appeal if they don't, and whether you can wait out the time all of this takes. Break any link and the paper right does not become a lived one.

This is not usually villainy. It is friction — distance, cost, language, delay, complexity — and friction falls hardest on exactly the people with the least time, money, and fluency to push through it. A right that in theory belongs to everyone can in practice belong mostly to those who can afford a lawyer or take a weekday off. The formal equality is real; the effective inequality is also real; both things are true at once.

:::uncertainty title="How wide is the gap, in numbers?"
It is tempting to quantify this — to say what fraction of people entitled to a given benefit or protection never actually receive it because of complexity, cost, or lack of information. Researchers study exactly this under headings like "non-take-up of social rights" and "access to justice," and the figures are often striking. But the specific numbers vary enormously by country, by right, by year, and by how the study is designed, and this book cannot responsibly assert a particular percentage. Citation needed — verify before publishing. Treat the claim here as a well-documented *pattern* — that formal rights routinely under-deliver in practice — not as a precise measured quantity. And remember that the underlying laws and procedures differ by country and change over time.
:::

## 2.4 Why procedure matters more than it looks

When you are tired and a right is not working, procedure feels like the enemy — more forms, more waiting, more boxes. It is worth understanding why the procedure is there, because it is often the very thing standing between you and someone's arbitrary power.

The core idea is :::glossary term="Due process":::: the requirement that before the state does something serious to you — denies your application, ends your status, penalises you — it must follow a fair, knowable procedure. You must generally be told what the case is, given a chance to respond, and have the decision made by someone impartial, with reasons, and usually with a route of appeal.[^dueprocess] Notice what procedure does: it converts power from a personal act into an accountable one. A decision that must be justified in writing, by rules, and can be reviewed is a decision that cannot easily be made out of prejudice, mood, or favour — because all of those are hard to write down as reasons.

So the same impersonal machinery that exhausts you is also what lets you say: *show me the rule, give me your reasons, tell me how to appeal.* Procedure is slow because arbitrariness is fast. The friction that fails you when you are trying to claim a right is the friction that protects you when someone is trying to take one away. This is not a consolation for the waiting; it is a reason to learn the procedure rather than despise it.

:::check reread="2.4"
question: What is the best account of *why* due process — being told the case, allowed to respond, and given reasons by an impartial decider — protects people?
- It speeds up decisions so officials can help more people.
- It guarantees that the outcome will be the one you want.
- It converts power from a private, unaccountable act into one that must be justified by rules and can be reviewed, which makes prejudice and favour harder to act on.
- It proves that the underlying laws are just.
answer: 3
explanation: Due process does not guarantee your preferred outcome, speed, or the justice of the underlying law. Its work is to make the *exercise* of power accountable — reasoned, rule-bound, and reviewable — which is precisely what arbitrary or biased decision-making cannot survive.
:::

## 2.5 What rights can and cannot do

So, honestly: what can a right do for you? A right can give you standing — a place to stand and say "this is owed to me, not begged for." It can bind an official to a rule. It can give you a procedure and an appeal. It can, over time and through many people using it, reshape what a society treats as normal.

What a right cannot do is enforce itself. It cannot find you, inform you, fill in your forms, shorten the queue, or pay for the time and help you need to claim it. It cannot make an unjust law just, and it cannot guarantee that the promise on paper arrives in your life. Between the right and the reality sits the whole machinery of Chapter 1 — power, authority, procedure, delay.

Understanding this is not meant to make you cynical. It is meant to make you *effective* and unsurprised: to expect the gap, to treat procedure as a tool rather than an insult, and to know that "I have a right to this" is the beginning of a claim, not the end of one. Which leads to the last question of the book — what happens to your voice and your standing when you are governed by a system in which you are not yet a full member.

:::reflect
Recall a time a right or entitlement of yours existed "on paper" but was hard to actually use. Looking back, which link in the chain broke — knowing it existed, affording the procedure, the office itself, the official's decision, the wait, or something else? Now steel-man the procedure that frustrated you: what arbitrary harm might that same procedure prevent for someone else? Write both down, and notice whether naming the specific broken link changes what you would do next time.
:::

[^locke]: John Locke, *Two Treatises of Government* (1689), especially the Second Treatise, argues for pre-political natural rights to life, liberty, and property, and for government as a trust established to protect them.

[^hart]: H. L. A. Hart, *The Concept of Law* (1961), is the classic modern statement of legal positivism, including the insistence on separating the question of what the law *is* from what it *ought to be*. Hart himself allowed for a "minimum content" of natural law.

[^bentham]: Jeremy Bentham dismissed the doctrine of imprescriptible natural rights as "nonsense upon stilts" in *Anarchical Fallacies* (written c. 1795, published 1843), his critique of the French Declaration of the Rights of Man.

[^dicey]: The phrase "rule of law" was given its influential modern formulation by A. V. Dicey, *Introduction to the Study of the Law of the Constitution* (1885), though the underlying idea is far older, reaching back through Aristotle to the ideal of "a government of laws, not of men."

[^dueprocess]: The idea of due process — notice, a hearing, an impartial decision-maker — has deep roots in the common-law tradition, often traced rhetorically to Magna Carta (1215). Its specific legal form differs greatly across jurisdictions; the principle, not any particular country's rules, is what is described here.
