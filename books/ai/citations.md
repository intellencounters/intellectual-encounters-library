# Citations and further reading

These are works the book draws on or points readers toward. They are cited cautiously: only titles the editorial project is confident exist, named at the level of the work rather than specific page numbers, results, or statistics. Any precise figure, benchmark, or dated capability claim in this fast-moving field has been kept out of the prose and placed in an `:::uncertainty` block instead. **Verify all bibliographic details — editions, years, and the exact content of any specific finding — against the primary source before reuse or publication.**

## On how machine learning works (Chapter 1)

- **Stuart Russell and Peter Norvig, _Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach_ (Pearson, multiple editions).** The standard comprehensive textbook; the reference behind the book's treatment of models, features, labels, supervised learning, generalization, and overfitting.
- **Melanie Mitchell, _Artificial Intelligence: A Guide for Thinking Humans_ (Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2019).** A clear, sceptical narrative introduction for non-specialists; useful counterweight to hype.

## On the limits of these systems (Chapter 2)

- **Judea Pearl and Dana Mackenzie, _The Book of Why: The New Science of Cause and Effect_ (Basic Books, 2018);** and **Judea Pearl, _Causality_ (Cambridge University Press, 2nd ed., 2009).** The framework distinguishing correlation from causation and explaining confounding.
- **Cathy O'Neil, _Weapons of Math Destruction_ (Crown, 2016).** How opaque automated scoring systems can encode and scale harm.
- **Safiya Umoja Noble, _Algorithms of Oppression_ (NYU Press, 2018);** and **Ruha Benjamin, _Race After Technology_ (Polity, 2019).** How technical systems reproduce existing social inequities — the substance behind "bias in, bias out."
- **John Searle, "Minds, Brains, and Programs," _Behavioral and Brain Sciences_ 3 (1980).** The Chinese Room argument, framing competence versus comprehension.
- **Alan Turing, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," _Mind_ 59 (1950).** The founding behaviour-first statement of the opposing tradition.

## On the human questions (Chapter 3)

- **David H. Autor, "Why Are There Still So Many Jobs? The History and Future of Workplace Automation," _Journal of Economic Perspectives_ 29:3 (2015).** A careful, non-alarmist account of automation reshaping rather than erasing work.
- **Erik Brynjolfsson and Andrew McAfee, _The Second Machine Age_ (W. W. Norton, 2014).** A broad survey of the economic stakes of digital automation.
- **Hannah Arendt, _The Human Condition_ (University of Chicago Press, 1958).** The distinctions among labour, work, and action drawn on in the discussion of dignity.
- **Amartya Sen, _Development as Freedom_ (Knopf, 1999).** Work and technology seen through human capability and freedom.
- **The _Bhagavad Gita_, esp. chapter 2.** The classical teaching on acting without attachment to the fruits of action; cited as a widely known text, not as endorsement of a single interpretation.
- **Viktor E. Frankl, _Man's Search for Meaning_ (first published in German, 1946).** Meaning as a stance toward circumstance rather than a matter of output.

## On building real systems (Chapter 4)

- **D. Sculley et al., "Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems," _Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems_ (NeurIPS, 2015).** The well-known practitioner account of production machine learning as a small learning core inside a large apparatus of data handling, configuration, and monitoring. Cited cautiously, for its qualitative argument rather than any specific figure.
- **Goodhart's Law (attributed to Charles Goodhart).** The widely known aphorism that a measure which becomes a target ceases to be a good measure; cited as a named idea with its standard attribution, not as a quotation from a specific text.

## On generative AI (Chapter 5)

- **Ashish Vaswani et al., "Attention Is All You Need," _Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems_ (NeurIPS, 2017).** The paper introducing the transformer architecture and its attention mechanism; the chapter's plain-language gloss should be checked against this primary source.
- **Claude Shannon, "A Mathematical Theory of Communication," _Bell System Technical Journal_ (1948).** The founding work treating language as a predictable statistical sequence; cited as intellectual lineage for next-token prediction, not as a description of modern systems.
- **Emily M. Bender and Alexander Koller, "Climbing towards NLU: On Meaning, Form, and Understanding in the Age of Data," _Proceedings of ACL_ (2020).** A prominent modern statement of the argument that training on linguistic form alone cannot yield meaning; source of the "octopus" thought experiment.
- **John Searle, "Minds, Brains, and Programs" (1980)** and **Alan Turing, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950)** — listed under Chapter 2 above; re-applied in Chapter 5 to systems trained on text alone.

## On careers (Chapter 6)

- **Cal Newport, _So Good They Can't Ignore You_ (Business Plus, 2012).** Source of the "career capital" framing — rare and valuable skills as the substance beneath satisfying work; the application to AI-era skill choices is the chapter's own.
- **David H. Autor, "Why Are There Still So Many Jobs?" (2015)** — listed under Chapter 3 above; its task-versus-job framing underlies Chapter 6's labour-market reasoning.

## A note on verification

This field changes month to month. Where a claim would have required a specific number — a parameter count, a dataset size, a benchmark score, an audit's exact disparity figure, a share of jobs at risk — the book deliberately declines to assert it and flags it for verification. Treat any such figure you encounter, here or elsewhere, as provisional until checked against the primary source, with attention to the exact system, version, and date it refers to.
