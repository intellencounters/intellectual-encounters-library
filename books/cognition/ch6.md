# 6. Thinking Together: When Groups Are Smarter, and When They're Dumber

This book has so far treated cognition as something that happens inside one skull — one budget of attention, one library of chunks, one tired brain deciding. But look at how you actually live. One friend in the circle knows the Anmeldung process cold; another remembers every deadline; a couple splits the household's knowledge so thoroughly that neither could run the flat alone; and this book itself is being read by a group that meets to argue about it. Much of your effective intelligence has never been inside your head. It is distributed across people who remember, notice, and check different things. The final chapter asks the question that frame makes urgent: under what conditions is a group of minds smarter than its best member — and under what conditions is it, measurably, dumber than its average one?

## 6.1 Memory in couples and teams

Start with the gentlest case. Daniel Wegner observed that people in close relationships develop a shared system he called :::glossary term="Transactive memory":::: each partner becomes the specialist for certain domains, each knows *who knows what*, and the pair functions as a memory larger and more organised than either brain alone.[^wegner] You have watched this: one partner stores the family birthdays and the landlord's quirks, the other the finances and the visa dates, and each retrieves from the other as naturally as from themselves — "ask Priya, she'll know" is a memory operation, not a confession of ignorance.

:::definition term="Transactive memory"
A shared system for encoding, storing, and retrieving knowledge that develops in couples, families, and teams, described by Daniel Wegner. Members specialise in different domains and maintain a directory of who knows what, so the group stores more, with less duplication, than the same individuals could alone. The system's strength is also its fragility: it must be built through shared experience, and it is partly why losing a long-term partner or a veteran colleague is a cognitive amputation — part of one's memory now lives in no one.
:::

Two implications reach beyond couples. Teams that train *together* tend to outperform identical individuals trained separately, because they build the directory — who checks what, who to believe about which topic — and studies of transactive memory in work groups link that directory to performance. And the modern extension is personal: search engines and phones now hold the "what," leaving us to store the "where," a shift Wegner himself began theorising before his death. Your circle's WhatsApp group, where someone always knows which office, which form, which Anlage — that is transactive memory with a user interface.

## 6.2 When the group closes ranks

Now the dark cases, because groups do not merely pool virtues; they pool and amplify failures too. The most famous diagnosis is Irving Janis's :::glossary term="Groupthink":::: cohesive groups under pressure, especially with a directive leader, come to prize unanimity over accuracy — dissent feels like disloyalty, doubters censor themselves, the silence is read as agreement, and the group sails confidently into a decision no member would have made alone.[^janis] Janis built the theory on post-mortems of American policy fiascos, the Bay of Pigs invasion above all, and the pattern is recognisable at every scale — including a friends' circle where everyone privately doubts the plan and nobody wants to be the one who ruins the mood, or a family call where a marriage decision hardens because each relative assumes the others' enthusiasm is real.

:::uncertainty title="How solid is groupthink?"
Groupthink is textbook material, but its evidential base is weaker than its fame. Janis worked from retrospective case studies of hand-picked fiascos — a method that cannot rule out hindsight bias — and decades of laboratory attempts to reproduce the full syndrome have yielded mixed, partial results; reviewers disagree about whether the model survives as stated or only as a loose family of documented mechanisms (self-censorship, conformity pressure, premature consensus). Do not treat "groupthink" as an established law or quote any figure about its prevalence. Citation needed — verify before publishing. The component mechanisms are individually well supported; the packaged syndrome is the contested part.
:::

The adjacent, better-replicated finding is *group polarisation*: groups of like-minded people tend to end discussion holding a more extreme version of the view they started with. Deliberation, which should moderate, often radicalises — each member hears new arguments for the shared side and calibrates toward the group's perceived centre. Any circle of friends who agree about most things — most circles — is running this process on itself, which is a reason to treasure the member who reliably disagrees.

## 6.3 The crowd, wise and otherwise

Against the pathology stands a genuinely startling result. Francis Galton, at a 1906 country fair, collected hundreds of guesses of an ox's weight and found the crowd's aggregate astonishingly close to the truth — the observation James Surowiecki built into the popular idea of the :::glossary term="Wisdom of crowds":::.[^surowiecki] The mechanism is not mystical: if guesses scatter around the truth with independent errors, aggregation cancels the errors and distils the signal. A crowd can beat its own experts — *when the conditions hold*.

The conditions are everything, and Surowiecki himself was clearer about them than his imitators. Errors cancel only if they are genuinely independent and the crowd genuinely diverse; the aggregation must actually combine private judgements rather than public posturing. Break independence — let each guesser see the running average, let a confident voice speak first, let everyone read the same three sources — and errors *correlate*: the crowd becomes an echo chamber with the confidence of a consensus. Note the elegant, uncomfortable implication: discussion, the thing thinking groups love most, is precisely what destroys the independence that makes crowds wise. Talking converts many independent estimates into one social one.

:::check reread="6.3"
question: To estimate a fair market salary, you ask your circle. In version A, each friend messages you a number privately before any discussion. In version B, the group discusses openly, the most senior friend answering first, until a consensus number emerges. Which is more likely to be accurate, and why?
- B — discussion always improves estimates by adding information.
- A — private, simultaneous answers keep errors independent, so aggregation can cancel them; in B the first confident voice anchors the rest and errors correlate.
- B — senior people are always right, so consensus around them is ideal.
- Neither differs; the same people produce the same aggregate regardless of procedure.
answer: 2
explanation: The wisdom of crowds is a property of aggregating independent judgements, not of groups as such. Version B breaks independence twice — through anchoring on the first number (Chapter 3) and conformity toward the emerging consensus — so the "group" answer is largely one person's answer, socially laundered. Collect first, discuss after: the order is the entire trick.
:::

## 6.4 The brainstorming surprise

Nowhere is the gap between how groups feel and how they perform better documented than in brainstorming. Alex Osborn's mid-century promise — that a group calling out ideas without criticism would far outproduce individuals — became office scripture. The experimental literature found close to the reverse.

:::argument title="The case against classic group brainstorming"
conclusion: For generating many good ideas, individuals working separately and then pooling results ("nominal groups") tend to outperform the same people brainstorming aloud together.
premise: In spoken groups only one person can talk at a time, so ideas queue in working memory, decay, or are self-censored while waiting — the mechanism Michael Diehl and Wolfgang Stroebe isolated as production blocking.
premise: Evaluation apprehension survives the "no criticism" rule, because members still anticipate silent judgement from people who matter to them.
premise: Responsibility diffuses in groups, so average individual effort drops.
premise: Experimental comparisons across decades repeatedly found nominal groups producing more ideas, and more good ideas, than interacting groups of the same size.
:::

:::counter title="What group ideation still does" to="The case against classic group brainstorming"
The honest objection comes in two parts. First, scope: the experiments mostly measure *idea counts* in short laboratory sessions on assigned problems — a narrow window on what real collaboration is for. Groups also select ideas, combine half-thoughts into wholes, build commitment to execute, and share knowledge across specialities, none of which the counting paradigm captures; and some structured formats (writing ideas silently before speaking, electronic pooling) remove production blocking while keeping the group's combinatorial benefits. Second, the field itself is contested: some researchers argue cognitive stimulation from others' ideas can be genuinely productive under the right conditions, and that the nominal-group superiority, while a robust laboratory result, is not the last word on collaborative creativity in the wild. The defensible summary is not "never think together" but "generate apart, combine together" — and even that is a rule of thumb, not a law.
:::

The transactive-memory finding and the brainstorming finding are not in tension; they triangulate the same principle. Groups are powerful as *structures* — for storing, checking, selecting, and combining — and weak as *simultaneous generators*, because a conversation is a single channel through which many minds must squeeze one at a time. Design accordingly.

## 6.5 Is there a group IQ?

A research programme associated with Anita Woolley and colleagues has pushed the question to its natural limit: is there a general :::glossary term="Collective intelligence"::: — a stable "c factor" that predicts a given group's performance across many different tasks, as IQ does for individuals? Early studies reported yes, and reported something more provocative: the group's collective intelligence correlated only weakly with its members' average intelligence, and more strongly with social factors — how evenly conversational turns were distributed, and members' sensitivity to one another's mental states.[^woolley]

:::uncertainty title="Collective intelligence, cautiously"
These findings are widely cited and would, if firm, be the tidiest possible ending for this chapter — which is exactly why caution is required. The literature is young; replications have supported some elements (a general factor across tasks) more strongly than others (the specific social predictors, including early claims about the proportion of women in groups, which later and larger work has qualified); and effect sizes vary across studies. None of the specific correlations may be stated as established fact. Citation needed — verify before publishing. The conservative reading: group performance is a property of the group and its process, not a simple sum of member ability — a directional claim that everything else in this chapter independently supports.
:::

## 6.6 A circle that actually thinks

This book will not end as a listicle, but the findings above do assemble into a discipline, and your reading circle is the obvious laboratory. Everything follows from the mechanisms, so hold the mechanisms rather than the rules. Independence before influence: whatever the group must judge — what a chapter's weakest argument is, what someone's salary ask should be — have members commit privately in writing first, because Section 6.3 showed that discussion consumes the independence that makes aggregation work, and Chapter 3's anchoring explains who will otherwise win. Structure against self-censorship: Janis's own prescriptions — a leader who states no preference and speaks last, an assigned devil's advocate whose dissent is a duty rather than a betrayal, an explicit second-chance meeting for doubts — target the silence that lets false consensus form; they are hygiene against a documented failure mode, whatever the fate of the packaged syndrome. Generate apart, evaluate together, because that split respects both halves of Section 6.4. And build the directory deliberately: a circle that knows who has actually read the contract, who understands the tax form, and who merely sounds confident is running transactive memory well — and the difference between *knowing who knows* and *deferring to who talks* is much of the difference between a wise crowd and a herd.

:::check reread="6.2"
question: In a study circle, the most fluent member speaks first and at length; others, privately unconvinced, say nothing, and each reads the silence as agreement. The plan adopted is one most members doubted. Which combination of this chapter's mechanisms best describes what happened?
- Transactive memory: the group correctly retrieved the answer from its specialist.
- Wisdom of crowds: many independent judgements were aggregated and errors cancelled.
- Self-censorship under conformity pressure plus an illusion of unanimity — the core groupthink mechanisms — amplified by anchoring on the first confident voice.
- Production blocking: members physically could not speak because the channel was busy.
answer: 3
explanation: Nothing was aggregated and no directory was consulted; private doubts were suppressed, suppressed doubt was mistaken for agreement, and the first fluent voice set the anchor. These component mechanisms are well supported even where the packaged "groupthink" syndrome is contested — and every one of them is addressable by procedure: private commitments first, the confident speaking last, dissent assigned as a role.
:::

:::reflect
Think of the most recent real decision your circle or family made together — a trip, a shared expense, advice pressed on one member about work, marriage, or moving. Reconstruct honestly: were the private views ever actually collected, or did the first confident voice set an anchor the rest adjusted around? Who self-censored, and what did their silence get counted as? Now steel-man the group against your critique: name one thing the group process genuinely added that no member could have produced alone. Finally, commit to one structural change — not a resolution to "speak up more," but a procedure — that you will propose before the next shared decision, and predict in writing what it will change.
:::

## 6.7 An honest close, for the chapter and the book

Six chapters ago this book began with a single tired reader at 23:40, and every chapter since has, in one way or another, delivered the same double message: the individual mind is a magnificent instrument with hard, non-negotiable limits — a working memory of a few slots, a memory that fades on schedule, a judgement that degrades with fatigue and never notices its own biases, an expertise that will not leave the domain where it was built. The honest response to those limits was never going to be a better productivity system. It is other minds. A partner who stores what you drop, a colleague whose feedback breaks your plateau, a friend who owns the language you are still building, a circle that catches the bias you cannot see from the inside — these are not supports for cognition; they are cognition, continued by other means. But this chapter has shown that the continuation is not automatic: groups amplify whatever they are structured to amplify, error as readily as insight, and the same conversation that pools knowledge can launder one person's confidence into everyone's belief. So the book ends where it began, with budgeting rather than magic. Spend attention deliberately; build memory through honest effort; guard sleep; distrust tired confidence; practise at the edge; and build around yourself a group disciplined enough to disagree — then extend to every idea in these pages, this last one included, the standing invitation to be tested against your own results. That invitation, taken seriously and taken together, is the whole method.
