# Glossary

## Working memory

The brain system that holds a small amount of information in an active, usable state while you think — reading, calculating, reasoning, following an instruction. It is limited in both capacity (only a handful of items at once) and duration (contents fade within seconds unless refreshed). It is a temporary workbench, not long-term storage, and its narrowness is the ceiling on how much you can consciously process at any moment.

## Attention

The limited capacity to select and sustain conscious focus on some information while ignoring the rest. Attention behaves like a budget: it is small, quickly spent, and reduced further by fatigue. Because it cannot truly be divided between two demanding tasks, apparent "multitasking" is really rapid switching, each switch carrying a cost.

## Cognitive load

The total demand a task places on working memory at a given moment. Cognitive load theory (John Sweller) distinguishes *intrinsic* load — the inherent difficulty of the material — from *extraneous* load added by how the material is presented or the conditions you study in. Learning stalls when total load exceeds working-memory capacity; cutting extraneous load frees capacity for the part that matters.

## Task-switching

Moving attention from one task to another. Because each task keeps its own contents in working memory, switching forces the mind to clear one set and reload another, incurring a "switch cost" in time and accuracy. This is why interleaving unfamiliar, effortful tasks — the everyday sense of "multitasking" — is slower and more error-prone than doing them one at a time, even though it feels productive.

## Encoding

The process of forming a memory trace when you first take information in. Encoding is deeper and more durable when you engage with the *meaning* of material — relating it to prior knowledge, asking why it is true — than when you merely re-expose yourself to its surface. Depth of processing at encoding matters more than sheer repetition.

## Retrieval practice

The act of recalling information from memory — a closed-book self-test, a flashcard, an explanation given aloud without notes. Retrieval is not only a way of measuring learning; the effort of reconstructing an answer is itself one of the strongest ways of strengthening the memory (the "testing effect"). Retrieval that is somewhat effortful helps more than retrieval that is easy.

## Spaced repetition

Distributing study of the same material across multiple sessions over time rather than massing it into one block ("cramming"). Spacing feels slower and harder in the moment but produces markedly more durable memory, because reviews that occur as you begin to forget require effortful — and therefore strengthening — retrieval.

## Interleaving

Mixing different types of problem or topic within a study session instead of completing all of one kind before moving to the next ("blocked" practice). Interleaving is harder and more error-prone in the moment because it forces you to decide which method each problem needs, but it improves the ability to select and apply knowledge in new situations — closer to what real exams and real work demand.

## Desirable difficulty

A difficulty introduced into learning (Robert Bjork) that lowers performance *in the moment* yet improves long-term retention and transfer. Retrieval practice, spacing, and interleaving are the main examples. The term warns against judging a study method by how smoothly it goes today rather than by what you can still do weeks later.

## Memory consolidation

The process by which a freshly encoded, unstable memory is strengthened, stabilised, and integrated with existing knowledge over hours and days, becoming more resistant to interference. A substantial part of consolidation occurs during sleep, which is why sleep is part of the machinery of learning rather than merely rest between study sessions.

## Dual-process theory

A family of accounts describing thinking as involving two contrasting modes: a fast, automatic, effortless mode (popularised by Daniel Kahneman as "System 1") and a slow, deliberate, effortful mode ("System 2"). The two "systems" are a useful shorthand for clusters of processes, not two literal structures in the brain. Fatigue pushes reliance toward the fast mode, because the deliberate mode draws on scarce attention.

## Heuristic

A mental shortcut — a quick, intuitive rule that yields a usable answer with little effort. Heuristics are not defects; they make fast, low-cost judgement possible and are right often enough to be worth having. Their weakness is that they fail in systematic, repeatable ways on certain problems, and those predictable failures are cognitive biases.

## Cognitive bias

A systematic, predictable error of judgement that arises when a normally useful heuristic misfires. Examples include confirmation bias (favouring evidence that fits what you already suspect), anchoring (over-weighting the first figure mentioned), and availability (judging vivid, easily recalled events as more likely than they are). Biases are the price of fast thinking, and they bite harder when fatigue discourages deliberate checking.

## Decision fatigue

The popular idea that making choices draws on a limited reserve that depletes through the day, degrading self-control and judgement by evening. It is usually grounded in the contested theory of "ego depletion"; large replication efforts have questioned the specific mechanism. The defensible core is modest: tired, hungry people tend to decide worse, so important decisions are best scheduled for rested moments.

## Chunking

The process by which separate items of information become bound into a single familiar unit that working memory can hold as one thing. To a novice a chess position is thirty pieces and a SQL query is a dozen tokens; to the practised eye each is a handful of recognisable shapes. Expertise consists substantially in acquiring a very large repertoire of such chunks within one domain — which is also why expert perception does not travel to domains where the chunks were never built.

## Deliberate practice

Practice designed specifically to improve performance, as characterised by Anders Ericsson: targeted at a component just beyond current ability, fully concentrated, guided by immediate and informative feedback, and repeated with refinement after errors. Distinct from work (performing the skill) and play (enjoying it). How much of the difference between performers it explains is contested — meta-analytic work argues for a smaller share than the strong claim — but its status as the main lever an individual controls is common ground.

## Mental model

An internal representation of how some part of the world works — how a database resolves a query, why a plane stays up, what the Ausländerbehörde needs and why — which allows prediction, explanation, and troubleshooting rather than mere recall. Experts differ from novices not only in knowing more facts but in organising them around deeper models; making a model explicit in words is one of the few ways its lessons transfer to new domains.

## Transfer of learning

The application of skill or knowledge learned in one context to a different one. Near transfer, between closely similar tasks, occurs routinely; far transfer, between genuinely different domains, has proven rare and hard to produce on demand — the central reason commercial "brain training" disappointed. Because expertise lives in domain-specific chunks and models, the practical rule is to practise on tasks as close as possible to the performance you actually need.

## Cross-linguistic interference

The intrusion of one language's words, structures, sounds, or conventions into another — a German word order surfacing in an English email, an English noun wearing a hopeful German article. Interference reflects how multilingual memory is organised: languages are simultaneously active and share machinery rather than occupying sealed compartments. It is a normal operating cost of multilingualism, heaviest under fatigue, not a sign of deficient knowledge of either language.

## Tip-of-the-tongue state

The experience of being unable to retrieve a word one certainly knows, often while sensing its first letter, syllable count, or rhythm. Research finds such states somewhat more frequent in bilinguals — plausibly because each of their words is used less often than a monolingual's single word for the same meaning, and because translation-equivalents compete at the moment of retrieval. A retrieval failure, not a storage failure: the word is there, and usually surfaces later unbidden.

## Executive function

An umbrella term for the general-purpose control processes that direct thought and action: holding goals in mind, inhibiting impulses and irrelevant information, and switching flexibly between tasks. Closely tied to working memory and the effortful "System 2" mode of thinking. Whether lifelong bilingualism measurably strengthens executive function beyond language itself is the contested heart of the "bilingual advantage" debate.

## Foreign-language effect

The finding, associated with Boaz Keysar and colleagues, that people presented with decision problems in a foreign language show reduced framing biases and more willingness to accept rational trade-offs than with the same problems in their native tongue — plausibly because a later-learned language carries less emotional charge, blunting the fast system's reactions and giving deliberation more room. A young, contested literature; treat it as a promising hypothesis and a cheap personal experiment, not a settled result.

## Comprehensible input

Language material a learner can mostly understand, pitched slightly beyond current ability — the driver of acquisition in Stephen Krashen's influential and contested input hypothesis. The strong theoretical claim (that input is essentially sufficient) has been criticised as unfalsifiable and incomplete; the durable practical core is that large volumes of nearly understandable listening and reading do work that worksheets cannot, while material far above one's level mostly passes by as noise.

## Transactive memory

A shared system for encoding, storing, and retrieving knowledge that develops in couples, families, and teams, described by Daniel Wegner: members specialise in different domains and maintain a directory of who knows what, so the group remembers more, with less duplication, than its members could alone. Built through shared experience and therefore fragile — losing a long-term partner or veteran colleague removes part of everyone's memory, not just one person's.

## Groupthink

Irving Janis's name for the failure mode of cohesive groups under pressure: unanimity becomes prized over accuracy, dissenters censor themselves, silence is read as agreement, and the group confidently adopts a decision its members privately doubt. The packaged syndrome rests on retrospective case studies and has replicated only partially in the laboratory; its component mechanisms — conformity pressure, self-censorship, the illusion of unanimity — are individually well documented, and its procedural remedies remain useful hygiene.

## Wisdom of crowds

The phenomenon, popularised by James Surowiecki from Francis Galton's ox-weighing observation, in which the aggregate of many independent judgements is more accurate than most individual ones, because independent errors cancel while shared signal remains. Strictly conditional: it requires genuine diversity, independence of judgement, and aggregation of private estimates. Discussion, anchoring, and shared sources correlate the errors — turning a wise crowd into an echo with the confidence of a consensus.

## Collective intelligence

The proposal, associated with Anita Woolley and colleagues, that groups have a general, stable capability ("c factor") predicting their performance across many different tasks — reportedly linked more to interaction patterns, such as evenly distributed speaking turns and social sensitivity, than to members' average intelligence. A young and partially contested literature whose specific correlations should not be quoted as fact; the conservative core is that group performance is a property of the group and its process, not a simple sum of member ability.
