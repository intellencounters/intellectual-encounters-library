# 2. What Do You Owe, and to Whom?

The message comes at 3 a.m. your time, because it is a reasonable hour theirs. A cousin's wedding, a roof that needs redoing, a sibling's fees — and the quiet assumption, never quite a request, that you will send what is needed. You lie in the dark doing arithmetic you already know the answer to: the transfer, or the buffer you have been building against the next time a shift is cut. You are not deciding whether you love your family. You are deciding what love *requires* — a different, harder question.

Chapter 1 gave you a way to examine a demand. This chapter is about the demands that survive examination — the ones where there really is a reason underneath, and the reason is *obligation*. What do you owe, and to whom? To family who raised you at a cost you can never fully see; to yourself and the one life you have; to strangers, whose claim is easy to forget and hard to justify ignoring. These pulls do not always point the same way, and no formula dissolves the tension. But three great traditions ask the question in different ways, and knowing them turns a private ache into something you can actually reason about.

## 2.1 Three ways to ask "what should I do?"

For two and a half thousand years, most serious thinking about how to act has flowed into three channels — three lenses, not teams to join. Most of us reach for all three without noticing, which is exactly why our moral reasoning so often feels like a tangle.

The first lens looks *forward*, to results: of the paths open to me, which produces the best outcomes for everyone affected? Call this the ethics of consequences. The second looks *sideways*, to rules and duties that hold regardless of outcome: what would I be *wronging* someone by doing, even if the results came out well? Call this the ethics of duty. The third looks *inward*, to the person you are becoming: what would a good person — honest, courageous, neither coward nor doormat — do here, and who do I become by choosing it? Call this the ethics of character.

Hold one situation — the 3 a.m. transfer — and turn each lens on it in turn.

## 2.2 Consequences: the arithmetic of outcomes

The ethics of consequences says an act is right insofar as it produces good results, and its most famous form is utilitarianism: act so as to produce the greatest well-being, counting each person's well-being equally. :::glossary term="Consequentialism"::: is the family this view belongs to; John Stuart Mill — the same Mill from Chapter 1 — gave it its most humane statement, arguing that the standard is not your own happiness but the total happiness impartially considered.[^mill2]

Turn it on the transfer. Consequences asks you to add it up honestly: the fees keep a sibling in school, perhaps lifting a whole household's trajectory — a large, real good. Against it: the deposit you are protecting is not selfishness but *your* future stability, and a person who never builds a buffer may end up unable to help anyone when the next crisis lands. The lens does not tell you the family loses. It tells you to stop pretending your own future counts for nothing — because in the impartial sum, it counts exactly as much as anyone's.

:::definition term="Consequentialism"
The family of ethical theories holding that the rightness of an act depends entirely on its consequences — how much good and harm it produces. Utilitarianism is its best-known form: maximise overall well-being, counting each affected person's interests equally, including your own. Its power is impartiality and honesty about trade-offs; its danger is that it can seem to license sacrificing a person whenever the numbers favour it.
:::

:::counter title="Objection: some things should not be put on the scale" to="the consequentialist reading of obligation"
The strongest objection is that running family life as an arithmetic of outcomes can quietly authorise betrayals whenever the sums come out right — and that some bonds are *degraded* by being weighed at all. If the only reason you keep a promise to your mother is that keeping it maximised total well-being this month, you have not really made her a promise; you have made a forecast, revisable the moment the numbers move. Critics from Bernard Williams onward argue that a life lived as a running total has no room for the deep commitments — to *these* people, not to well-being in general — that give a life its integrity. The consequentialist replies that rules like "keep promises" themselves produce good outcomes and so earn their place, and that a decent utilitarian does not recompute every love from scratch each morning. The debate is genuinely unsettled. But feel the force of the worry: not everything that matters is the kind of thing you should be caught weighing.
:::

## 2.3 Duty: lines you do not cross

The second lens denies that results are the whole story. Some acts, it says, are owed or forbidden *as a matter of principle*, whatever the outcome — because persons are owed a certain treatment simply as persons. Immanuel Kant built the most rigorous version. :::glossary term="Deontology"::: is the name for this duty-based ethics, and its heart is a test: act only on a principle you could will everyone to follow, and never treat a person merely as a means to your ends — always also as an end in themselves.[^kant]

This lens reframes the 3 a.m. question sharply, and it cuts both ways. Is the family treating you as an end — a person whose own life and plans have weight — or merely as a means, a remittance pipe whose function is to fund the household? And do you treat *them* as ends, people to whom you may owe genuine gratitude and care, or merely as a background you would like to mute? Duty can license you to refuse an unreasonable demand — because you are a person, not an instrument — and it can bind you to a yes you would rather dodge, because gratitude to those who bore real costs for you is not optional just because repaying it is inconvenient.

:::definition term="Deontology"
The family of ethical theories holding that some acts are right or wrong in themselves, by principle or duty, independent of their consequences. In Kant's version, morality means acting only on principles you could consistently will to be universal, and always treating persons as ends in themselves, never merely as means. Its strength is that it protects individuals from being sacrificed to a total; its difficulty is that duties can collide, and it does not always tell you which yields.
:::

Duty falls unevenly, and honesty requires naming it. A daughter may hear it as "obey and marry"; a son as "provide, and never show the strain." Both are handed a principle dressed as a personal command. The Kantian test answers without either rebellion or surrender: ask whether the principle behind the demand treats *you*, too, as an end — a person owed reasons — or only as a means to someone else's plan. That question is available equally to the daughter and the son, and it is not disrespect. It is the exact respect the theory demands you show yourself.

- Will sending the money produce more total happiness than keeping it?

The first question is consequentialist, the third belongs to virtue ethics, and the fourth is the custom question from Chapter 1. The Kantian lens asks whether persons are being respected as ends — and it presses that question on the family's demand *and* on any dismissive refusal of it.
:::

## 2.4 Character: who you become by choosing

The third lens is the oldest, and for many the most intuitive. It shifts the question from *what act is right?* to *what person am I becoming?* Aristotle argued that a good life is the activity of a good character — and that character is built by habit, one choice at a time, until acting well becomes second nature.[^aristotle] :::glossary term="Virtue ethics"::: is this tradition, and it asks of the transfer not "what do the sums say?" nor "what does the rule command?" but: what would a person of good character — generous but not self-erasing, dutiful but not servile, honest with the family and with themselves — actually do?

This lens is uniquely good at catching two failures the others can miss. There is *deficiency*: the person so guarded that they give nothing, calling their meanness prudence. And there is *excess*: the person so unable to disappoint anyone that they give until nothing of their own life is left, calling their collapse devotion. Virtue lives in neither extreme. The generous person gives, and also builds the buffer that lets them keep giving; the good son provides, and also says the true thing about the strain instead of performing an ease he does not feel. Character asks for the action a whole, undamaged person would take — rarely the loudest option on either side.

:::definition term="Virtue ethics"
The ethical tradition, rooted in Aristotle, that takes the central question to be not "which act is right?" but "what character is good, and what would a person of good character do?" Virtues — courage, honesty, generosity, justice — are stable traits lying between extremes of excess and deficiency, formed by habit. Its strength is realism about how moral life is actually lived and grown; its difficulty is that "what the virtuous person would do" can be hard to specify when you most need an answer.
:::

Notice that the three lenses often converge — a generous act with good outcomes that treats everyone as an end is right by all three standards, and much of ordinary decency is like that. They earn their keep precisely at the hard cases, where they pull apart, because then each names a distinct thing you might otherwise lose sight of: the outcomes, the persons, the self you are shaping.

## 2.5 Filial duty and the life that is yours

Now the deepest tension in this chapter, the one the whole book circles. On one side stands :::glossary term="Filial duty"::: — the obligation to honour, care for, and defer to the parents who made your life possible. It is among the most widely held moral ideas in human history; the *Bhagavad Gita* frames right action partly in terms of one's duties within a web of relationships one did not choose, and Confucius made filial devotion the root from which all other virtue grows.[^gita] To wave it away as mere backwardness is neither fair nor true; it names something real.

On the other side stands self-determination — the claim, pressed hard by the world you now live in, that a life is only fully a person's own if its central choices are theirs: whom to marry, what work to do, where to make a home. Amartya Sen has argued that development itself is best understood as the expansion of people's real freedom to live lives they have reason to value — and a life scripted entirely by others, however lovingly, is a freedom curtailed.[^sen]

:::argument title="The case for weighting self-determination heavily"
conclusion: On the central choices of a life — marriage, vocation, where to live — the person living it should hold the deciding weight, even against sincere family expectation.
premise: These choices shape the daily texture and long arc of one life more than any other, and their costs and rewards fall first and hardest on the one who makes them.
premise: A person is the author of their life only if its defining choices are genuinely theirs to make.
premise: Being the author of one's own life is a central human good, not a luxury — the very thing that makes a life fully a person's own.
:::

:::counter title="Objection: the self that decides was made by others" to="the case for weighting self-determination heavily"
The strongest reply is that the "autonomous self" this argument exalts is not a self-made thing at all. Your language, your values, your very sense of what a good life looks like — even the ideal of independence you now invoke — were poured into you by a family that fed, taught, and sacrificed for you, often at costs you will only understand when it is your turn. To treat your preferences as sovereign while treating the people who *formed* those preferences as mere external pressure is, the objection runs, ingratitude dressed as philosophy. Many traditions answer that the self is constituted *by* its relationships, so that honouring family is not a limit on freedom but part of what a flourishing life *is*. The reply does not erase self-determination — a debt of gratitude is not a blank cheque, and no one who bore costs for you thereby earns the right to script your whole life. But it forbids the easy picture in which family is only ever a weight pressing from outside a self that owes it nothing. You are, in part, made of them. That is why the tension is hard, and why anyone who tells you it is easy — in either direction — is not to be trusted.
:::

There is no formula that dissolves this. What you can refuse is the false picture on either side — that you owe everything, or that you owe nothing. You owe something real, to people who are partly you; and your life is nonetheless yours to answer for. Reasoning well here is not finding the equation but holding both truths at once, without letting either bully the other into silence.

:::uncertainty title="What I won't assert about money sent home"
It is often claimed that migrants send a large and rising share of their earnings home, or that remittances exceed foreign aid to many countries. Figures of this kind are studied by economists and international bodies, and something in the vicinity is likely true — but the specific magnitudes vary by year, country, and definition, and I will not print a number I cannot verify here as though it were settled — citation needed — verify before publishing. Treat the pressure described in this chapter as a real and common experience, not as a quantified statistic.
:::

- Nothing — one of the three theories is provably correct and the others are simply errors.
- Consequences, duty, and character each reliably track something that genuinely matters — outcomes, persons, and the self you become — so on hard cases they can pull apart without one being obviously the whole truth.
- Moral theories are just opinions, so reasoning about obligation is pointless.
- Only the theory your own culture teaches can be correct for you.
answer: 2
explanation: The chapter does not crown a winner. Each lens captures a real dimension of moral life the others can neglect. Their disagreement on hard cases is informative, not embarrassing: it shows you the distinct things at stake — the outcomes, the persons involved, and the character you are forming — rather than letting any one of them hide the others.
:::

:::reflect
Take a real obligation you are weighing right now — money home, a marriage timeline, a move, a career your family did not choose for you. Write three short paragraphs, one from each lens: what does the ethics of *consequences* recommend, weighing your future honestly alongside theirs? What does *duty* say, applying the means/ends test to both the demand and any refusal? What would a person of good *character* — neither a doormat nor a wall — actually do? Where the three disagree, name the exact good each is protecting. You are not looking for a tie-breaker. You are making sure no real stake stays invisible to you.
:::

The three lenses tell you *how* to weigh what you owe. They do not yet tell you what the whole thing is *for* — what a life well lived even amounts to, and whether, under this much pressure, your choices are meaningfully your own at all. That is the final chapter.

[^mill2]: John Stuart Mill, *Utilitarianism* (1863), is the classic accessible statement of the view that the right act is the one that best promotes overall happiness, impartially considered. The earlier systematic source is Jeremy Bentham.
[^kant]: Immanuel Kant, *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785), sets out the "categorical imperative," including the formulas of universal law and of humanity (treat persons always also as ends, never merely as means).
[^aristotle]: Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* (4th century BCE), develops the account of virtue as a settled disposition lying between excess and deficiency, formed by habituation, and of the good life as activity in accordance with virtue.
[^gita]: The *Bhagavad Gita* frames right action in terms of duty (*dharma*) within relationships one has not chosen; Confucius, in the *Analects*, treats filial devotion (*xiao*) as the root of the other virtues. Both are cited here as major, canonical expressions of duty within family and role, not as settling the tension this section describes.
[^sen]: Amartya Sen, *Development as Freedom* (1999), argues that the expansion of substantive human freedoms — including the freedom to choose a life one has reason to value — is both the primary end and the principal means of development.
