# Citations & Sources

This book cites only canonical, widely available works — texts you can find in any decent library or reputable edition. It deliberately avoids specific page numbers, editions, translation-dependent wordings, and empirical statistics, because those vary between printings and cannot be responsibly asserted without checking. Where a specific empirical claim was tempting, it appears inside an `:::uncertainty` block in the chapter rather than here.

## Works cited

**John Stuart Mill, *On Liberty* (1859).** Chapter 3, "Of Individuality," is the source for the "despotism of custom" and the argument that beliefs held by habit alone, never examined by the person living inside them, are not truly that person's own. Grounds the custom-versus-justification distinction in Chapter 1.

**John Stuart Mill, *Utilitarianism* (1863).** The classic accessible statement of the consequentialist standard: the right act best promotes overall happiness, impartially considered. Used in Chapter 2's account of the ethics of consequences. (The earlier systematic source for utilitarianism is Jeremy Bentham.)

**Immanuel Kant, *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785).** Sets out the categorical imperative, including the formula of universal law and the formula of humanity — treat persons always also as ends, never merely as means. Grounds the duty-based (deontological) lens in Chapter 2.

**Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* (4th century BCE).** Source for two ideas: virtue as a settled disposition lying between excess and deficiency and formed by habit (Chapter 2), and *eudaimonia* as flourishing across a whole life, requiring activity in accordance with virtue plus a sufficiency of external goods (Chapter 3).

**The *Bhagavad Gita*.** Cited in Chapter 2 as a major canonical expression of right action (*dharma*) as duty within relationships one has not chosen. Referenced to illustrate the depth and reach of filial and role-based obligation, not to settle the tension the chapter describes.

**Confucius, *Analects*.** Cited in Chapter 2 for the treatment of filial devotion (*xiao*) as the root from which the other virtues grow — a central historical statement of filial duty.

**Amartya Sen, *Development as Freedom* (1999).** Cited in Chapter 2 for the view that the expansion of substantive human freedoms — including the freedom to choose a life one has reason to value — is both the end and the principal means of development. Supports the weight given to self-determination.

**Harry Frankfurt, "Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person" (1971).** Cited in Chapter 3's discussion of free will for the distinction between acting on one's desires and endorsing, at a higher level, the desires one acts on — a widely read modern contribution to what freedom requires.

**Epictetus, *Enchiridion* (2nd century CE), and Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* (2nd century CE).** Cited in Chapter 3 as classic sources for the Stoic discipline of distinguishing what is "up to us" from what is not, and concentrating effort on the former. Cited for the practical stance, not as proof of any metaphysical thesis. Marcus Aurelius is cited again in Chapter 6 for the *memento mori* strand of Stoic practice.

**Plato, *Theaetetus*.** Cited in Chapter 4 as the classical source in which knowledge as true belief plus an account is explored and already problematised. Grounds the distinction between knowledge and confident belief.

**Edmund Gettier, "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?" (1963).** Cited in Chapter 4 (footnote) as the standard modern complication of the justified-true-belief analysis: even justified true belief can be a matter of luck. Cited as a caveat, not developed in the text.

**Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011).** Cited in Chapter 4 for the finding that fluency, repetition, and ease of recall generate feelings of confidence and truth that are only loosely coupled to accuracy.

**C. A. J. Coady, *Testimony: A Philosophical Study* (1992).** Cited in Chapter 4 as a widely cited modern treatment of the epistemology of testimony and of the dependence of nearly all individual knowledge on the word of others.

**Thomas Bayes, "An Essay towards solving a Problem in the Doctrine of Chances" (1763, posthumous).** Cited in Chapter 4 as the historical source of Bayes's theorem. The chapter uses only its everyday moral — weigh evidence by how much more expected it is if the claim is true than if false — not its mathematics.

**C. Thi Nguyen, "Echo Chambers and Epistemic Bubbles" (*Episteme*, 2020).** Cited in Chapter 4 for the distinction between epistemic bubbles (contrary voices absent, repaired by exposure) and echo chambers (contrary voices pre-discredited, not repaired by exposure).

**Aristotle, *Sophistical Refutations* (4th century BCE).** Cited in Chapter 5 as the founding catalogue of fallacious reasoning and the ancestor of every later list of fallacies.

**David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40), Book III.** Cited in Chapter 5 for the classical statement of the gap between descriptive claims and evaluative conclusions ("is" and "ought"), on which the appeals to tradition and to nature founder.

**Nicholas Shackel, "The Vacuity of Postmodernist Methodology" (2005).** Cited in Chapter 5 as the paper that introduced the castle analogy and the name "motte and bailey" for the retreat-and-re-advance argumentative pattern.

**Epicurus, "Letter to Menoeceus" (4th–3rd century BCE).** Cited in Chapter 6 as the source of the argument that "death is nothing to us," presented at full strength before being answered.

**Lucretius, *De Rerum Natura* (1st century BCE), Book III.** Cited in Chapter 6 for the symmetry argument: the time after death mirrors the untroubling time before birth.

**Thomas Nagel, "Death" (1970; reprinted in *Mortal Questions*, 1979).** Cited in Chapter 6 as the classic modern statement of the deprivation account — that death can be bad as a loss of goods without being an experience — against Epicurus.

**Bernard Williams, "The Makropulos Case: Reflections on the Tedium of Immortality" (1973).** Cited in Chapter 6 for the provocation that an endless life would exhaust the desires that give a person reason to go on. Cited as contested, not settled.

**Seneca, *On the Shortness of Life* (*De Brevitate Vitae*, 1st century CE).** Cited in Chapter 6 for the argument that life is long enough if well invested, and for the charge that we guard property jealously while letting anyone seize our time.

**Derek Parfit, *Reasons and Persons* (1984).** Cited in Chapter 6 for the view that personal identity over time is less deep than assumed, reframing prudence toward one's future self as concern for someone real who cannot yet speak.

**Alasdair MacIntyre, *After Virtue* (1981).** Cited in Chapter 6 for the claims that actions are intelligible only within narratives and that the unity of a life is the unity of a narrative quest — the core of narrative identity.

**Galen Strawson, "Against Narrativity" (2004).** Cited in Chapter 6 as the best-known counterview: many people are genuinely and non-defectively episodic, and narrativity is not a requirement for a good life.

**Plato, *Phaedo*.** Cited in Chapter 6 (footnote) as the source of the ancient characterisation of philosophy as a preparation for death.

## A note before you reuse any of these

Treat every reference above as a pointer, not a finished bibliographic entry. Before quoting, teaching from, or citing any of these works elsewhere, verify the exact wording, chapter or section, translation, and edition against a reliable copy — translations of Aristotle, Kant, the *Gita*, the *Analects*, and the Stoics differ substantially, and a phrase remembered is not a phrase quoted. Nothing here should be reproduced as an exact quotation without that check. And any specific statistic, dated fact, or empirical result belongs behind the phrase **"citation needed — verify before publishing"** until it has been confirmed against a primary source — verify before reuse.
