# 3. Belonging in the In-Between

It is Diwali, and you are in Germany. Perhaps there is a small gathering — a few friends from home, food that is almost right, messages flying in a WhatsApp group where everyone is celebrating in a different time zone. The lamps at home are being lit without you. And you notice a feeling with no clean name: you are not fully *here*, in the grey November evening outside the window, and no longer fully *there*, in the festival you can see only through a screen. You are in a third place, the in-between, and this chapter is a map of it — because millions live there, it has been studied carefully, and its loneliness is not a private failure but a describable condition with a shape and even, at times, a strange dignity.

## 3.1 The stranger who stays

More than a century ago the sociologist Georg Simmel wrote a short, piercing essay on a figure he called *the stranger* — not the traveller who comes today and goes tomorrow, but the one who comes today and *stays*.[^simmel] The stranger is a full member of the group in the sense of being physically present and economically woven in, yet is never quite *of* it: near and far at once, inside the walls and outside the family. Simmel saw that this position is not simply a lack. The stranger's half-distance gives a particular clarity — the freedom to see the local rules as *rules*, because he did not grow up taking them for granted — along with a particular loneliness, the sense of being a permanent guest.

You may recognise yourself in this. To your German colleagues you are competent, present, part of the team — and also, gently, marked as from elsewhere, asked where you are "really" from, complimented on your German in a way that quietly reminds you it was ever in question. To your family you are the one who left, increasingly consulted about the wider world and increasingly unable to be present for the small daily life of home. Simmel's point is that this is a *social position*, one societies have always produced, with its own predictable gifts and costs. You did not invent it, and you are not failing at it. You are occupying it.

## 3.2 The double absence

The most exact account of what this costs was given by the Algerian-French sociologist Abdelmalek Sayad, who spent his life studying migration and named its central wound the :::glossary term="Double absence":::.[^sayad] The migrant, Sayad saw, is doubly absent: absent from the place they left, and not fully present in the place they arrived. At home they become the one who is *not there* — missing from the weddings and the funerals, the illnesses and the ordinary Tuesdays, present only as remittances and as a voice on a call. In the new country they are physically present but socially provisional — a worker, a permit, a case number at the Ausländerbehörde — rarely received as a full person with a past and a future, only as a function the host society needs.

Sayad's insight was that migration is always *two* stories, not one: an emigration (a subtraction from one society) and an immigration (an incomplete addition to another), and that most accounts, in both countries, tell only the half that suits them. The receiving country sees only the immigrant — the labour, the paperwork, the question of integration. The sending country sees only the emigrant — the absence, the money, the one who is expected to have "made it." Neither sees the whole person suspended between the two. To feel unseen in *both* directions at once is the double absence, and naming it matters, because a pain you can name is a pain you can stop mistaking for a defect in yourself.

:::definition term="Double absence"
Abdelmalek Sayad's term for the migrant's condition of being absent from two worlds at once: no longer fully present in the society of origin (missing from its daily life, reduced to remittances and a voice on the phone) and not yet fully present in the society of arrival (received as a worker or a legal case rather than a whole person). The double absence is not homesickness alone; it is the structural experience of being incompletely seen in both places simultaneously.
:::

## 3.3 What you carry in your body

Why can you not simply choose to belong fully in the new place — learn the language, adopt the customs, and be done with the in-between? Part of the answer lies in a concept from the sociologist Pierre Bourdieu: the :::glossary term="Habitus":::.[^bourdieu] The habitus is the set of dispositions — tastes, reflexes, bodily habits, gut senses of what is comfortable, polite, funny, or shameful — that a society writes into you in childhood, below the level of conscious thought. It is why a food tastes like home and another like effort; why one kind of silence feels respectful and another rude; why you know, without deciding, how close to stand, how loudly to laugh, when to look away.

The habitus is durable. It was laid down in your first world and you carry it into your second, where it does not quite fit — and the mismatch is felt not as an opinion but as a bodily unease, a half-second of hesitation, a sense of being slightly wrong in ways hard to articulate. This is why belonging cannot be willed in an afternoon. You are not merely learning new rules; you are living with an old set written into your reflexes while a new set is only slowly, partially, laid over it. Over years, a second habitus does form — you acquire new reflexes, new comforts — and many migrants come to move with real fluency between both. But the first does not simply erase, which is why even the most settled migrant can be ambushed by homesickness at the smell of a spice or the sound of a song.

## 3.4 Belonging as something built, not found

Here the story turns, because the in-between is not only loss. Out of it people build something: a :::glossary term="Diaspora":::. The word once meant a scattered people, but sociologically a diaspora is not just dispersion — it is the *community that scattered people create across distance*, held together by shared origin, memory, ritual, food, language, and now by the ceaseless traffic of the group chat. The Diwali gathering in a German flat, the temple or mosque or church found in a new city, the friend who understands without explanation why the phone call from home was so heavy — these are not consolation prizes for having lost home. They are a new form of belonging, made in the in-between and available nowhere else.

The political scientist Benedict Anderson observed that even a nation is an *imagined community*: its members will never meet most of their fellow-members, yet feel a real bond with them, sustained by shared stories and symbols.[^anderson] A diaspora is imagined in exactly this way, only stretched across borders — a community you hold in your mind and reach through a screen, as real in its effects as any village. And the cultural theorist Stuart Hall argued that diasporic identity is not a fixed essence you either keep or lose but something continually *produced* — always becoming, always negotiated, never simply the preservation of an original.[^hall] On this view you are not a diminished version of who you would have been at home. You are becoming someone new, in a community that is itself new, and that is a making, not only a losing.

:::argument title="The in-between is a distinctive place to belong from, not merely a failure to belong"
conclusion: Living between two societies can be a genuine and valuable form of belonging in its own right, not only a deficient version of belonging fully to one.
premise: The migrant's half-distance grants a real vantage point — the ability to see each society's rules as rules rather than as nature — that people settled wholly inside one society rarely gain.
premise: Diaspora communities are not mere substitutes for a lost home but new forms of solidarity, with their own bonds, rituals, and mutual understanding available nowhere else.
premise: Identity built across two worlds is a making of something new, not only the losing of something old, and a made thing can be owned and valued.
:::

:::counter title="Objection: this risks romanticising real and unequal suffering" to="The in-between is a distinctive place to belong from">
The honest objection is that celebrating the in-between can become a comfortable story told by those who landed softly, papering over people for whom it is mostly pain. The "vantage point" of the outsider is easier to enjoy with a secure visa, a professional job, and a supportive community than for someone isolated, undocumented, exploited, or barred from the host society by racism — for whom the double absence is not a rich third identity but daily grinding loss. Diaspora solidarity is real, but it is uneven: it can also police its members, reimport the very expectations they left, and exclude those who do not fit. To say the in-between *can* be a place to belong from must never harden into a claim that it *is*, for everyone, or into a demand that people find dignity in a position that is, for many, simply imposed and hard. The vantage point is a possibility to be honest about, not a consolation to be prescribed.
:::

## 3.5 Negotiating belonging

So what does one actually *do* in the in-between? The unhelpful answers are the two pure ones. Full :::glossary term="Assimilation":::: — dissolving entirely into the host society, shedding the first world completely — is rarely available (the habitus and the host's own gaze see to that) and, where it is pursued, often costs more than it returns, purchased by cutting away a part of oneself. Full preservation — living in the host country while refusing any part of it, keeping the community hermetically sealed — buys safety at the price of the very openness that migration was partly *for*. Most people, sensibly, do neither. They *negotiate*.

Negotiating belonging means treating identity not as a single vote you cast once for one country but as an ongoing set of choices, situation by situation: which parts of home to keep, which parts of the new place to take up, which expectations from each to honour and which to let go. It is deciding to keep the festival and drop the marriage timeline; to take the German directness at work and keep the family warmth at home; to send money and also, sometimes, to say honestly that you cannot. This is not a betrayal of either world. It is the ordinary, unheroic labour of the person who belongs to two, done a little more consciously — the same move this whole book has been about, of turning what is done *to* you into what is decided *by* you, applied now to the largest question of all.

:::uncertainty title="How do most migrants actually resolve the in-between over time?"
It would sharpen this chapter to state how the in-between typically resolves — for instance, what proportion of long-term migrants report a stable dual identity versus enduring alienation, or how belonging shifts across the second and third generations. There is a large and serious research literature on integration, acculturation, and second-generation identity, but the findings differ markedly by country of origin, host society, class, race, legal status, and generation, and no single figure would honestly represent them. Citation needed — verify before publishing. Take the account of "negotiation" here as a well-attested pattern among many, not as the measured outcome for all.

## 3.6 An honest close

I promised at the start that this book would not make the weight vanish, and it has not. The Sunday call will come again; the festival will be far away again; the question of where you truly belong will not be settled by any chapter. What has changed, if the book has done its work, is smaller and more durable than a solution. The pressure that felt like a private accusation now has a public shape: norms and roles, social facts, constructed success, inherited and unequal scripts, the double absence, the negotiated self. You have not been given an escape from the weight. You have been given its map — and a map does not lift the load, but it tells you that you are not lost, that others have crossed this ground, and that the paths are more numerous than they looked from inside the fog.

Let me be honest about the limits, too. Understanding is not the same as change, and no amount of sociology pays the rent, mends a strained bond with a parent, or grants a visa. Some of the weight described here is genuinely unjust and ought to be resisted, not merely understood; some is the reasonable cost of belonging to people you love, worth carrying with open eyes; and telling those two apart is the work of a life, not a book. What sociology offers is not relief but company and clarity: the knowledge that the heaviness you feel is real, is shared, has causes outside you, and leaves — even so — a genuine space in which you get to choose. That space is small some weeks and larger others. It is yours. Guard it, use it kindly, and remember that the people back home, and the strangers who became your friends in the in-between, are mostly trying, as you are, to carry their own weight of expectation with grace.
