# 1. The Invisible Rules

It is a Sunday evening. You have finished a warehouse shift, you have an exam on Thursday, and the phone lights up: a video call from home. The faces are warm and you are glad to see them. Then, somewhere between the news about a cousin's wedding and a question about your health, it arrives — the question that lands like a weight on the chest. *When are you going to settle down? When will this studying be finished? When do we see you doing well?* Nobody is angry. Nobody has said anything cruel. And yet you end the call heavier than you began it, carrying a pressure you cannot quite name and cannot quite put down.

This book is about that weight — not about how to make it disappear, but about where it comes from, why it presses so hard, and what changes when you can see its shape. The first move, and the hardest, is to stop treating the weight as a private defect of your own life and start treating it as an object you can examine. Sociologists call it turning a *trouble* into a *structure*.

## 1.1 The rules nobody wrote down

Begin with something small. When you board a crowded U-Bahn in Germany, you do not push to the front; you let people off first, you keep your voice low, you do not stare. Nobody handed you a rulebook at the airport. And yet within weeks you were following rules you could not have written down — and, more tellingly, you began to feel a small flush of discomfort when someone *else* broke them. That discomfort is the clue. A rule you can feel being broken is a rule that lives in you.

Sociologists call these rules :::glossary term="Norm":::s — shared, largely unspoken expectations about how people in a given situation should behave. Some norms are trivial (which side of the escalator you stand on). Some are heavy (whom you may marry, how you must treat your parents, what counts as a respectable job). What they share is that they feel less like opinions you hold and more like facts about the world you inhabit. You do not experience "arrive on time in Germany" as a preference; you experience it as simply *how things are done here*.

Bound up with norms is the idea of a :::glossary term="Role":::. A role is a bundle of expectations attached not to you personally but to a *position* you occupy — daughter, son, elder sibling, student, employee, the-one-who-went-abroad. When you pick up the phone to your parents, you do not merely talk; you step into the role of *child*, with everything that role is expected to carry. Much of the strain in a migrant's life comes from holding several roles at once whose expectations do not agree: the dutiful son who should be home, the ambitious professional who had to leave, the independent adult who pays their own rent, the family's future who must not fail. Each role is reasonable on its own. The weight is in the collision.

:::definition term="Norm"
A norm is a shared expectation about how people ought to behave in a particular situation, enforced not usually by law but by ordinary social approval and disapproval — a warm nod, an awkward silence, a raised eyebrow, gossip, pride, shame. Norms are learned so early and held so deeply that they are often mistaken for instinct or common sense. Naming a norm as a norm does not automatically make it wrong; it makes it *visible*, and therefore open to question.
:::

## 1.2 Durkheim and the things that press from outside

At the end of the nineteenth century, one of sociology's founders, Émile Durkheim, was trying to establish that society was a real object of study and not merely a collection of individuals doing as they pleased. His key concept was the :::glossary term="Social fact":::.[^durkheim] A social fact is a way of acting, thinking, or feeling that exists *outside* any single person, yet exercises a real force *over* them. Language is a social fact: you did not invent Tamil or Hindi or German, you were born into them, and they shape what you can easily think. Money is a social fact. So is marriage. So is the going definition of a "good job."

Durkheim's test for a social fact was beautifully simple: try to resist it and feel the push-back. Try speaking a language no one else knows; try refusing to use money; try telling your family you will never marry and never explain why. The resistance you meet — the friction, the disapproval, the sheer difficulty — is the social fact making itself felt. It is *external* (it was there before you and will outlast you) and it is *constraining* (it presses on you whether or not you consent).

This reframes the Sunday phone call. The pressure you feel is not simply your mother's personality, or your own weakness, or a failure to "just not care what people think." It is a social fact bearing down through a particular person who loves you — and she is, in a sense, also its carrier, not only its author, feeling the same weight from *her* mother, her neighbours, her community. Seeing this does not dissolve the pressure. But it moves it from *me versus my family* toward *all of us inside a structure none of us built alone*, and that is a more honest and more workable place to stand.

Durkheim gave us one more gift here. In studying suicide, he noticed that when the rules that bind people to one another loosen too far — when old expectations dissolve faster than new ones can form — people can fall into a state he called :::glossary term="Anomie":::: a disorientation that comes not from too many rules but from too few that still hold. The migrant knows this territory. You left a world of dense, sometimes suffocating expectation and arrived in one that often shrugs and says *do as you like*. That freedom is real and precious. It can also be strangely lonely, and it helps to know the loneliness has a name and is not a personal failing.

## 1.3 Mills: from private trouble to public issue

Sixty years after Durkheim, the American sociologist C. Wright Mills gave the move we have been making a name that has never been bettered: the :::glossary term="Sociological imagination":::.[^mills] It is the capacity to see the link between *personal troubles* and *public issues* — between what happens in your own biography and the larger structures and history you happen to be living inside.

Mills's example was unemployment. If one man in a city of a hundred thousand is out of work, that is his personal trouble, and we look to his character, skills, and choices to understand it. But when fifteen million are out of work, no account of individual character will do; we are looking at a public issue, rooted in how the economy and society are arranged. The mistake — and it is the default mistake, the one everyone makes — is to keep explaining a public issue in the language of private trouble: *I must be lazy, I must have chosen badly, something is wrong with me.*

Apply this to the weight you carry. That the wages of a working student are low, that immigration paperwork is exhausting, that families expect marriage by a certain age, that "success" is measured in a currency you did not mint — none of these is your personal failing, and none can be fixed by trying harder to feel differently. They are patterns, shared by thousands of people in situations like yours, produced by structures and histories larger than any of you. The sociological imagination does not tell you what to do. It does something quieter and more freeing: it lets you stop spending your strength on self-blame for things that were never only about you.

:::argument title="Why naming the structure helps, even when it changes nothing outside you"
conclusion: Learning to see a personal trouble as part of a public structure is worth doing even when it does not remove a single external pressure.
premise: Much of the pain of expectation is not the expectation itself but the added, private conviction that failing to meet it reveals a flaw in *you specifically*.
premise: Seeing that a pressure is structural and widely shared removes that second, self-directed layer of suffering without requiring anyone else to change.
premise: A person who is no longer spending energy on self-blame has more of it left for the choices that are genuinely theirs to make.
:::

:::counter title="Objection: naming a structure can become an excuse" to="Why naming the structure helps"
The honest objection is that this move can curdle. If every difficulty is "the structure," a person can talk themselves out of the responsibility and agency they really do have — treating an explanation as a permission slip. Some of what looks like an immovable social fact is in fact a choice we could contest, and calling it "structural" can quietly excuse not contesting it. Structural analysis is meant to *locate* agency accurately, not to dissolve it; used carelessly, it can leave someone comfortably diagnosed and permanently stuck. The corrective is to ask, after every structural insight, the harder follow-up: given that this is not all my fault, what part of it is nonetheless *mine to act on*? Mills himself insisted the point of the sociological imagination was to enlarge freedom, not to supply alibis.
:::

## 1.4 The invention of "success"

Now to the heaviest of the invisible rules: the idea of *success* itself. It feels like a fixed target — a salary, a title, a house, a marriage, a certain look on a parent's face. But look closely and the target dissolves into something built by people, in a particular time and place. It is, in the sociologist's phrase, a :::glossary term="Social construction":::.[^construction]

To call success socially constructed is *not* to call it fake or unimportant. Money is socially constructed and will still get you evicted if you lack it. The claim is narrower and sharper: what counts as a successful life is not written into nature; it is assembled by a society and could have been assembled otherwise — and, in fact, *is* assembled differently in the different worlds you move between. In your parents' world, formed by scarcity and hard-won respectability, success may mean a stable government job, a home owned outright, a family established early, a name spoken well of by neighbours. In the German professional world you are entering, it may mean autonomy, a specialised skill, mobility, the freedom to define your own path. In your own generation's global, online world, it may mean something else again, changing by the month.

Here is the quiet cruelty of it: you are often being measured, at once, by several definitions of success that *cannot all be satisfied together* — marry early enough to reassure your parents, build the demanding career that required you to leave, and enjoy the freedom your new country dangles in front of you. The exhaustion is not proof that you are failing at success. It is what it feels like to be graded, simultaneously, by three examiners marking three different exams. Seeing that the exams are constructed — that no one of them is simply *reality* — is the beginning of being able to choose which paper you will actually sit.

:::uncertainty title="How much of 'success' has really shifted between generations?"
It is tempting to state precisely how much the definition of a successful life has changed in one or two generations — for instance to claim that some specific percentage of young adults now rank personal fulfilment above financial security, or that the average age of marriage has risen by an exact number of years in a given community. Comparative survey evidence on changing life goals and marriage timing does exist and is worth consulting, but the specific figures are easy to get wrong and vary sharply by country, class, and method. Citation needed — verify before publishing. Treat the generational contrast in this section as a well-supported qualitative pattern, not as a precise measurement.
:::

## 1.5 What this chapter has bought us

We have not made the weight vanish. The phone will ring again next Sunday. But we have changed what kind of thing the weight *is*: it was a private accusation, and it is now an object with parts. Norms and roles are the shared rules you live inside. Social facts are those rules made real enough to push back when you resist them. The sociological imagination is the habit of tracing your private trouble back to the public structure that shaped it. And "success" — the heaviest expectation of all — turns out to be something built by people, differently in each world you inhabit, and therefore something you are allowed to examine rather than simply obey.

The rest of this book follows the weight into the two places it presses hardest: the family, with its inherited and unequal scripts for men and women (Chapter 2), and the strange country of belonging when you live between two societies at once (Chapter 3). None of it will tell you what to choose. All of it is meant to help you choose with your eyes open, and to be kinder to yourself while you do.

:::reflect
Think of one expectation you currently experience as a personal failing — something you quietly blame yourself for not meeting. Write it down in one sentence. Then, deliberately, rewrite it as a *public issue*: name the norm, role, or constructed standard of success behind it, and name at least a few other people who face the same pressure. Finally, and this is the harder half, mark honestly which part of it really is structural (not your fault, not yours to fix alone) and which part remains genuinely yours to act on. Keep what you write; Chapter 3 will ask you to look at it again.
:::

[^durkheim]: The concept of the *social fact* is developed in Émile Durkheim, *The Rules of Sociological Method* (1895); his account of *anomie* appears in *The Division of Labour in Society* (1893) and, most famously, in *Suicide* (1897).
[^mills]: C. Wright Mills, *The Sociological Imagination* (1959). The distinction between "personal troubles of milieu" and "public issues of social structure" is drawn in its opening chapter.
[^construction]: The classic statement that everyday reality is socially built is Peter L. Berger and Thomas Luckmann, *The Social Construction of Reality* (1966). For the argument that a specific *idea* of success — worldly achievement read as a sign of worth — has particular historical and cultural roots, see Max Weber, *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (1905).
