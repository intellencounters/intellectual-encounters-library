# 4. Why Rituals Work: Festival, Ceremony, and a Diwali Far From Home

It is Diwali, and you are on shift. Back home the house is full of light and noise and frying oil; your phone fills with videos you will watch on your break, by the loading dock in the cold. On your windowsill there is one small diya — battery-powered, because the Hausordnung forbids open flames — and when you get home at midnight you will sit with it a few minutes before you sleep.

Ask the coldly rational question: what is the point? A lamp is a lamp. You could skip it and call home tomorrow. Yet skipping it is exactly what you cannot do. Why does a small repeated gesture carry such weight, and its absence ache more than logic says it should?

Anthropology's central finding about ritual is quietly startling: rituals are not decoration on real life, and their power does not depend on believing anything in particular. They are social machinery — for marking time, transforming persons, and binding groups — and it works on believers and sceptics alike. Once you see how, your festivals far from home stop looking like nostalgia — and Germany stops looking like a country without rituals.

## 4.1 What a ritual actually does

A :::glossary term="Ritual"::: is a repeated, patterned, symbolic action — done in a set way, at set times, where the *form* of the act matters beyond its practical effect. Lighting a diya is a ritual; switching on the kitchen light is not. The test is that the act carries meaning a stranger could not deduce from its physics.

:::definition term="Ritual"
A repeated, patterned, symbolic action whose form matters beyond its practical effect — performed in set ways at set times, and legible as meaningful to insiders. Rituals need not be religious: handshakes, graduations, birthday songs, and the after-work drink all qualify. Their characteristic work is social — marking time, changing a person's status, binding a group — rather than informational.
:::

Notice what the definition does *not* say: that rituals are religious (many are not), or that they communicate beliefs. Anthropologists mostly read the arrow the other way: rituals are things you *do*, together, in a set form — and the doing itself produces effects no statement could: a marked moment, a changed status, a felt bond. That is why your grandmother and your atheist cousin can light the same lamp side by side and both leave the room altered. The machinery does not check your beliefs at the door.

## 4.2 Three doors: separation, liminality, incorporation

In 1909 the folklorist Arnold van Gennep noticed that ceremonies marking life transitions — birth, initiation, marriage, death — share one deep structure across cultures.[^vangennep] He called such a ceremony a :::glossary term="Rite of passage":::, and it has three phases. First **separation**: detachment from the old status — the bride leaves her parents' house. Then a strange middle phase he named the *liminal* stage, from the Latin for threshold: **betwixt and between**, no longer what you were, not yet what you will be. Finally **incorporation**: reception into the new status — the couple returns as married, the graduate gains a title.

The middle phase is the one to stare at. :::glossary term="Liminality"::: is uncomfortable by design — the initiate is often secluded, humbled, stripped of rank — because you cannot become someone new while everyone treats you as the old someone. The discomfort is the mechanism.

Now read your own migration through this lens; the fit is uncomfortably good. Leaving home was textbook separation — the airport farewell is as formal a rite as any village has. Being settled, "arrived" in both senses, is the incorporation you are working toward. And the years between — visa renewals, shift work, being no longer quite a Chennai person and not yet a settled German resident? That is liminality, lived not for three ceremonial days but for years — a state some people occupy long-term, with real strain and a strange freedom: the person on the threshold stands partly outside the rules of both houses.[^turner] Chapter 3 gave the psychology of the in-between; van Gennep gives its deep structure, and a consolation psychology cannot: the threshold is a recognised *place*, with a name, that ceremonies have always built — not a crack you personally fell into.

## 4.3 The current in the crowd

Rites of passage transform individuals. But the moments a festival actually delivers are collective: the second the fireworks start and a whole street gasps as one; an aarti sung by two hundred voices in a rented hall in Frankfurt. Victor Turner noticed that liminal moments do something remarkable to groups: ordinary rank dissolves, and people meet for a while not as manager and worker but as bare fellow humans. He called this feeling :::glossary term="Communitas"::: — the intense, levelling togetherness that flares up when the usual structure is suspended.[^turner]

Émile Durkheim had caught the same current half a century earlier: a group acting in unison — singing, moving, focusing on one object together — generates a felt surge of energy and unity he called :::glossary term="Collective effervescence":::, and this shared intensity, not doctrine, is the beating heart of religious life.[^durkheim] You have felt it, whatever you believe — at a sangeet, in a stadium. It is why Diwali alone over a video call is better than nothing and still nothing like enough. The lamp you can light alone; the current needs the crowd. And it is why the big diaspora celebration — hired hall, potluck, the children's dance that overruns — is not a copy of the real festival but a generator, producing the one thing back home makes that no phone can transmit.

## 4.4 Why it works even if you don't believe

Now the chapter's central claim, stated and then challenged honestly.

:::argument title="The participation argument: ritual works through doing, not believing"
conclusion: Taking part in your community's rituals is worth doing even if you do not hold the beliefs behind them, because most of what ritual gives — belonging, marked time, communitas, continuity with your past — is produced by shared participation itself.
premise: The observable effects of ritual — bonding, marked transitions, collective effervescence — arise from synchronised, patterned action in company, and occur in avowedly secular rituals (graduations, anthems, memorials) where no shared doctrine is present.
premise: A ritual's meaning is held socially, by the group and the tradition, not privately assembled in each head — so one participant's scepticism does not subtract from what the performance does.
premise: The goods in question are goods for sceptics as much as for believers, and for a migrant they are scarce and hard to replace.
:::

:::counter title="Objection: participation is never neutral" to="The participation argument: ritual works through doing, not believing"
The strongest reply is that "just participate" ignores what participation *does*. First, rituals are not innocent machinery: they encode and renew a social order, and joining in endorses that order whether you mean to or not — the wedding rite that moves you may also stage dowry display, caste boundary, and a script about a woman's place. Second, ritual binds partly by exclusion: a "we" is felt most strongly against a "they," so the warmth inside and the wall outside are one force. Third, for a genuine non-believer there is an integrity cost: performing reverence you do not feel, week after week, can corrode the sense that your actions mean what they say. The honest position is not "participation is free" but that it has real benefits and real costs — with the freedom to join some rituals, reform others, and refuse a few outright.
:::

Both halves are true, and the tension between them is where adult judgment lives. But notice what the disagreement is *not* about: neither side thinks ritual is empty. On that the sceptic and the priest agree.

:::uncertainty title="What the experiments do and do not show"
There is a lively experimental literature on ritual-like behaviour — synchronised movement and bonding, shared arousal at festivals, effects of ceremony on cooperation. This chapter quotes none of its specific findings: the field is young, replication records vary, and any particular figure ("synchrony increases cooperation by X per cent") is not one this book can stand behind — citation needed — verify before publishing. What rests on the broader anthropological record is the qualitative pattern itself: that rituals mark transitions, generate collective feeling, and bind groups. How large, how lasting, by what mechanism — open questions.
:::

## 4.5 Feierabend, Glühwein, Verein: Germany's rituals, read properly

It is tempting to conclude you moved from a ritual-rich culture to a ritual-poor one: Diwali against grey November. But run the definition from 4.1 over German life without assuming ritual must mean religion or noise, and the picture flips.

Take **Feierabend** — not just "end of work" but a small daily rite of separation: the deliberate closing of the working self, marked with a set phrase ("Schönen Feierabend!"), often a specific drink, and a boundary colleagues respect. It is a micro rite of passage performed daily, and the German insistence on it is ritual discipline, not coldness. Take the **Christmas markets**: repeated annually in set form, thick with prescribed foods and objects, generating Durkheim's shoulder-to-shoulder effervescence at two degrees above freezing — and almost nobody there for the theology. Take the **Verein** — football club, choir, allotment association. From outside, bureaucracy attached to a hobby; from inside, a ritual container: fixed meeting nights, annual festivals, ceremonies for long membership. Vereine are where a famously private society manufactures communitas on a schedule — which is why joining one is the most reliable door into German social life. Even the Sunday **Ruhezeit** — no drilling, shops shut — is a whole society jointly marking one day as different, long after the churchgoing that justified it faded.

The lesson is Chapter 1's, applied to ceremony: Germany is not less ritualised than India. Its rituals are quieter, more secular, more punctual — and invisible to you for the same reason yours are invisible to your colleagues: each side files its own rituals under "just normal life."

## 4.6 Remaking Diwali — and the rituals you have already invented

So what about your Diwali — compressed into the nearest Saturday, in a rented hall with families from four Indian states who would barely have met at home, half the aartis learned from YouTube? Is that a diminished copy of the real thing?

The historian Eric Hobsbawm gave us the sharpest tool for this worry. Studying traditions that feel timeless, he and his colleagues showed that many were recent constructions — an :::glossary term="Invented tradition"::: is a practice claiming deep continuity with the past that was in fact assembled recently.[^hobsbawm] The point is not that such traditions are fake, but that *all* living tradition is continually remade. The "authentic" Diwali your parents keep differs from their grandparents' — electric lights, cinema songs — and nobody calls their version a betrayal. Tradition is not a museum object that diaspora damages; it is a performance every generation edits in order to keep. Your Saturday Diwali is this generation's edit — and in fifty years, someone's grandmother will defend parts of it as the way it has always been done.

And once you can see ritual being made, you will notice you are already making some. Look at your closest WhatsApp group. The Sunday-evening call everyone quietly protects. The exact sentence sent before every exam. The birthday cascade with its fixed order of emojis. Every element of 4.1 is present: repetition, set form, symbolic weight, a group bound by the performance. Nobody planned this liturgy; groups under strain secrete ritual the way they secrete slang, because scattered people need marked time and shared form most of all. Your grandmother lights the lamp; you send the lamp emoji at the agreed hour. The theology differs; the machinery is the same.

:::reflect
Take stock of your ritual life in three honest lists: rituals kept from home — noting whether you keep each from conviction, from love of the people it binds you to, or from unexamined habit; German rituals you have adopted or could; and rituals your circle has invented without noticing. Then choose one ritual to keep deliberately, one to remake to fit your actual life, or one to set down — and justify the choice, in a sentence, to the strictest voice from 4.4, the one that says participation is never neutral.
:::

None of this makes the diya equal to the house full of light. Ritual is powerful machinery, but it is not magic: it cannot put your mother in the room, and a chapter pretending otherwise would deserve your distrust. What anthropology offers is smaller and solid. The ache of a working Diwali is not sentimentality; it is the accurate perception of a real absence — of communitas, the current that needs a crowd. And the remedies you have half-built — the hall, the potluck, the group's liturgy of emojis — are the real thing under construction: tradition being remade, as always, by the people who need it. The next chapter turns to the medium most of your rituals run on: what happens to meaning, and to you, when you live in two languages at once.

[^vangennep]: Arnold van Gennep, *The Rites of Passage* (1909; English translation 1960). Source of the three-phase structure — separation, liminality (from *limen*, threshold), incorporation.

[^turner]: Victor Turner, *The Ritual Process: Structure and Anti-Structure* (1969). Turner developed van Gennep's liminal phase into a general account of threshold states and introduced "communitas" — noting also that some persons and groups occupy liminal positions long-term.

[^durkheim]: Émile Durkheim, *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912). Source of "collective effervescence," which Durkheim placed — rather than doctrine — at the root of religious life.

[^hobsbawm]: Eric Hobsbawm and Terence Ranger (eds.), *The Invention of Tradition* (1983). Documents traditions that present themselves as ancient but were constructed recently; invention and revision are normal features of living tradition, not corruptions of it.
