# Glossary

## Culture

The learned, shared systems of meaning through which a group interprets experience and organises behaviour — its assumptions, values, norms, and the codes by which people read one another. Culture is transmitted socially rather than inherited biologically, held in common rather than invented privately, and it operates mostly below awareness, which is why it feels to insiders like plain reality rather than one option among many. Visible customs (food, festivals, dress) are its surface; the interpretive layer beneath is what makes cross-cultural life confusing.

## Ethnocentrism

The near-automatic tendency to treat one's own culture as the normal, natural standard and to judge other cultures as deviations from it. Not the same as hatred or bigotry; it is quieter and far more common — the default setting of a mind that learned one culture first. The remedy is not to feel no reflex (that is not on offer) but to notice it and refuse to mistake it for a conclusion.

## Cultural relativism

The principle that a practice should first be understood in its own cultural context — its history, function, and meaning to insiders — before being evaluated. Best understood as a *method* ("understand before you judge"), not as a doctrine that no practice may ever be criticised. Held as method it corrects the ethnocentric reflex; misread as blanket doctrine it can silence legitimate criticism, including criticism voiced by a culture's own members.

## Emic

The insider's account of a practice: how it feels and what it means to the people who actually live it. Paired with the etic (outsider) view, it supplies the meaning that frameworks alone cannot see. An emic view without an etic one can be blind to its own patterns; both together are stronger than either alone.

## Etic

The outside analyst's account of a practice: the patterns, comparisons, and frameworks visible only from a distance. Paired with the emic (insider) view, it supplies structure and comparison. An etic view without an emic one is arrogant and thin — a chart of "dimensions" with none of the lived meaning that matters.

## High-context communication

A communication style in which much of the message lives outside the explicit words — in tone, relationship, setting, and what is deliberately left unsaid — and the listener is expected to read the context. Common, on average, in Indian and many other settings. A soft "we'll see" can be a complete and polite refusal, fully legible to another insider.

## Low-context communication

A communication style in which the message is carried in the words themselves: you say what you mean plainly, and meaning it is treated as honesty and respect. Common, on average, in German and many other settings. A blunt "no, that's wrong" is typically about the work, not the person, and is not intended as an insult.

## Collectivism

An orientation in which the basic social unit is the group — family, kin, community — and a good decision is one that honours obligations to that group; contrasted with individualism, where the basic unit is the autonomous person and choices are presumptively one's own. Cultures sit on a continuum between the two. Collectivism's cost is constrained autonomy; its gift is a net that will not let you fall and a belonging you never have to earn.

## Power distance

The degree to which a culture accepts and expects visible hierarchy — between boss and worker, teacher and student, elder and younger. In lower-power-distance settings, open disagreement with a senior is acceptable and even expected; in higher-power-distance ones, deference to seniority is itself a virtue and open contradiction can read as disrespect. A group-level tendency, not a measure of any individual.

## Face

Social standing and dignity as granted and recognised by the group; to "lose face" is to be publicly shamed or diminished. In settings where face weighs heavily, the imperative is to avoid situations that publicly embarrass anyone, which is why hard messages are best delivered privately and silence in a meeting may preserve face rather than signal agreement. Contrasted with dignity (worth felt as inner and inherent) and honour (worth tied to reputation that must be defended).

## Polychronic time

An orientation in which time is elastic and relational: several things may happen at once, plans flex around people and events, and sustaining a relationship can rightly outrank keeping to a clock. Contrasted with monochronic time, in which time is a line cut into segments, appointments are near-sacred, and a schedule is treated as a promise. Neither is discipline versus chaos; each carries its own gift (responsiveness; reliability) and its own cost (unpredictability; rigidity).

## Culture shock

The disorientation that follows the loss of all one's familiar cues for how to behave and be understood — the predictable cost of having one's invisible cultural operating system removed at once. Often described as a curve running from honeymoon through frustration to adjustment and adaptation, though the exact stages and timing are debated. Its most useful lesson: the frustration phase is a normal, usually temporary stage, not proof that one does not belong.

## Code-switching

The ordinary act of shifting one's behaviour — directness, register, body language, sometimes language itself — to fit the cultural setting one is in. A form of cultural fluency rather than fakery, comparable to a bilingual speaker moving between languages. It becomes corrosive only when forced and total: when the world demands that one hide something essential (faith, name, accent) merely to be tolerated. The tiredness of fluency and the tiredness of hiding are different things.

## Third culture

A distinct identity built by people who live between cultures: not culture A and not culture B, but a coherent third thing assembled from both, with its own blend of values, humour, and ways of living. Not a consolation prize for failing to belong "properly" anywhere, but a real identity authored deliberately from the examined and chosen parts of each inheritance — and increasingly common in a world of migration.

## Ritual

A repeated, patterned, symbolic action whose form matters beyond its practical effect — performed in set ways at set times, and legible as meaningful to insiders. Rituals need not be religious: handshakes, graduations, birthday songs, Feierabend, and a WhatsApp group's fixed Sunday call all qualify. Their characteristic work is social — marking time, changing a person's status, and binding a group — and much of that work does not depend on what any individual participant believes.

## Rite of passage

A ceremony that moves a person from one social status to another — birth, initiation, graduation, marriage, death — analysed by Arnold van Gennep as sharing a three-phase structure across cultures: separation from the old status, a liminal (threshold) middle phase, and incorporation into the new status. The skeleton appears in secular ceremonies (naturalisation, passing-out parades) as readily as in religious ones; migration itself can be read as a rite of passage with an unusually long middle phase.

## Liminality

The threshold phase of a rite of passage (from Latin *limen*, threshold): the state of being betwixt and between — no longer the old status, not yet the new. Deliberately uncomfortable in most ceremonies, since transformation requires suspension of the old identity. Some people occupy liminal positions long-term; the migrant's years of being no longer quite of the home country and not yet settled in the new one are liminality lived at the scale of a life rather than a ceremony.

## Communitas

Victor Turner's term for the intense, levelling fellowship that flares up among people in a liminal state, when ordinary structure and rank are suspended and participants meet as bare fellow humans — the feeling of the festival crowd, the pilgrimage, the stadium, the singing hall. Contrasted with "structure," the ordinary differentiated order of roles and hierarchies; societies oscillate between the two, and rituals are the main machinery for producing communitas on purpose.

## Collective effervescence

Émile Durkheim's term for the felt surge of energy and unity generated when a group assembles and acts in unison around a shared focus — singing, moving, celebrating together. Durkheim placed this shared intensity, rather than doctrine, at the root of religious life; the same current runs through secular gatherings, from Christmas markets to football stadiums, which is why festivals celebrated alone over video calls feel accurate but insufficient.

## Invented tradition

Eric Hobsbawm's term for a practice that claims or implies deep continuity with the past but was in fact constructed recently, sometimes within living memory. The point is not that such traditions are fake, but that all living tradition is continually remade by each generation — which reframes diaspora adaptations of festivals not as diminished copies of a fixed original, but as the normal ongoing editing by which traditions stay alive.

## Linguistic relativity

The idea that the language one speaks shapes thought, popularly attached to Edward Sapir and Benjamin Lee Whorf. Comes in two strengths with very different standing: the strong version (language *determines* thought; no word, no thought) is rejected by the scholarly consensus, while the weak version (language *influences* habitual attention and categorisation, modestly) remains genuinely contested, with disputed findings on colour, space, and grammatical gender. What no one disputes is that languages differ in what they make easy, obligatory, or socially expected to say.

## T–V distinction

The grammatical choice between an informal and a formal second-person pronoun — German *du* versus *Sie*, from Latin *tu*/*vos* — obliging speakers to encode the relationship in every address. Different languages map such respect systems along different lines: aap-type systems (Hindi *aap/tum/tu*) weight age and kinship heavily, while the German system weights social distance and setting, which is why transferring one map onto the other misfires in both directions.

## Gift economy

A system of exchange in which goods and services circulate as gifts — unenforced by contract yet binding through relationship — rather than as priced transactions. Marcel Mauss's analysis found three obligations at its core: to give, to receive, and to reciprocate; the "free" gift with no strings is largely a myth, and the strings are the point, weaving people into ongoing bonds. Remittances sent home read precisely as a gift economy operating at distance, with returns flowing in registers no bank records.

## Reciprocity

The return of giving, sorted by Marshall Sahlins into three modes arranged by social distance: generalized (uncounted giving as needs arise — close kin, where tallying would offend), balanced (roughly equivalent return in due course — wedding notebooks, alternating invitations, the €2.50 transfer), and negative (each side seeking to get more than it gives — the bazaar, strangers). Because the mode tracks the relationship, how people give reveals what they take the relationship to be — the key to many cross-cultural stings around bills and small debts.

## Commensality

Eating together, considered as a social institution: one of the oldest human technologies for making and marking belonging. Who eats with whom, who cooks for whom, and who eats first map a society's inclusions and exclusions — which is why food rules (veg/non-veg, halal, fasting) can carry identity, faith, and boundary at once, and why the shared or separate table at a workplace canteen is never only about lunch.
