# Citations and sources

This book uses cultural frameworks as *thinking tools*, not as settled measurements. The works below are genuinely influential and worth reading, but several are also seriously contested — a fact this book treats as part of the material, not a footnote to it. Where a framework's status is disputed, that dispute is stated in the text itself (in the counterargument and uncertainty blocks), not hidden here.

## Works cited

- **Clifford Geertz, *The Interpretation of Cultures* (Basic Books, 1973).** Source for the view of culture as "webs of significance" and for "thick description" — interpreting meaning in context rather than seeking law-like explanation. A foundational text of interpretive anthropology; also critiqued for being hard to test or generalise.

- **Franz Boas, *The Mind of Primitive Man* (1911).** Associated with the development of cultural relativism as a *method* of understanding cultures in their own terms. This book deliberately reads Boasian relativism as methodological, and flags (with Sen) the danger of inflating it into a doctrine that forbids all cross-cultural judgment.

- **Amartya Sen, *The Argumentative Indian* (2005) and *Identity and Violence* (2006).** Cited for the argument against treating "culture" as a single fixed voice and against using appeals to cultural authenticity to silence internal dissent. Used to steel-man the *limit* of relativism.

- **Kenneth Pike** (linguist). Origin of the emic/etic distinction, coined by analogy with "phonemic"/"phonetic." Used here for the insider-meaning vs outsider-pattern contrast that runs through the book.

- **Edward T. Hall, *Beyond Culture* (1976); also *The Silent Language* (1959) and *The Hidden Dimension* (1966).** Source for the high-context / low-context distinction and for cultural codes of time and space (monochronic / polychronic). Hall treated these as continua, a nuance often lost in popular retellings.

- **Geert Hofstede, *Culture's Consequences* (1980) and later editions.** Origin of the cultural-dimensions framework (power distance, individualism–collectivism, and others). Widely used *and* widely criticised — for treating nations as uniform cultures, for its original IBM-survey basis, and for methodological choices. This book uses the *dimensions* but refuses to quote specific national scores as fact.

- **Richard Nisbett and Dov Cohen, *Culture of Honor* (1996)**, and the broader dignity / honour / face literature in cultural and moral psychology. Cited for the three-way typology of where personal worth is felt to reside. Boundaries and country placements are debated; treated here as heuristics.

- **John W. Berry**, work on acculturation strategies. Source for the integration / assimilation / separation / marginalisation model and the general finding that integration is often linked to better psychological outcomes — with the important qualification that outcomes depend on whether the receiving society permits integration.

- **Sverre Lysgaard** (1950s) and later elaborations. Associated with the "U-curve" (and "W-curve" for re-entry) of cultural adjustment. Widely taught; the empirical support for fixed universal stages is contested, so this book presents it as a normalising narrative rather than a law.

- **Arnold van Gennep, *The Rites of Passage* (1909; English translation 1960).** Source of the three-phase structure of rites of passage — separation, liminality, incorporation — observed across ceremonies of birth, initiation, marriage, and death. Used in Chapter 4, including the reading of migration itself as a long liminal passage.

- **Victor Turner, *The Ritual Process: Structure and Anti-Structure* (1969).** Developed van Gennep's liminal phase into a general account of threshold states and introduced "communitas" for the levelling fellowship of people in liminality. Also the source for the observation that some persons and groups occupy liminal positions long-term.

- **Émile Durkheim, *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912).** Source for "collective effervescence" — the felt surge of unity generated by a group acting in unison around a shared focus — which Durkheim placed at the root of religious life. Applied in Chapter 4 to secular gatherings as well, as Durkheim's framework invites.

- **Eric Hobsbawm and Terence Ranger (eds.), *The Invention of Tradition* (1983).** Source for "invented tradition": practices claiming deep continuity that were in fact recently constructed. Used to argue that remaking festivals in diaspora is the normal life of tradition, not its corruption.

- **Benjamin Lee Whorf, *Language, Thought, and Reality* (ed. John B. Carroll, 1956),** with Edward Sapir's earlier writings. The textual root of "linguistic relativity." Cited with deliberate caution: the strong deterministic reading is rejected by the scholarly consensus, the weak reading remains contested, and the "Sapir-Whorf hypothesis" label is largely a later construction of the field's debate. Chapter 5 states this status in the text itself.

- **Steven Pinker, *The Language Instinct* (1994).** A forceful statement of the case against strong linguistic determinism. Cited as one pole of the debate, with the note that Pinker's strongly universalist position is itself contested.

- **Guy Deutscher, *Through the Language Glass* (2010).** An accessible survey of the weak-relativity evidence (colour, spatial frames, grammatical gender) that rejects strong Whorfianism while defending modest, real effects. Used to represent the careful middle of the debate.

- **Penelope Brown and Stephen C. Levinson, *Politeness: Universals in Language Usage* (1987),** with the classic sociolinguistic literature on pronouns of "power and solidarity" associated with Roger Brown and colleagues. Background for Chapter 5's treatment of the T–V distinction and respect systems; the universality claims are themselves debated.

- **Shana Poplack and Carol Myers-Scotton** (sociolinguists). Associated with the mainstream finding that code-switching is rule-governed, socially precise skill rather than deficit — demonstrated by both of their competing grammatical models. No specific study results are asserted.

- **Marcel Mauss, *The Gift* (*Essai sur le don*, 1925; English translations 1954 and later).** Foundation of the anthropology of exchange: the obligations to give, receive, and reciprocate, and the gift as social fabric rather than free transfer. Chapter 6 also keeps Mauss's own clear-eyed note that gift systems carry compulsion as well as solidarity.

- **Marshall Sahlins, *Stone Age Economics* (1972).** Source of the generalized / balanced / negative reciprocity typology (in "On the Sociology of Primitive Exchange") and of the observation that the mode of exchange tracks social distance — the key Chapter 6 applies to bills, small debts, and remittances.

- **Mary Douglas, *Purity and Danger* (1966)** and the essay "Deciphering a Meal" (1972). Source for the argument that food rules and meal structures encode a community's categories of order, belonging, and pollution — Chapter 6's frame for food as identity and boundary.

## A note on verification

Titles, authors, and the broad ideas above are cited in good faith as canonical and widely known. Publication years and editions are given from memory and general knowledge and should be **verified against the actual editions before any reuse, quotation, or republication** — do not treat a date or edition here as authoritative. No page numbers, quotations of exact wording, statistics, or specific country scores are asserted as fact anywhere in this book; where a number would have strengthened a claim, the text instead flags it with "citation needed — verify before publishing." If you extend this book, keep that standard: cite only what you can genuinely stand behind, and mark honestly what you cannot.
