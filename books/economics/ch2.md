# 2. The Mind Is a Bad Accountant

The economics of Chapter 1 assumed a clear-headed chooser weighing costs and benefits. This chapter is about the actual chooser: a tired human on a train, running on a mind that was built for a world without wages, invoices, or WhatsApp requests for money. That mind is astonishing at many things. Keeping a fair ledger is not one of them. The field that studies its predictable errors is behavioural economics, and its central lesson is oddly freeing: your worst money mistakes are not signs that you are bad with money. They are the standard-issue bugs of a normal brain, and once you can name them, you can plan around them.

## 2.1 Losses loom larger than gains

Start with the single most important finding in the field. In the 1970s Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed that people do not feel gains and losses on the same scale: the pain of losing a given amount is roughly stronger than the pleasure of gaining the same amount.[^prospect] This is :::glossary term="Loss aversion":::, and it quietly bends dozens of everyday choices.

:::definition term="Loss aversion"
The tendency to feel the pain of a loss more intensely than the pleasure of an equivalent gain, so that avoiding a loss motivates us more than acquiring a same-sized gain. A consequence is that whether an option feels like a "loss" or a "gain" depends on the reference point we compare it to — and that reference point can be shifted by how the choice is framed.
:::

Loss aversion is why a €50 fee for cancelling a plan stings far more than a €50 discount pleases; why we hold a failing investment or a dying side-hustle long past sense, because selling *realises* the loss and makes it real; why "don't lose your progress" motivates better than "make progress." None of this is irrational in isolation — an organism that treated threats and opportunities symmetrically would not have lasted long. But in a money life it produces a systematic bias toward the status quo and toward whatever is framed as protecting what you already have. Notice, too, how easily the reference point moves: a shift that pays €90 feels like a gain if you expected nothing and a loss if you had mentally already spent €120.

## 2.2 Money in labelled jars

The second bug is that we do not treat money as what it is — fungible, every euro identical to every other — but as if it lived in separate labelled jars. The economist Richard Thaler called this :::glossary term="Mental accounting":::.[^thaler]

:::definition term="Mental accounting"
The habit of sorting money into mental categories — "rent money," "fun money," "the tax refund," "money sent from home" — and treating the categories as if they obeyed different rules, even though a euro is a euro wherever it sits. Mental accounting can help (it is a crude budget) or hurt (it leads to holding cash in a low-interest "savings" jar while carrying high-interest debt in another).
:::

Mental accounting explains behaviour that is otherwise baffling. A person will splurge a tax refund or a bonus on something they would never buy with "normal" wages, because it is filed as "found money" rather than as what it plainly is — income, identical to the rest. The same person may keep €500 sitting in a current account earning nothing while paying 15% interest on a credit-card balance, because one jar says "my safety savings, don't touch" and another says "the card, deal with it later." Treated as one pool, the move is obvious: the €500 should crush the debt. Treated as separate jars, the obvious move is invisible. The labels are not neutral; they are quietly making decisions for you.

There is a kinder side, though, and Chapter 3 will use it: because the mind insists on jars anyway, you can *design useful jars* — a separate account the wages never touch — and let the same bias that traps people work for you instead.

:::check reread="2.2"
question: You receive a €600 tax refund and, the same week, are carrying a €600 balance on a credit card at 18% interest while €600 also sits idle in your current account. Treating money as fungible, what does the reasoning recommend?
- Spend the refund on something nice — it is "extra" money you were not counting on.
- Keep the refund as separate emergency savings and leave the card balance as it is.
- Recognise that all these euros are interchangeable, and use available money to clear the 18% debt, since paying it off is a guaranteed 18% "return."
- Split the refund evenly across every jar so each category grows a little.
answer: 3
explanation: A euro is a euro regardless of which mental jar it arrives in; the labels "refund," "savings," and "the card" are stories, not economic facts. Because clearing an 18% debt is equivalent to earning a guaranteed 18%, using idle money against it beats almost any alternative. Options 1 and 2 are mental accounting steering the decision — the "found money" and "don't touch savings" labels hiding the obvious move.
:::

## 2.3 The tug of now

The third bug is about time. Ask people whether they would prefer €100 today or €110 in a week, and many take the €100; ask whether they would prefer €100 in a year or €110 in a year and a week, and the same people happily wait the extra week. The one-week delay is identical in both cases — but when "now" is one of the options, it exerts a special pull. Economists call this :::glossary term="Present bias":::, or hyperbolic discounting.[^thaler]

:::definition term="Present bias"
The tendency to weight immediate rewards far more heavily than future ones, and to discount the near future much more steeply than the distant future. Present bias is why plans made for "next month me" — who will surely save, study, and go to bed early — are so reliably overturned when next month becomes today and the sofa is right there.
:::

Present bias is the engine behind almost every gap between intention and behaviour with money. You genuinely intend to save; you genuinely intend to start the assignment; the future self who benefits is real but abstract, and the present self who would have to pay is vivid and tired. The deep point is that this is not a war between a wise you and a weak you. It is two versions of you with genuinely different preferences, and the present one keeps winning because it is the one holding the phone. The reason "just have more willpower" fails as advice is that it asks the present self to defeat itself in single combat, every day, forever. The reliable fixes work differently: they let the rested, future-minded you *bind* the tired, present-minded you in advance — automatic transfers, cancelled subscriptions, food bought before hunger — so the decision is already made when the weak moment arrives.

## 2.4 The number that was there first

The fourth bug is :::glossary term="Anchoring":::: our estimates are pulled toward whatever number happened to be in front of us, even an irrelevant one.

:::definition term="Anchoring"
The tendency for a judgement or estimate to be dragged toward a reference number encountered beforehand — a "was €80, now €50" tag, an opening salary offer, a suggested tip — even when that number carries no real information about the thing's value.
:::

Anchoring is why "was €80, now €50" sells things you would never have paid €50 for cold: the €80 is an anchor, and the point of the anchor is to make €50 feel like a €30 gain rather than a €50 spend. It is why the first salary figure named in a negotiation exerts such gravity on the final number, and why "recommended" tip buttons of 18/22/25% drag the whole table upward. The defence is not to be cleverer in the moment — anchoring works even on people who know about it — but to arrive with your *own* number. Decide what a thing is worth to you, or what your labour is worth, before you see the seller's or employer's figure. An anchor only moves you if you had nowhere else to stand.

## 2.5 The feelings folded into the euros

None of these bugs runs in a vacuum. They run inside a life with a family, a culture, and a reputation — and money carries feelings that the textbook leaves out. For this book's readers three loom especially large.

The first is **guilt and status**. Spending on yourself can feel like a small betrayal when others sacrificed for you to be here; spending on visible things — a phone, a watch, a round of drinks, a car photographed for home — can feel like a duty, proof to distant eyes that the migration "worked." Both are loss aversion and anchoring wearing cultural clothes: the reference point against which you feel rich or poor is set by others, and the loss you are avoiding is the loss of face.

The second is **remittance pressure** — the expectation, sometimes spoken and sometimes not, of sending money home. This deserves its own honest treatment, and gets one in Chapter 3. Here the only point is psychological: money labelled "for family" sits in a jar that feels untouchable, and rightly carries real love and obligation — but the jar can also quietly grow until the person earning it has budgeted nothing for their own future, and no one has decided that on purpose.

The third is that these pressures fall **differently on men and women, and heavily on both**. A man may carry an unspoken script that his worth is measured by what he provides and sends, that asking for help is failure, that visible success is owed to the family that invested in him. A woman may carry a script that her money is provisional or secondary, that saving for her own independence looks like disloyalty, that spending on herself needs an excuse a brother would not be asked for — while she is often also expected to do the unpaid caring work that has no line in any budget. These scripts are different, and each is a real weight; neither is the "harder" one, and this book takes both seriously rather than ranking them.

:::argument title="The case for naming the feeling before doing the sum"
conclusion: Before making a significant money decision, you should name the emotion and the expectation attached to it, out loud or on paper.
premise: Money decisions are shaped by loss aversion, mental accounting, present bias, and anchoring, which operate below awareness and are amplified by guilt, status, and family expectation.
premise: A bias you have named loses much of its grip, because naming moves the decision from automatic reaction to deliberate choice.
premise: Naming the expectation does not oblige you to defy it — you may still send the money, buy the gift, take the shift — but it lets you do so as a decision rather than a reflex.
:::

:::counter title="Objection: over-examining every euro is its own dysfunction" to="The case for naming the feeling before doing the sum"
The honest objection is that a life in which every purchase is interrogated for hidden bias is exhausting and a little joyless, and can tip into anxiety or scrupulosity — turning money, which should mostly be a background tool, into a foreground obsession. There is also something cold in analysing away a gift to your parents as "mental accounting"; some of what looks like bias is just love, and love does not have to clear a rationality test. The reasonable version of the argument confines the naming to *significant, recurring* decisions — the standing remittance, the big purchase, the debt — and lets the small ones be. Reserve the accountant for the accounts that matter; do not audit a birthday present.
:::

:::check reread="2.3"
question: A friend says: "I keep planning to save, but every payday something comes up and I spend it — I just need more discipline." Which response best reflects this chapter?
- They are right; saving is fundamentally a matter of willpower, and they should try harder each payday.
- The problem is likely present bias, and the fix is to remove the payday decision — e.g. an automatic transfer to a separate account the moment wages arrive — rather than to win a willpower fight monthly.
- They should feel guilty, since spending instead of saving is a moral failing.
- Saving is impossible on a low wage, so there is nothing to be done.
answer: 2
explanation: Present bias means the tired present self reliably overrides the plans of the future self, so advice that asks the present self to out-discipline itself every payday tends to fail. The robust fix binds the decision in advance — automating the transfer so the money is moved before it can be spent. That is a structural fix, not a character verdict.
:::

:::reflect
Pick one money decision you have made recently that you are uneasy about. Write down, honestly, which of the four biases in this chapter — loss aversion, mental accounting, present bias, anchoring — was at work, and which feeling (guilt, status, family expectation) was folded in with it. Then write what you would decide now, having named both. Do not resolve to make the "correct" choice; resolve only to make it deliberately.
:::

The point of this chapter is not to make you distrust yourself. It is the opposite. A bad accountant who knows he is a bad accountant can hire a system — checklists, automatic transfers, decisions made in advance — that a confident one never would. You do not need to become a coldly rational calculator; nobody is one, and the people selling you things are counting on you believing you are. You need only to know the specific ways the mind mis-keeps its books, so that when it matters you can quietly override it. Chapter 3 is that system.

[^prospect]: Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's prospect theory (1979), and Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011), are the sources for loss aversion, reference points, and framing.
[^thaler]: Richard Thaler, *Misbehaving: The Making of Behavioural Economics* (2015), and Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein, *Nudge* (2008), are the sources for mental accounting, present bias / hyperbolic discounting, and the design of "choice architecture" that binds the present self.
