# 3. Foundations, Not Advice

Chapter 1 gave you the two numbers; Chapter 2 gave you the bugs. This chapter builds the system — the small set of foundations that let a normal, biased, stretched person handle money without either becoming an obsessive or being quietly robbed by their own habits. A warning about what this chapter is *not*, before anything else, because it matters more than any technique in it.

## 3.1 A necessary disclaimer

I am not a financial advisor, and this is not financial advice. Nothing here is a recommendation to buy, sell, save, borrow, or send any particular amount, or to use any particular product. This is *education*: an explanation of how a few well-established ideas work, so that you can think more clearly and ask better questions. The specifics that would turn any of it into a real plan — your income, your visa status, your debts, your family's needs, and above all the tax, banking, and benefit rules of the particular country you live in — vary enormously from person to person and from one country to the next, and they change over time. For anything consequential, check the rules that apply to *you*, ideally with a qualified, independent professional who has no product to sell you. Read this chapter as a map of the terrain, not directions to your house.

With that firmly in place, the terrain.

## 3.2 Budgeting is just Chapter 1, written down

A budget has a bad reputation as an instrument of self-denial. It is really the opposite: it is Chapter 1's opportunity cost made visible, so that money goes where *you* decided rather than where it drifts. The mechanism that makes budgets fail is Chapter 2's present bias — the plan made by the rested self, overturned by the tired self at the point of spending. So the only budgets that survive contact with real life are the ones that need almost no willpower in the moment.

That usually means *paying yourself first and automating it*: the day wages arrive, a fixed amount moves — by standing order, before you can feel it — into a separate account you have mentally labelled "not for spending." This deliberately weaponises the mental accounting from Chapter 2. The jar is a bias; here you build the jar on purpose, on the side that helps you. What remains in the everyday account is, by construction, spendable, so you are freed from policing each purchase. You do not need the perfect percentage split that finance blogs argue over; you need a split you will actually keep. This is a place for :::glossary term="Satisficing":::.

:::definition term="Satisficing"
Choosing an option that is good enough to meet your real needs, rather than exhaustively searching for the theoretically optimal one. Coined by Herbert Simon, the idea is that under limited time and attention, "good enough, and decided" reliably beats "perfect, and endlessly deferred." A budget you will keep at 70% effort beats an ideal one you abandon in three weeks.
:::

The good-enough budget is not a compromise forced on you by poverty. It is the correct response to the scarcity of attention itself — the same bandwidth tax from Chapter 1. A plan simple enough to run while exhausted is not a lesser plan; for a working student it is the only kind that works.

## 3.3 The buffer between you and a bad week

Before saving *toward* anything, most explanations of personal finance put one thing first: a small pool of ready money kept for emergencies. Call it an :::glossary term="Emergency buffer":::.

:::definition term="Emergency buffer"
A modest amount of easily accessible money set aside specifically to absorb unexpected shocks — a lost shift, a broken laptop the degree depends on, an urgent flight home — without resorting to high-interest debt. Its job is not to earn returns; it is to keep one bad week from becoming a spiral of borrowing.
:::

The buffer's whole value is behavioural and structural, not mathematical. Without it, an unexpected €300 expense becomes €300 on a credit card at punishing interest, and Chapter 1's scarcity mindset switches on — the bandwidth tax, the tunnelling, the worse decisions that follow from being short. With even a small buffer, the same shock is a nuisance, not a crisis, and your judgement stays intact. This is why the buffer usually comes *before* investing or extra debt repayment in most explanations: it is the thing that stops a single event from undoing everything else. How large it should be is exactly the kind of personal, country-specific number this book will not hand you — it depends on your rent, your job security, your visa, and what a genuine emergency would cost where you live.

:::uncertainty title="How big should an emergency buffer be?"
Popular personal-finance sources often quote a target of "three to six months of essential expenses," and sometimes other figures. This book will not assert any specific number as correct, because the right size depends heavily on individual circumstances — income stability, immigration status, family obligations, local costs — and because the empirical basis for any one figure over another is not something I can verify here. Citation needed — verify before publishing. Treat the *existence* of a buffer as the well-supported idea, and its exact size as a personal judgement to work out for your own situation.
:::

## 3.4 Compound interest, in both directions

The one piece of arithmetic worth internalising is :::glossary term="Compound interest":::, because it is the mechanism behind both the slow building of wealth and the fast building of debt — the same force, pointed either way.

:::definition term="Compound interest"
Interest calculated not only on the original sum but also on the interest already added, so that the amount grows on itself. Over time this produces acceleration rather than a straight line: the growth feeds the growth. Working for you (on savings or investments), it is the strongest ally a patient saver has; working against you (on debt), it is the reason unpaid balances swell alarmingly.

The two things that most control the result are the *rate* and, above all, the *time* the money is left to compound — which is why starting small and early can matter more than starting large and late.
:::

Pointed at savings, compounding rewards *time* more than *amount*, which is genuinely good news for someone young and short of cash: small sums left alone for a long time can outgrow larger sums started late, because the early money has more years to compound on itself. Pointed at debt, the same mechanism is why a credit-card balance left unpaid does not grow in a gentle line but accelerates, and why the "minimum payment" can leave you paying for years. Understanding this one idea reframes Chapter 2's example: paying off an 18% debt really is equivalent to earning a guaranteed 18% compounding return, which almost no ordinary investment reliably beats. I will not put numeric growth examples here, because the outcome depends entirely on rates and periods that vary by product and country; the shape of the idea — acceleration, and the primacy of time — is the durable part.

## 3.5 Good debt, bad debt, and the honest middle

"Debt" is not one thing, and treating it as uniformly shameful is as much an error as treating it as harmless. A workable distinction, offered as a lens rather than a rule:

*Bad debt* is borrowing at high interest to pay for consumption that does not increase your future earning power or hold its value — a financed phone, a rolled-over credit-card balance, most "buy now, pay later," a high-interest loan for a holiday. Here compound interest works against you on something that is only shrinking in value. *Good debt*, if the phrase is to mean anything, is borrowing at a manageable rate to fund something with a real chance of raising your future income or wellbeing by more than it costs — the textbook case being education, or sometimes a tool or qualification that pays for itself. Even "good" debt is not automatically good: a degree bought with expensive debt into a field with poor prospects can be a bad trade, and the honest treatment steel-mans the sceptic here.

:::argument title="The case for treating education debt as an investment, not a burden"
conclusion: Debt taken on to fund education or a genuine qualification should be evaluated as an investment — by comparing its total cost against the future earnings and options it plausibly creates — rather than avoided on principle as "debt."
premise: Some spending increases your future earning power, and borrowing that lets you make such spending now can be worth more than it costs.
premise: Education and recognised qualifications are, for many people, among the more reliable ways to raise lifetime earnings and open options that would otherwise stay closed.
premise: A cost that is exceeded by the value it creates is a good trade regardless of whether it is labelled "debt," so refusing all debt on principle can mean refusing worthwhile investments.
:::

:::counter title="Objection: 'good debt' is a story that has ruined careful people" to="The case for treating education debt as an investment, not a burden"
The strongest reply is that "it's an investment in yourself" is exactly the reassuring phrase used to sell debt that does not pay off. The future earnings are a *forecast*, not a fact; the debt is certain and the payoff is not. Fields get saturated, credentials get devalued, health and immigration status intervene, and the interest compounds regardless of whether the degree delivered. Many diligent people followed the "good debt" logic into qualifications that never produced the promised income, and were then told their misfortune was a personal failing. The honest position keeps the framework but demands humility about the forecast: treat the future earnings as an uncertain estimate, borrow at the lowest rate you genuinely can, and keep the amount recoverable if the bet does not land. "Investment" is a reason to analyse the trade carefully, never a reason to skip the analysis.
:::

## 3.6 Sending money home, honestly

Finally, the money that flows from this book's readers back to families — a :::glossary term="Remittance":::. The economist Amartya Sen's work reframes what money is even *for*: not an end in itself but a means to *capabilities* — real freedoms to do and be things you have reason to value.[^sen] Money sent home can buy immense capability: a sibling's schooling, a parent's medicine, a roof repaired. It is often among the most valuable uses to which a wage can be put, and nothing here argues against it.

But the same Sen-shaped question — *whose* capabilities, over *what* time horizon — has to be asked honestly, and the honest answer includes *your own future self* as a real person with real needs, not an afterthought. A remittance is Chapter 2's mental accounting and loss aversion at full strength: the "for family" jar feels sacred, and reducing it feels like an unbearable loss of face and love. That is exactly why it is the decision most worth moving out of the tunnel and deciding deliberately, when rested — how much, on what schedule, alongside your own buffer and future — rather than sending whatever is asked in whatever amount the moment demands, until nothing is left for the person doing the earning.

And this pressure, once more, falls on men and women differently and heavily on both. A son may face an unspoken expectation that providing is his identity and that keeping anything back is selfishness or failure. A daughter may face an expectation that her earning is secondary, that saving for her own independence reads as disloyalty, or that her contribution is owed while her own future is no one's priority — even as she is also counted on for unpaid care. Neither script is lighter than the other. The economics cannot tell you the right amount to send; that is a question of love and duty, not arithmetic, and this book will not pretend otherwise. What it can say is that sending money and building your own foundation are not enemies unless scarcity forces them to be — and that deciding both on purpose, out loud, is the whole of the good-enough approach.

That is the whole system, and it is deliberately small: see the second number, distrust the accountant, automate the plan, keep a buffer, respect compounding in both directions, analyse debt honestly, and decide the money you send with the same care you would want someone to spend on you. None of it requires you to be rich, disciplined, or good at maths. It requires only that you make the important decisions once, while rested, and let the tired version of you follow the plan. Given real scarcity, that is what a good-enough life actually looks like — and good enough, decided and kept, is the quiet victory the whole of this book has been pointing at.

[^sen]: Amartya Sen, *Development as Freedom* (1999), is the source for the "capability approach" — the idea that the point of income is the real freedoms (capabilities) it secures, not money as an end in itself.
