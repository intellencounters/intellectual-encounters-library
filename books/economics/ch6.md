# 6. Risk, Time, and the Long Game

Every chapter has ended by handing the decision back to you, because the honest ones depended on specifics no book can know. This final chapter is about the deepest reason for that humility: you are deciding about a future that does not exist yet. The visa renewal, the job market in five years, your health, your parents' health, which skills survive automation — none of it is knowable, and all of it must be decided anyway. Economics cannot remove the uncertainty. What it offers is a small set of tools for choosing *well under* it — and a clear view of where those tools break.

First, the disclaimer from Chapter 3, restated because it binds hardest here: this is not financial advice. Nothing in this chapter recommends any investment, insurance product, or financial decision. It is education about thinking under uncertainty, and every consequential application depends on personal and country-specific facts you must check, ideally with a qualified, independent professional.

## 6.1 The average of futures that never happen

The workhorse tool for uncertain choices is :::glossary term="Expected value":::: list what could happen, weight each outcome by its probability, add.

:::definition term="Expected value"
The probability-weighted average of an uncertain prospect's outcomes: each possible result multiplied by its chance of occurring, summed. A ticket paying €100 with probability one-in-ten has an expected value of €10. The standard first tool for comparing gambles — whose limits matter as much as its uses.
:::

Expected value already improves everyday reasoning. It is why a lottery ticket is a bad purchase *as economics* (its expected value sits far below its price; you are paying for the daydream), and why an application with a 10% chance at a life-changing job can be worth an evening though it will probably fail. Expected value normalises worthwhile failure: nine rejected applications are not evidence the tenth was irrational.

But expected value has a famous crack, found early. In the eighteenth century, Daniel Bernoulli studied a coin-flip game whose expected value is, on paper, infinite — yet no sane person would pay more than a few coins to play.[^bernoulli] His diagnosis rebuilt the foundations: what matters is not the euro amount but its *usefulness to you*, which shrinks as you have more euros. The €500 that is a nuisance to a wealthy family is a catastrophe to a student a week before rent. Three corollaries organise this chapter. Averages mislead when outcomes are extreme: a small chance of ruin is not "priced in" by a good average, because ruin ends the game. Identical gambles are different decisions for different people: risk is relative to your buffer, your obligations, your visa. And avoiding catastrophic downside is worth paying for — the entire logic of insurance.

:::check reread="6.1"
question: A gamble offers a 99% chance of winning €200 and a 1% chance of losing €10,000 — a positive expected value (€98). For a student with €2,000 of total savings, what does this chapter say about "take every positive-expected-value bet"?
- Take it — positive expected value settles the question for everyone.
- Refuse it — expected value is a useless concept.
- The rule fails here: the 1% outcome is ruinous for this person, and a small chance of ruin is not compensated by a good average, because ruin ends the ability to keep playing. The same bet might be reasonable for someone who could absorb the loss.
- The maths must be wrong, since positive-expected-value bets cannot involve losses.
answer: 3
explanation: Bernoulli's insight is that outcomes matter in usefulness, not raw euros: a €10,000 loss against €2,000 of savings is catastrophe, not five times a €2,000 loss. You live only one of the averaged futures, and a ruinous branch cannot be offset by pleasant branches you never reach. Risk is relative to the decider's situation, which is why identical gambles are different decisions for different people.
:::

## 6.2 Don't carry your life in one basket

If ruin is what to avoid, the first defence is :::glossary term="Diversification":::: arranging your affairs so no single failure takes everything down at once.

:::definition term="Diversification"
Spreading exposure across multiple things unlikely to fail together, so that no single event is ruinous. In finance, not concentrating wealth in one asset; as a life principle, wherever failures can be made independent — skills, income sources, networks, documents, plans. Its price: never doing as well as the single best pick would have. Its payoff: surviving the worst one.
:::

The financial version is famous — "all my savings in one company's shares" alarms any advisor, especially when the company is also your employer, so one bankruptcy takes job and savings in the same week. But the principle is larger than portfolios; for a working student it is mostly not about assets. A skill set that only fits one employer's systems is concentrated; one with a transferable core travels. An income that is one shift job is one scheduling conflict from zero; the same hours with even a small second stream — tutoring, a freelance skill — fail differently. A social world entirely inside one workplace or WhatsApp group concentrates your belonging in one place that can sour; friendships across circles fail independently. Even documents can be diversified — ask anyone who lost the phone holding the only copy of everything the Ausländerbehörde wants. The principle always has a cost: the diversified life is never optimal in the best case — two income streams grow slower than one obsession, and the person who bet everything on one skill and won looks like a genius. Diversification is insurance paid in foregone upside, which is the next idea.

## 6.3 Why insurance feels like waste until it isn't

Insurance may be the most resented purchase in a stretched budget: money leaving monthly for, apparently, nothing. The economics says the "nothing" is the product working; the mechanism is :::glossary term="Risk pooling":::.

:::definition term="Risk pooling"
The arrangement by which many people each pay a small, certain amount into a common pool, from which the few who suffer a large loss are paid. It works because misfortunes are unpredictable one by one but stable in aggregate — no one knows *who* will be hit, while the pool can estimate *how many* — converting a small chance of ruin for each into a small certain cost for all. The founding logic of insurance and, writ large, of the welfare state.
:::

Seen through Section 6.1, insurance is a bet with *negative* expected value that is nonetheless rational: on average you pay in more than you take out, but you are not buying euros back on average — you are deleting the ruinous branch of the future. The premium is the fee for making Bernoulli's catastrophe impossible. A useful heuristic follows, as a way of thinking and not advice about any product: insurance earns its cost against losses you could not absorb, and loses its point against losses you comfortably could — why insuring a phone screen is questionable while liability insurance, beloved of every German, guards against the accident that would otherwise follow you for decades. It also reframes the years you pay in and claim nothing: not waste but being one of the pool's lucky members this year, holding up someone whose year went otherwise — a role you have already played in an extended family; a Versicherung is the same logic with actuaries.

## 6.4 Doors that close: optionality and the irreversible

Uncertainty also changes how to weigh *time* in decisions — the difference between choices you can undo and choices you cannot. Keeping futures open has a name: :::glossary term="Optionality":::.

:::definition term="Optionality"
The state of holding options you are not obliged to exercise: choices kept open, capacity in reserve, decisions kept reversible. Under uncertainty, the right to decide later — after more is known — has real value, so a reversible path can be worth choosing over an irreversible one that looks somewhat better today.
:::

:::argument title="The case for paying for open doors"
conclusion: Under high uncertainty, it is often rational to accept a worse expected outcome today in exchange for more open options tomorrow.
premise: The value of a choice depends on information, and information arrives with time — next year you will know things about your field, visa, and family that you cannot know today.
premise: Reversible choices let you use that future information, while irreversible ones lock in today's ignorance.
premise: The right to decide later with better information is therefore genuinely valuable, and giving it up is a real cost that today's comparison omits.
:::

:::counter title="Objection: optionality hoarding is how lives go unlived" to="The case for paying for open doors"
The strongest reply is that options expire, cost rent, and can become a way of hiding. Every year of "keeping doors open" is a year of not walking through any of them, and many of life's best things — a marriage, a child, a homeland recommitted to, a craft mastered — are only available as irreversible commitments; optimising for exit forfeits the goods that require *not* having one. The reader postponing every commitment "until things are more certain" should hear it plainly: certainty never comes, and permanent postponement is itself a default choice with its own enormous invisible cost. The honest synthesis is not "maximise options" but "spend them deliberately": hold options while genuinely learning, then close them on purpose.
:::

The discipline: sort choices by reversibility before agonising over them. Most — a course, a side project, a city within the Schengen area, most jobs — are more reversible than they feel at 2 a.m., and deserve quick, satisficing treatment. A few — abandoning a degree near completion, surrendering a residence status, marriage, a child — genuinely close doors, and deserve the full apparatus: rested deliberation, the second number, named biases, steel-manned counterarguments.

## 6.5 Compounding beyond money, and one honest caveat

Chapter 3 introduced compound interest as arithmetic. Its final form is a way of seeing time. Skills compound: each thing learned makes adjacent things cheaper to learn, which is why the tenth month of German or Python moves faster than the first, and an hour of learning at twenty-seven can outweigh one at fifty-seven — more years to earn interest on itself. Reputation compounds: every kept promise makes the next introduction easier, and trust built over years arrives exactly when you need the reference or the warning. Health compounds in both directions, the sleep debt and the exercise habit each feeding on themselves. None of this is mystical; it is Chapter 3's acceleration applied to assets no bank tracks — carrying Chapter 2's warning label, since present bias is precisely the bug that makes compounding invisible: the early payments are small and the acceleration arrives years after the choice.

One caveat belongs here, stated carefully because it is easy to overclaim. The expected-value tradition averages across possible futures as though you could experience them all; but you live one life, in sequence, and a run of losses you do not survive is not redeemed by the branches where you won. Recent researchers have pressed this old point under the banner of :::glossary term="Ergodicity":::, arguing that many textbook conclusions quietly assume the average across futures equals the average along one lifetime — which, for many real gambles, it does not.

:::uncertainty title="How far does the ergodicity critique reach?"
That sequence and survival matter — that a ruinous branch cannot be averaged away — is old, sound, and already reflected in Bernoulli's fix. How much further the modern "ergodicity economics" programme overturns standard decision theory is genuinely contested, and this book will not adjudicate it; nor can it vouch for specific claimed results in that literature: citation needed — verify before publishing. The modest version is safe: averages flatter gambles that can end you, so protect survival first and optimise second. The strong version — that expected-utility economics is broadly built on an error — remains an open dispute.
:::

:::check reread="6.4"
question: A reader concludes from this chapter: "Optionality is valuable, so I should keep all my options open indefinitely — no point committing to a specialisation, a partner, or a country until the uncertainty resolves." What is the best assessment?
- Correct — the chapter shows that more optionality is always better.
- Incorrect — the chapter shows options are worthless and everything should be committed to immediately.
- Partly wrong: options have value but also expire and cost rent, some of life's central goods exist only inside irreversible commitments, and the uncertainty will never fully resolve — so the discipline is to hold options while genuinely learning, then close them deliberately.
- Correct for careers but not relationships.
answer: 3
explanation: The chapter's counterargument targets exactly this reading. Optionality justifies paying to keep doors open under uncertainty, but permanent postponement is itself a default choice with large invisible costs, and waiting for certainty means waiting forever. The synthesis: keep reversible things quick, reserve deep deliberation for the truly irreversible, and close doors on purpose rather than never.
:::

## 6.6 An honest close

:::reflect
This book opened by asking you to see the second number. Close it by writing three sentences to revisit in five years. One: the single ruinous risk in your life you most need to defuse (one income, one document, one country's rules, one relationship carrying all your belonging). Two: the one compounding investment — a skill, a practice, a relationship — you commit to feeding small amounts on a schedule, starting this month. Three: one door you have been "keeping open" that you now either commit to walking through by a written date, or close on purpose. Save the answers with your earlier reflections; the comparison, years from now, is the real final exam.
:::

Here is what six chapters amount to, said without decoration. You are managing scarce time and energy with a mind that mis-keeps its own books, inside labour markets that price your hour by rules you did not choose, between two countries whose gap in rules is the largest economic fact of your life, under uncertainty that never fully lifts. Nothing there is reason for despair; every clause came with a tool. See the second number. Distrust the accountant and automate around him. Know what your wage is actually paying for, and finish the credential. Judge nations' rules, and your own position, with the honesty of someone who has lived under two systems. Protect survival first; diversify what you cannot afford to lose; pool what you cannot carry alone; spend your options on purpose; and feed the things that compound.

What none of this supplies is what economics is sometimes accused of pretending to supply: what any of it is *for*. The discipline can price the shift, but not tell you whether the degree, the remittance, the marriage, or the return ticket is the life you want; it can keep you from ruin, but not tell you what to build in the safety it buys. Those questions belong to you, the people you love, and older conversations than this one. What a stretched, tired, capable person can fairly ask of economics is what this book has tried to give: that when you finally sit down — rested, biases named, both numbers visible — to decide what your one sequential, uninsurable, compounding life is for, at least the accounting will be honest. The rest was never the economist's to answer. It was always yours.

[^bernoulli]: Daniel Bernoulli's 1738 essay, translated as "Exposition of a New Theory on the Measurement of Risk" (*Econometrica*, 1954), analyses the St. Petersburg paradox and introduces the idea that money's usefulness diminishes as wealth grows — the founding document of expected-utility thinking. Peter Bernstein's *Against the Gods* (1996) tells the broader history of risk accessibly.
