# 1. Scarcity Is the Whole Game

It is a Tuesday in exam week. Your manager messages the shift group at nine in the morning: someone has called in sick, can anyone cover 16:00 to midnight? The pay is real. So is the assignment due Thursday. You have maybe four usable hours of concentration left in you today, and the shift will eat them. You reply "yes" before you have finished the thought, because the money is concrete and the assignment is not yet on fire. That small decision — made in a second, on a train, half-asleep — is a complete economics problem. This chapter is about what was really being decided.

## 1.1 The one word behind every economic idea

People assume economics is about money. It is not, at bottom. It is about **scarcity**: the plain fact that you cannot have everything you want, because the things you want to do all draw on the same limited stock of time, energy, attention, and cash. The most quoted definition of the field, from Lionel Robbins in 1932, calls economics the study of human behaviour "as a relationship between ends and scarce means which have alternative uses."[^robbins] Strip away the formality and it says something you already know in your body during exam week: your hours have *alternative uses*, and using them one way spends them for every other.

:::definition term="Scarcity"
The condition in which the means available — time, money, attention, energy — are limited relative to the ends they could be put toward. Scarcity is not the same as poverty; a billionaire faces scarcity of time, and a student on minimum wage faces scarcity of both time and money. What makes something an economic problem is not that it is expensive, but that spending a resource on it forecloses other uses.
:::

For a working student, the binding scarcity is usually not money — money can, in principle, be earned. It is time, and the narrower resource inside time: hours in which your mind actually works. You have a fixed number of those per day, they do not roll over, and no one can lend you theirs. Once you see that time is the true budget, the rest of this chapter follows.

## 1.2 Opportunity cost: what a shift really costs

Ask what the covered shift "cost" you and the obvious answer is: nothing — you *earned* money, you did not spend it. But that is the accountant's answer, and it is wrong in the way that matters. The economist's answer is that the cost of anything is what you gave up to get it: its :::glossary term="Opportunity cost":::.

:::definition term="Opportunity cost"
The value of the best alternative you did not choose. Every choice has one, because choosing to do a thing is simultaneously choosing not to do everything else you could have done with those same resources. The opportunity cost of a euro spent is the most useful other thing that euro could have bought; the opportunity cost of an hour worked is the most valuable other thing that hour could have held.
:::

The cost of that eight-hour shift was not zero. It was the four hours of clear-headed study it displaced, plus the tired hours afterwards, plus a slightly worse exam, plus whatever that worse exam does to the degree that is the whole reason you are in this country working nights. Set the shift's pay against *that*, not against zero, and the decision looks different. Sometimes the shift still wins — rent is not optional, and one assignment is not a career. But you cannot know that until you price the thing you actually gave up.

This is not an argument for never taking the shift. It is an argument for seeing the second number. Most bad decisions under pressure are not made by choosing wrongly between two visible options; they are made by comparing one visible option against a phantom of "nothing," because the alternative was invisible in the moment.

:::check reread="1.2"
question: You skip a friend's birthday dinner to pick up an overtime shift that pays €90. Which figure best represents the shift's opportunity cost?
- €0 — you earned money rather than spending it, so there is no cost.
- €90 — the amount the shift paid you.
- The value to you of the evening with your friend (plus any rest or study it held) that you gave up.
- The cost of the dinner you would have paid for had you attended.
answer: 3
explanation: Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative forgone — here, the evening you gave up, in whatever terms matter to you. The €90 is the shift's benefit, not its cost; the dinner's price is a separate spending decision. Pricing the choice against "nothing" hides exactly the thing you sacrificed.
:::

## 1.3 Trade-offs, and thinking at the margin

Because means are scarce, choices come as **trade-offs**: more of one thing means less of another. You cannot maximise sleep, grades, income, and a social life in the same week; you can only choose a mix. Naming this honestly is a relief, because it ends the fantasy that with enough discipline you could have all of it. You cannot. Nobody can. The task is not to escape the trade-off but to choose it deliberately.

The tool for choosing well is :::glossary term="Marginal thinking":::. Big life questions feel all-or-nothing — "should I be working while I study?" — but almost no real decision is all-or-nothing. The real question is nearly always *at the margin*: given everything else already fixed, is *this next* shift, *this next* hour, *this next* €20, worth what it costs? The first ten hours of work a week might cost you very little in grades and buy you rent. The thirtieth hour might cost you a whole grade band. Same job, same wage, completely different trade-off — because what matters is the value of the *next* unit, not the average.

:::argument title="The case for treating your own time as priced"
conclusion: A working student should assign an explicit personal value to an hour of their own clear-headed time and use it when deciding whether to take extra work.
premise: Every extra shift is really a trade of time for money, and trades are only good ones when what you get exceeds what you give up.
premise: Without a rough price on your time, the money side of the trade is vivid and the time side is invisible, so the comparison is systematically biased toward taking the shift.
premise: A rough, explicit number — even "this hour is worth at least €X to me tonight" — makes the invisible side visible and improves the decision at the margin.
:::

:::counter title="Objection: pricing your hours can become its own trap" to="The case for treating your own time as priced"
The strongest reply is that not everything valuable survives being priced. Turn every hour into a number and you can end up refusing rest, refusing a friend, refusing an unpaid thing that mattered, because it "did not clear the rate" — a joyless accounting that misreads life as a spreadsheet. Worse, the number is often fake precision: you cannot really know what an hour of studying is worth in euros, and dressing a guess in a currency sign can make a bad decision feel rigorous. The honest version of the argument is narrower: use a priced hour as a *prompt to notice the trade-off*, not as a tyrant that settles it. The point is to make the sacrifice visible, not to pretend it is exactly measurable.
:::

## 1.4 Sunk cost: the money already gone

Now the mirror image. Opportunity cost is about the future you are choosing between. **Sunk cost** is about the past you should ignore — and usually cannot.

:::definition term="Sunk cost"
A cost already incurred that cannot be recovered whatever you now choose. Because it is the same under every option, a sunk cost is irrelevant to a forward-looking decision — yet people routinely let it drive them, "throwing good money after bad" so the earlier spend will not feel wasted.
:::

You paid €400 for a certification course you now realise is teaching you nothing. The €400 is gone; no decision you make tomorrow brings it back. The only live question is whether the *next* hours you would spend on the course beat their opportunity cost — and if the course is useless, they do not. Yet the pull is to continue, precisely because stopping makes the €400 feel wasted. It was already wasted the moment the course turned out to be poor; continuing only adds your scarce hours to the loss. The same trap keeps people in the wrong module, the wrong flat, sometimes the wrong relationship: "I've already put so much in." That sentence is a description of the past presented as a reason about the future, and it is not one.

:::check reread="1.4"
question: You bought a €300 annual gym membership two months ago and have gone twice; you now dread it and would rather spend that time on coursework. Which reasoning is soundest?
- Keep going — you have already paid €300, and quitting wastes that money.
- Keep going until you have been enough times that the cost-per-visit feels acceptable.
- The €300 is spent either way; decide only whether future gym hours beat their opportunity cost — and if you would rather study, stop.
- Quitting is always right once you dislike something, regardless of cost.
answer: 3
explanation: The €300 is a sunk cost: identical under "continue" and "quit," so it should not enter the decision at all. The only forward-looking question is whether future gym hours are worth more than what you would otherwise do with them. Options 1 and 2 both let a gone-and-unrecoverable cost steer the future, which is the fallacy itself.
:::

## 1.5 When scarcity taxes the mind

There is a final twist, and it is the one most relevant to a stretched life. Scarcity does not only shape which choices you face; it changes the *mind that is choosing*. In *Scarcity*, the economist Sendhil Mullainathan and the psychologist Eldar Shafir argue that being short of something — money, time, calories — captures attention so forcefully that it degrades the very capacities you need to manage it well.[^scarcity] They call the effect a tax on **bandwidth**: when part of your mind is permanently running a background loop about rent, or a deadline, or the money you owe, there is simply less of it left for everything else.

:::glossary term="Scarcity mindset":::, in this sense, is not a character flaw or a failure of willpower. It is a predictable cognitive load. It explains why people who are short on money make decisions that look impatient or short-sighted from the outside — not because they are worse at thinking, but because scarcity has borrowed the bandwidth they would need to think. It is why the tired, over-scheduled version of you agrees to the shift before finishing the thought: tunnelling on the immediate ("cover the gap, get the money") while the important-but-not-urgent ("protect Thursday") sits outside the tunnel, unseen.

:::uncertainty title="How large is the bandwidth tax?"
Mullainathan and Shafir report experiments in which inducing thoughts of financial strain measurably lowered performance on reasoning tasks, and describe the effect as comparable in size to a meaningful drop in fluid intelligence or a night without sleep. The direction of the finding is well supported, but the specific magnitude — and how far laboratory results generalise to a real over-worked week — is the sort of precise empirical claim this book should not assert as a settled number. Citation needed — verify before publishing. Treat the mechanism as real and the exact size as unsettled.
:::

The practical upshot is gentle, not moralising. If scarcity itself taxes your judgement, then the answer is not to try harder to think clearly while exhausted; it is, wherever possible, to *move the decision out of the tunnel* — to decide the recurring questions in advance, when you are rested, so the tired version of you is merely following a plan rather than reasoning from scratch. We will build exactly those advance decisions in Chapter 3. For now it is enough to notice that the mind doing your economics is itself one of the scarce resources, and it does not perform at its best precisely when the stakes are highest.

:::reflect
Think of one recurring decision in your week that you keep making in the moment, while tired — picking up shifts, ordering food, staying up, saying yes to a favour. Write down what its opportunity cost usually is, and what its sunk cost usually is. Then draft a single rule you could decide *now*, while rested, that your tired self would only have to follow. Keep the rule; you will test it against Chapter 3.
:::

None of this tells you whether to have taken the shift. That depends on your rent, your degree, your week, your body — specifics no book can know. What the economics gives you is not the answer but the right two numbers to hold up against each other: the money in front of you, and the scarce thing it quietly spends. Most of the rest of managing a stretched life is learning to see the second number as clearly as the first.

[^robbins]: Lionel Robbins, *An Essay on the Nature and Significance of Economic Science* (1932), is the source of the "ends and scarce means which have alternative uses" definition that still frames introductory economics.
[^scarcity]: Sendhil Mullainathan and Eldar Shafir, *Scarcity: Why Having Too Little Means So Much* (2013), is the source for the "bandwidth tax," tunnelling, and the argument that scarcity itself degrades decision-making.
