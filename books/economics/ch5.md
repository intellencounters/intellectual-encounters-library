# 5. Why Nations Differ: Institutions, Development, and the Economics of Moving

Chapter 4 ended on a fact so large it is easy to stop seeing: the same person, with the same hands and degree, earns several times more on one side of a border than the other. You did not become more productive somewhere over the Arabian Sea; something about *Germany* — not about you — multiplied the value of your hour. This chapter asks what that something is, then turns to the economics of moving itself — who gains, who loses, what the money sent home does at national scale, and whether the gap that made you pack a bag is closing.

## 5.1 The question behind the visa queue

Why are some countries rich and others poor? It is the oldest question in economics — Adam Smith's book was an inquiry into the *wealth of nations* — and for this book's readers it is not academic: every queue at the Ausländerbehörde is full of people whose lives are organised around the fact that place matters more than almost anything about the individual. The immediate causes are not mysterious — rich countries have more capital per worker, more schooling, better technology and infrastructure — but that only pushes the question back a step: why did *they* accumulate those things and not others? Machines can be bought and ideas famously refuse to stay fenced; something upstream determines who accumulates and who does not. The most influential modern answer is: :::glossary term="Institutions":::.

:::definition term="Institutions"
The humanly devised rules of the game in a society — laws, property rights, courts, constitutions, and the informal norms and enforcement that make them real. In the tradition of Douglass North, institutions shape the incentives behind every economic act: whether effort is rewarded or expropriated, whether contracts are kept, whether strangers can safely trade. On this view they, more than resources or geography, determine long-run prosperity.
:::

## 5.2 The deep-causes debate, honestly staged

Douglass North argued that the wealth of nations is downstream of their rules: where property is secure, courts are honest, and rulers are constrained, people invest, innovate, and trade with strangers; where any effort's fruits can be taken, they rationally do not.[^north] Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson sharpened this into the distinction organising *Why Nations Fail*: **inclusive** institutions, which spread opportunity and power broadly, versus **extractive** ones, which concentrate both in an elite that siphons what others produce.[^whynationsfail] Their evidence rests on history's accidental experiments — places split by an arbitrary line into different rule-systems, where one side prospers and the other stagnates despite identical geography and culture. The theory explains something raw in many readers' experience: why a shopkeeper in one country needs forty permissions and a bribe where another needs a form, and why talent responds by leaving.

:::argument title="The institutional explanation of prosperity, at full strength"
conclusion: Differences in institutions — the rules governing property, power, and opportunity — are the principal deep cause of the vast differences in national prosperity.
premise: Sustained prosperity requires millions to invest, innovate, and trade with strangers, which they only do when rules credibly protect the fruits of effort.
premise: Natural experiments dividing similar populations and identical geographies under different rule-systems have produced dramatically divergent prosperity across the line.
premise: Rival deep causes struggle with such cases: geography cannot explain divergence across an arbitrary border, and culture changes too slowly to explain rapid turnarounds when a country's rules change.
:::

:::counter title="Objection: institutions are not the whole story, and may not be the start of it" to="The institutional explanation of prosperity, at full strength"
The strongest reply comes in two waves. First, geography: Jared Diamond and others argue that deep physical facts — which continents had domesticable crops and animals, disease burdens like malaria, access to coasts and navigable rivers — shaped who could accumulate surplus and state capacity in the first place, so measured institutions are partly *products* of geography rather than an independent first cause.[^diamond] Second, culture and human capital: trust between strangers, norms of education, and accumulated know-how plausibly drive both good institutions and growth, and the celebrated natural experiments cannot fully separate the rules from the people operating them. There is also a directionality problem: prosperity may build good institutions as much as the reverse. The institutional view remains perhaps the strongest single account — but it is a leading hypothesis in a live, unresolved debate, not a proven theorem, and its most careful proponents say as much.
:::

Development economics has partly stepped back from the grand question altogether. Abhijit Banerjee and Esther Duflo built a movement around refusing it: rather than asking why nations are poor, ask which specific policies — this deworming programme, this microcredit design — actually work, and test them like medicines, with randomised trials.[^poorecon] The approach has its own critics — small answers may not add up to growth, and what works in one district may not travel — but it embodies this book's temperament: prefer a modest claim you can check to a grand one you cannot.

:::check reread="5.2"
question: Two regions share climate, crops, language, and ancestry, but were divided decades ago under different political systems; one is now several times richer. Which conclusion does this evidence best support, and with what caveat?

answer: 3
explanation: Because geography and culture are held constant by the split, the divergence points to the thing that differed — the institutions — which is why such natural experiments anchor the Acemoglu–Robinson case. :::

## 5.3 The economics of moving: who gains, who loses

If prosperity attaches to places, the sharpest personal response is to change places — which is what you did. Economically, migration is arbitrage: the same labour is priced differently in two markets, and moving captures the difference. The gains to the mover are typically the largest sums in this entire book — often a multiple of income, not a percentage — which is why people cross deserts and seas for the chance: no scholarship or aid programme rivals a visa in its effect on a family's finances.

But an honest ledger has more lines. In the destination country, migration enlarges the economy and benefits employers and consumers, while its effect on local wages is the contested entry: newcomers compete with some workers (most plausibly earlier migrants and those in similar jobs) and complement others, and economists genuinely disagree about the net effect on the lowest-paid natives. In the origin country, the family gains remittances and the returnee's skills — but loses presence: the daughter not at the table, the son not there when a parent falls ill, the care work money cannot perform. And the mover pays costs no payslip records: years of separation, the long-distance relationship, demotion from professional to "foreigner," the exhaustion of living in a second language. Chapter 4's compensating differentials apply to countries too — part of the wage gap is compensation for what migration takes.

:::uncertainty title="How much does immigration affect local wages?"
This is among the most politically weaponised questions in economics, and the research literature is genuinely divided: some prominent studies find negligible effects of immigration on native wages even from large sudden inflows; others find meaningful negative effects concentrated on the lowest-paid or on previous migrants. Method, place, and period drive the answers, and any specific percentage — in either direction — is a figure this book cannot responsibly assert: citation needed — verify before publishing. Be suspicious of anyone, on any side, who quotes one number as though the matter were closed.
:::

## 5.4 Brain drain, or brain gain?

The oldest moral charge against migration from poor countries is :::glossary term="Brain drain":::: the country that paid to train its doctors and engineers watches richer countries harvest them. The charge deserves its full weight. Health systems in some poor regions genuinely struggle while their trained nurses staff hospitals abroad; the state subsidised the training, and the return on it walks out through the departure gate. And if talented people can always leave, the pressure to fix institutions at home weakens — exit substitutes for voice.

The counter-case — sometimes called *brain gain* — is stronger than it first sounds. The *prospect* of migration raises the incentive to acquire education, and since not everyone leaves, the origin country can end up with more trained people, not fewer. Migrants send back money, but also ideas, standards, business links, and sometimes themselves: return migrants and diaspora networks have been central to celebrated success stories, India's IT industry the canonical example. And an uncomfortable question of ownership is buried in the metaphor: a person is not a national asset that can be drained, and restricting the emigration of the trained would mean conscripting individuals into staying poor for others' benefit. Which effect dominates plausibly depends on country, profession, and decade — the loss most credible for small countries losing scarce medical staff, the gain most credible for large countries with deep talent pools.

:::uncertainty title="Does skilled emigration hurt or help origin countries on net?"
Both mechanisms are real and documented; their net balance is unsettled and almost certainly varies by context. Claims that skilled emigration clearly devastates, or clearly enriches, origin countries outrun the evidence, and any specific estimate of the effect needs checking: citation needed — verify before publishing. Hold this one as genuinely open.
:::

## 5.5 Remittances at the scale of nations

Chapter 3 treated the :::glossary term="Remittance"::: as a household decision. Zoom out and it becomes one of the largest financial flows on earth: money sent home by migrants worldwide is of the same order of magnitude as — by many accounts exceeding — all official development aid combined, and for several countries it is a substantial share of the entire economy. Unlike aid, it arrives person-to-person, targeted by love rather than ministries, and it tends to keep flowing in crises when investors flee. The caveats are real: remittances can let governments postpone reform, can push up local prices and currencies, and are earned at the human cost the previous section counted.

:::uncertainty title="The size of global remittance flows"
The orders of magnitude above — remittances rivalling official aid and forming a large share of some economies — reflect widely reported figures from bodies like the World Bank, but totals change yearly and informal channels make measurement hard, so this book fixes no number: citation needed — verify before publishing. The durable claim is qualitative: remittances are among the largest and most stable financial flows to developing countries.
:::

The dignity in this deserves saying plainly: the tired person wiring money home after a night shift is, aggregated across millions, a development programme larger than the official ones — and run without a single committee.

## 5.6 Two economies in one country, and the long hope of catching up

One more thing the payslip conceals. In Germany your work is visible to the state: contracted, taxed, insured, pensioned. Across much of the world, most work is not. The :::glossary term="Informal economy"::: — street vendors, cash-paid labour, unregistered workshops — employs the majority of workers in many developing countries. Informality is a rational response to the institutions of Section 5.2: where registering a business takes months and invites predation, staying invisible is protection. Its price is steep — no contract enforcement, no scale, no credit, no pension — which is how weak institutions become low productivity one small firm at a time. The improvisational genius this breeds has an affectionate name in India: :::glossary term="Jugaad":::, the frugal workaround, the machine kept running with wire and wit. Jugaad deserves its celebration — human capital flourishing without institutional support — and its honest accounting: an economy that *must* improvise everything is one whose rules have failed it.

So: is the gap closing? Standard growth theory predicts :::glossary term="Convergence":::: poorer economies should grow faster than rich ones — they can adopt existing technology rather than invent it — until incomes draw level. The record is honestly mixed. The East Asian catch-ups, and the post-reform growth of China and India, confirm at historic scale that convergence can happen and has lifted hundreds of millions from poverty. But convergence is *conditional* — it appears mainly where institutions and policies let accumulation proceed — and other regions have stagnated for decades. The gap between Kochi and Cologne is not a law of nature; it has narrowed within your parents' lifetime. Nor is it closing on any schedule your career can wait for. Both halves of that sentence are true, and both belong in the decision you already made.

:::reflect
The institutional view implies your income depends mostly on the rules you live under; the personal view implies it depends mostly on effort and skill. Both are partly true, and which you *emphasise* changes how you judge yourself and others. Write down how you currently split the credit for your own position between your effort and the accident of which rules you moved under. Then steel-man the opposite split: argue, as strongly as you can, for the allocation you rejected. Note which argument was harder to write, and what that suggests about your default story.
:::

None of this chapter tells you whether moving was right — that ledger includes entries no economist can price, and only you can read them all. What it offers is the honest frame: you performed arbitrage on the largest price gap in the world; that gap is mostly made of rules, not of worth; the money you send home is part of something vast; and the two cities on your two payslips are moving points, not fixed ones. The final chapter turns from nations back to you: how to make decisions when you cannot know how any of this turns out.

[^north]: Douglass North, *Institutions, Institutional Change and Economic Performance* (1990), is the foundational statement of institutions as the "rules of the game" shaping incentives and long-run performance.
[^whynationsfail]: Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson, *Why Nations Fail: The Origins of Power, Prosperity, and Poverty* (2012), develops the inclusive-versus-extractive framework and the natural experiments discussed here.
[^diamond]: Jared Diamond, *Guns, Germs, and Steel* (1997), is the best-known statement of the geographic explanation of long-run development differences — the strongest counterweight to purely institutional accounts.
[^poorecon]: Abhijit Banerjee and Esther Duflo, *Poor Economics* (2011), presents the experimental approach to development — testing specific interventions rather than debating grand causes — for which the authors shared a Nobel prize.
