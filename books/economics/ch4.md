# 4. What a Wage Is: Labour Markets, Credentials, and Bargaining Power

Two payslips arrive in the same week. Yours, from the warehouse in Germany: a rate that felt astonishing when you first converted it to rupees and feels ordinary now that you pay German rent. And a WhatsApp screenshot from a cousin in Kochi, celebrating a raise that would not cover your monthly transport pass. Same species, similar age, comparable effort — wildly different prices for an hour of a human being's time. This chapter asks who sets that number: what a wage actually is, and why yours looks the way it does.

## 4.1 A wage is a price, and prices come from two sides

The unromantic starting point: a wage is a price — the price of an hour of a particular kind of labour, in a particular place and time, set at the meeting of two sides: how many people can supply that work, and how much employers demand it. Neither side alone settles anything. A genuinely difficult skill can pay badly if many people have it; a modest one can pay well if it is scarce where it is needed. The wage is not a grade for effort or virtue; it is a measure of scarcity on one side and usefulness-to-someone-paying on the other.

This is uncomfortable, and worth sitting in rather than rushing past. The night shift you work is *hard* — harder than plenty of desk jobs that pay triple — but the market does not pay for hardness; it pays for the intersection of "few people can or will do this" and "someone profits from it being done." A nurse's work is more essential than a footballer's; the footballer earns more because millions will pay to watch one nearly irreplaceable person.

What you carry to that market — the skills, knowledge, health, and languages that make your hour productive — economists call your :::glossary term="Human capital":::, a term made central by Gary Becker.[^becker] The word "capital" is deliberate: like a machine, your capabilities were costly to build, raise the value of what you produce, and can be invested in further. Every evening spent on a German course or a data-analysis certificate is, in this frame, capital formation — which is precisely why you are doing a degree at all.

## 4.2 Why the night shift pays extra

If wages were only about skill scarcity, identical jobs would pay identically. They do not, and one honest reason is the :::glossary term="Compensating differential":::: pay differences that offset differences in how pleasant, safe, or convenient the work itself is.

:::definition term="Compensating differential"
The portion of a wage that compensates for a job's non-monetary characteristics — night hours, danger, monotony, insecurity — rather than for the skill it requires. Where workers can choose between jobs, unpleasant conditions must offer extra pay (or pleasant ones can offer less) to attract anyone. The observation goes back to Adam Smith.
:::

The night premium on your shift is a compensating differential: the employer is buying not just your labour but your circadian rhythm, missed dinners, the Sunday you sleep through. Read this way, some baffling wage patterns make sense. A boring-but-secure job can "pay" partly in security, so its cash wage is lower; a glamorous industry can underpay because the glamour is part of the compensation — which is how creative fields get away with poverty wages for queueing interns. The lens also cuts the other way, less kindly: the worst-paid jobs are often *also* the most unpleasant, which should not persist if workers could freely choose. Where it persists, it usually means the workers in question — often migrants, often people whose visa or language limits their options — cannot. A compensating differential requires alternatives; bargaining power, which we come to below, is the name for having them.

:::check reread="4.2"
question: A hospital pays cleaners on the infectious-diseases ward more than cleaners elsewhere, for identical cleaning skills. Which concept best explains the gap?
- Human capital — the ward cleaners must be more skilled.
- A compensating differential — extra pay offsetting the unpleasant and riskier conditions, not extra skill.
- Loss aversion — the cleaners fear losing their jobs.
- The minimum wage — the law requires higher pay on that ward.
answer: 2
explanation: The skills are stipulated to be identical, so human capital cannot explain the difference. The extra pay compensates for the non-monetary disadvantage of the work — risk and unpleasantness — which is the definition of a compensating differential: the wage pricing the job's conditions, not the worker's training.
:::

## 4.3 The minimum wage: an honest look at a live fight

Germany introduced a statutory minimum wage in 2015; it is the floor under many of the jobs this book's readers work. Whether such floors help or harm the people they name is one of the most fought-over questions in economics, and you deserve the state of the fight rather than a slogan.

The textbook case against is clean: if the law sets the price of labour above what some workers' output is worth to employers, employers hire fewer of them — so the floor helps those who keep jobs at the higher rate and harms those priced out entirely, often the youngest and least experienced. The case for is equally serious: real labour markets are not textbook auctions. A few large employers in a town, workers who cannot easily move or search, visa holders tied to a sponsor — in such markets employers have wage-setting power, pay less than labour is worth, and a floor can raise pay *without* costing jobs by clawing back some of that power. The famous empirical challenge came from David Card and Alan Krueger, who compared fast-food employment across a state border after one side raised its minimum wage, finding none of the job losses the simple model predicts.[^cardkrueger]

:::uncertainty title="What do minimum-wage rises actually do to employment?"
Studies point in different directions depending on country, period, method, and how high the floor sits relative to typical wages. A fair summary seems to be that moderate minimum wages have produced smaller job losses than the simple model predicted — sometimes none detectable — while very high floors remain riskier; but any specific effect size, including any claim about the German minimum wage's measured impact, is exactly the kind of figure this book cannot responsibly assert: citation needed — verify before publishing. Treat this as a live empirical debate in which honest economists disagree, not as settled in either direction.
:::

The takeaway is not a verdict but a habit: whoever tells you the minimum wage obviously destroys jobs, or obviously never does, is selling a side. The truthful answer — "it depends on how high, where, and on who holds the bargaining power" — is less satisfying and more accurate.

## 4.4 What is the degree actually buying? Two rival stories

Now the question closest to home. You are paying for a degree with money and, more painfully, night shifts and years. What exactly are you buying? Economics offers two rival answers, and the argument between them is about your life.

The first story is Becker's: education builds human capital. The degree makes you genuinely more productive — statistics, systems, methods, the language of your field — and employers pay more because you can *do* more. The learning is the product, and every hour of real understanding is an investment that pays.

The second story, formalised by Michael Spence, is that education is substantially :::glossary term="Signalling":::.[^spence] Employers cannot directly observe how smart, conscientious, and persistent you are; a completed degree is a credible *signal* of those pre-existing traits, credible precisely because it is much harder to finish for people who lack them. On this story the payoff comes not from what the degree taught you but from what finishing it *proves* — the content could be half-forgotten by graduation without the signal losing value. Bryan Caplan has pushed this view to its provocative limit.[^caplan]

:::argument title="The signalling case, stated at full strength"
conclusion: A substantial part of what a degree earns its holder comes from certifying pre-existing ability and persistence to employers, not from skills created by the coursework.
premise: Employers reward completed degrees far more than almost-completed ones, though one missing semester can hardly erase years of skill — which fits certification better than skill-building.
premise: Much tested course content is rarely used in graduates' actual jobs, and much is forgotten within a few years, yet the wage premium persists.
premise: If employers mainly bought skills, cheap ways of demonstrating the same skills without the credential would rapidly erode the degree premium; in practice the credential retains its grip.
:::

:::counter title="Objection: the human-capital story survives the attack" to="The signalling case, stated at full strength"
The strongest reply is that the sharp opposition is false: the two stories are shares of a mixture, and the human-capital share is larger than the signalling case admits. Much of what education builds is not testable content but transferable capacity — writing, abstraction, working to deadline in a second language, learning how to learn — which graduates use daily without recognising its origin, so "I never use my coursework" undercounts the skill. The forgetting argument proves less than it seems: you forget the theorems but keep the trained reasoning. And the completion premium partly reflects licensing, visa rules, and employers' legal caution, not merely signalling. Separating the shares empirically is notoriously difficult; the honest position is that both mechanisms are real, their proportions are unsettled, and anyone quoting a precise split is ahead of the evidence.
:::

Why does the split matter to you? Because the two stories give different advice at the margin. If the degree is mostly human capital, *understanding* is the asset: skipping the learning while collecting the certificate cheats yourself. If it is substantially signalling, *completion* is the asset — and the cruellest outcome is the one signalling theory predicts to be worst: years of study, debt, and night shifts with no certificate at the end, buying neither the skill premium nor the signal. Under either story one rule survives: protect completion first, extract real skill second, and treat "I'll drop out and the knowledge will still count" as the most expensive sentence in this book.

- Correct — a wage premium can only come from skills the education created.
- Incorrect — the premium is entirely signalling, and degrees teach nothing of value.
- The premium alone cannot distinguish the two stories: it would appear whether degrees build productivity (human capital) or certify pre-existing traits (signalling), and the real mixture between them is empirically unsettled.
- The premium exists only because of minimum-wage laws.
answer: 3
explanation: Both theories predict the observed fact — graduates earning more — so the premium alone cannot decide between them. Human capital says education creates the productivity; signalling says it certifies what was already there. Distinguishing the shares requires subtler evidence, and the chapter's honest position is that both are real and the proportions are contested.
:::

## 4.5 The credential that did not board the plane

Signalling theory explains one of migration's quiet cruelties: a signal is only worth what the receiver can read. A degree, a licence, ten years of experience are certificates issued inside one institutional world, and when you cross a border the receiver changes. The German employer cannot easily judge what your Indian qualification certifies; the professional body may not recognise it at all. This is why the doctor drives a taxi and the engineer stacks shelves — each instance a person whose human capital crossed the border intact while the *signal* of it was held at customs.

The economics here is two-sided: recognition rules partly protect the public — you want the person titled "engineer" to meet a known standard — and partly protect incumbents from competition; every professional body has an interest in blurring which is which. For the individual, the lesson is strategic rather than bitter. Your capital and your credential are separate assets, and the credential's value is jurisdiction-specific. Time spent converting it — the recognition procedure, the local certificate, the German professional exam — is not bureaucratic humiliation, however much it feels like it at the Ausländerbehörde; it is re-issuing your signal in a currency the local receiver accepts, and often among the highest-return investments available to a migrant, precisely because the skill already exists and only the proof is missing.

## 4.6 Bargaining power, and why Kochi pays differently from Cologne

A wage is a price — but a *negotiated* price, and negotiation depends on what each side can do if talks fail. A worker who can credibly walk away — a buffer from Chapter 3, an in-demand skill, a visa not chained to one employer — captures more of the value they create; a worker who cannot afford one missed paycheque takes what is offered. Unions exist to change this arithmetic: through :::glossary term="Collective bargaining":::, workers negotiate as one body that *can* credibly walk away together, which is why wages in strongly unionised German sectors are set in industry-wide agreements rather than one exhausted person at a time. Whether unions on net help workers overall or protect insiders at outsiders' expense is another honest, live debate — but the conceptual point stands: your wage reflects not only what your hour produces but what share of it you can *bargain* for.

Which returns us to the two payslips. Why does the same person earn several times more in Cologne than Kochi? Not because Germans work harder. Mostly because an hour of labour in Germany is embedded in more capital — machines, logistics, software, infrastructure — and in institutions that make each hour more productive and let workers bargain for a share of it. Part of the gap is illusory: prices differ, which is why economists compare incomes using :::glossary term="Purchasing power parity"::: rather than raw exchange rates — your euro wage shrinks when spent on German rent. But even adjusted for prices, a large real gap remains, and it attaches to the *place*, not the person. That is the most consequential economic fact in this book's readers' lives — in a real sense, it is why you are reading this in Germany — and it demands an explanation deeper than individual effort. That explanation, and the economics of the move itself, is Chapter 5.

[^becker]: Gary Becker, *Human Capital: A Theoretical and Empirical Analysis* (1964), is the foundational treatment of education, training, and health as investments that raise a person's productivity and earnings.
[^spence]: Michael Spence, "Job Market Signaling," *Quarterly Journal of Economics* (1973), is the canonical model of education as a costly, credible signal of unobservable ability; the work contributed to Spence's Nobel prize.
[^cardkrueger]: David Card and Alan Krueger, *Myth and Measurement: The New Economics of the Minimum Wage* (1995), presents the border-comparison studies — most famously of New Jersey and Pennsylvania fast-food restaurants — that challenged the consensus that minimum wages must reduce employment.
[^caplan]: Bryan Caplan, *The Case Against Education* (2018), argues that a large share of education's payoff is signalling — cited as the strongest recent statement of that side, not as a verdict.
