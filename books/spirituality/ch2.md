# 2. Desire, Suffering, and Letting Go

Stoicism handed us a territory — what is up to us — and told us to tend it and release the rest. But it left a question standing. *Why* do we grip so hard in the first place? Why does the promotion, once wanted, feel like oxygen? Why does the cousin's flat, which changes nothing in your life, sit in your chest for a week? The traditions born in India put desire itself at the centre. They ask not only how to bear what we cannot control, but why craving has such a hold, and what it would mean to loosen it without going numb.

We approach these traditions as philosophies of living. Buddhism and the strands of Hindu thought we draw on are, for millions, religions in the fullest sense, with rituals, communities, and metaphysical commitments. Nothing here asks you to accept or reject those. We are borrowing the practical core — the diagnosis of suffering and the discipline of letting go — the way one might learn from a doctor's account of the body without adopting the doctor's politics.

## 2.1 The Buddhist diagnosis: dukkha and its cause

The Buddha's first teaching is usually rendered as "life is suffering," which is both memorable and misleading. The word is :::glossary term="Dukkha":::, and it means something wider and subtler than pain: unsatisfactoriness, friction, the sense of things not quite holding.

:::definition term="Dukkha"
A central Buddhist term, imperfectly translated "suffering" but closer to "unsatisfactoriness." It names not only acute pain but the low, pervasive ache of experience that does not last, does not fully satisfy, and does not stay as we want it — the gap between how things are and how we wish they would remain.
:::

You know dukkha without the vocabulary. It is the flatness an hour after the thing you wanted arrives. It is refreshing the feed for a satisfaction that never quite lands. It is the good evening spoiled by the thought that it will end. The Buddhist claim is that this friction is not a personal failing or a run of bad luck but a structural feature of a mind that wants the world to be fixed when the world is not.

Its cause, on this account, is craving — *tanha*, thirst. Not desire in the broad sense of caring about things, but the grasping, clinging quality of desire: the demand that a pleasant thing stay, that an unpleasant thing vanish, that the self be shored up against loss. The famous, liberating turn is that craving is something we do, and therefore something we might do less of. The Dhammapada, an early collection of sayings, puts the whole psychology in a line: what we are is made by our minds; suffering follows a grasping mind as the wheel follows the ox.[^dhp]

## 2.2 Impermanence and non-attachment

Behind the diagnosis stands a plain observation: :::glossary term="Impermanence":::. Everything conditioned arises, changes, and passes — sensations, moods, jobs, relationships, the body, the self you think you are defending. Much of dukkha, the Buddhists argue, is simply the collision between this fact and our wish for permanence. We suffer not because things end but because we insist they should not.

The response is :::glossary term="Non-attachment":::: to hold experiences, people, and outcomes without clinging, without staking your identity or peace on their staying fixed.

:::check reread="2.2"
question: A friend says: "Non-attachment means not caring about your girlfriend or your career — just going cold." What has he mistaken?
- Nothing; Buddhism does teach becoming emotionally cold toward people.
- Non-attachment targets the *clinging* — the anxious demand that things stay fixed — not the caring itself; the aim is to love and act fully without being enslaved by the need to control outcomes.
- Non-attachment applies only to objects, never to people.
- He is right that the two are the same thing.
answer: 2
explanation: The tradition distinguishes clinging from caring. Non-attachment loosens the grasping demand that a person, mood, or result never change; it is meant to make love steadier, not thinner, by freeing it from the panic of possible loss. Cold detachment is the caricature, not the teaching.
:::

Held well, non-attachment is not less love but love with the fear taken out. You call your parents not to discharge an obligation you dread but because you want to, and you can hear the ageing in their voices without the evening collapsing, because you are not demanding that time stop. Held badly, of course, it can curdle into an excuse for distance — and we will press exactly that worry in a moment.

## 2.3 The Hindu turn: dharma, karma, and acting without clinging

The Hindu traditions share much of this soil but plant something different in it. Where one strand of Buddhism can tilt toward withdrawal, the *Bhagavad Gita* — a dialogue set on a battlefield, the most read of the Hindu texts — insists on *action*.[^gita] Its hero, Arjuna, wants to lay down his weapons and walk away from a war he finds unbearable. Krishna's counsel is not "withdraw" but a harder thing: act, but change your relationship to the fruits of the act.

Two ideas anchor this. The first is :::glossary term="Dharma":::: the duty, the right action, the work that is genuinely yours to do given your situation and station. The second is :::glossary term="Karma":::, action and its consequences — read here not as a cosmic scoreboard but as the plain truth that a life is largely made of its accumulated acts, and that what you do repeatedly is what you become.

Krishna's synthesis is :::glossary term="Nishkama karma":::, action without attachment to its fruits.

:::definition term="Nishkama karma"
The *Bhagavad Gita*'s teaching of doing what your dharma requires wholeheartedly while releasing your grip on the result. Neither indifference nor half-heartedness: Krishna counsels Arjuna to act fully and skilfully, but to surrender the anxious craving to control how the action turns out — "your right is to the action, never to its fruits."
:::

Notice how close this lands to the Stoic dichotomy of control from Chapter 1, arrived at by a different road. Do the work that is yours; do it as well as you can; then let go of the outcome, which was never wholly yours to command. Two traditions, separated by thousands of miles and a different picture of the cosmos, converge on almost the same practical instruction — a convergence worth pausing on, because it suggests the counsel may be tracking something real about how minds and outcomes actually relate.

:::reflect
Pick one thing you are currently gripping by its outcome — an exam result, a reply you are waiting on, a relationship's future, a parent's approval. Write down what the *action* part of it is (the part genuinely up to you) and what the *fruit* part is (the part that is not). Then commit, in writing, to one week of pouring yourself into the first while deliberately not checking, chasing, or rehearsing the second — and note what you expect that to feel like, so you can compare later.
:::

## 2.4 The strongest objection: does letting go mean giving up?

We have now stated the Buddhist and Hindu counsels in their most attractive form. Honesty requires the hardest objection, and it is a real one.

:::argument title="The case for non-attachment as a way of living"
conclusion: Learning to act fully while holding outcomes loosely is a wiser way to live than gripping every result.
premise: Much of our suffering comes not from events but from the craving that events turn out a particular way, and from the refusal to accept that they did not.
premise: Outcomes are, in large part, not within our control, so staking our peace on them hands our wellbeing to forces we cannot command.
premise: We can practise separating wholehearted action, which is ours, from clinging to results, which poisons the action and the wait alike.
premise: A person who acts fully and clings less both acts better — with a clearer head — and suffers less in the waiting.
:::

:::counter title="Objection: non-attachment can shade into passivity and quietism" to="The case for non-attachment as a way of living"
Here is the sharp version. If you truly stop craving outcomes, why fight for a raise, contest a visa refusal, leave a bad relationship, or organise against an injustice? Every one of those requires *wanting the world to be different* and refusing to accept how it is. Critics — including reform-minded voices within these traditions and secular readers outside them — worry that "letting go" can become a spiritual anaesthetic: a way to make peace with conditions that ought to be changed, to counsel the exploited toward serenity instead of action. The tradition's own reply is that non-attachment concerns the *inner* grip on results, not the *outer* effort — Krishna, after all, tells Arjuna to fight, not to flee. But the reply only works if the line between "clinging to a result" and "committing to a goal" can actually be held in practice, and under pressure that line blurs. A person can talk themselves into calling surrender "acceptance." The worry is not a knock-down refutation; it is a permanent hazard the practice has to be honest about.
:::

The traditions do have resources here — the insistence on dharma, on right action, on the world-engaged bodhisattva who returns to help — and it would be unfair to caricature them as world-denying. But it would be equally unfair to pretend the tension is fully resolved.

:::uncertainty title="How far do these traditions counsel worldly engagement versus withdrawal?"
Buddhism and Hindu thought each contain a genuine internal spectrum — from renunciant, world-leaving strands to intensely world-engaged ones (socially engaged Buddhism; the Gita's insistence on doing one's duty in the world). Which strand is "the real one" is a contested interpretive question that scholars and practitioners argue about, not a fact this book can settle. Any confident claim that "Buddhism teaches passivity" or "the Gita endorses this or that politics" would be an overreach. Citation needed — verify before publishing; treat the balance sketched here as one defensible reading among several, not the definitive one.
:::

## 2.5 What to carry forward

Strip away what you cannot verify and what remains is a usable discipline, common to both traditions: notice how much of your suffering is the friction of craving rather than the event itself; see clearly that things change; act on what is yours to do; and loosen the grip on results without loosening your commitment to the work. It rhymes with the Stoic answer and deepens it, because it goes after the *root* of the grip — desire — rather than only its management.

:::check reread="2.4"
question: What is the fairest way to state the "passivity" worry about non-attachment?
- That non-attachment is obviously false because everyone clearly suffers.
- That if letting go of outcomes is taken too far, it can become an excuse to accept conditions that ought instead to be resisted — and the line between accepting and surrendering is genuinely hard to hold.
- That Buddhism and the Gita openly instruct their followers never to act.
- That the traditions have no resources at all for worldly engagement.
answer: 2
explanation: The strongest version grants that the traditions distinguish inner clinging from outer effort, and presses on how hard that line is to hold under pressure — where "acceptance" can quietly become surrender. It does not caricature the traditions as commanding inaction, which they do not; the Gita explicitly tells Arjuna to act.
:::

[^gita]: The *Bhagavad Gita*, part of the *Mahabharata*, presents Krishna's counsel to Arjuna on acting according to one's dharma without attachment to the fruits of action ("your right is to action alone, never to its fruits").
