# Glossary

## Dichotomy of control

The Stoic distinction between what is "up to us" and what is not. Our judgements, choices, and responses are up to us; outcomes, other people's opinions, the body, reputation, and fortune are not. The counsel is not to stop caring about the second group but to stop staking our peace of mind on it — to invest our effort where we have real power and to meet the rest with acceptance. The classic statement opens Epictetus' *Enchiridion*.

## Amor fati

Latin for "love of fate." A Stoic-flavoured attitude (later sharpened by Nietzsche) of not merely accepting but embracing what actually happens, including hardship, as the raw material of a life well lived. It is distinct from resignation: it asks you to say yes to the reality you are in rather than to a version you wish you had.

## Memento mori

Latin for "remember that you will die." A contemplative practice, common to Stoicism and many religious traditions, of holding one's mortality in view — not to induce dread but to clarify what matters and to loosen the grip of trivial anxieties. In the *Meditations*, Marcus Aurelius returns to it repeatedly as a spur to act well now.

## Eudaimonia

A Greek term usually translated "flourishing" or "a life well lived," rather than "happiness" in the sense of good mood. For the Stoics, eudaimonia consists in living according to virtue and reason; it is something you do, not something that happens to you, which is why they held that it cannot be taken away by bad luck.

## Virtue

In the Stoic (and broader Greek) sense, not primarily rule-following but excellence of character — wisdom, justice, courage, and self-control exercised well. The Stoics made the strong claim that virtue is *sufficient* for a good life: a person of good character living in hard circumstances still lives well, because the good life lies in how one acts, not in what one obtains.

## Dukkha

A central Buddhist term, imperfectly translated as "suffering" but closer to "unsatisfactoriness" or "stress." It names the friction running through ordinary experience: not only acute pain but the subtler ache of things not lasting, not satisfying, not staying as we want them. The first of the Buddha's Four Noble Truths is that dukkha is a feature of unawakened life.

## Non-attachment

The Buddhist and Hindu counsel to hold experiences, possessions, people, and outcomes without clinging — without staking one's identity or peace on their staying fixed. It is offered as a release from the anxiety of grasping, and is distinguished, in both traditions, from cold detachment or not caring; the aim is to love and act fully without being enslaved by the need to control results.

## Impermanence

The observation, foundational in Buddhism (Pali *anicca*), that all conditioned things arise, change, and pass — sensations, moods, relationships, selves. Much of dukkha is diagnosed as the mismatch between this fact and our wish for permanence. Seeing impermanence clearly is meant to loosen clinging rather than to breed despair.

## Dharma

A rich Hindu concept with no single English equivalent, spanning duty, right action, the moral order, and one's proper role in the world. In the *Bhagavad Gita* it centres on doing the work that is genuinely yours to do, in accordance with your situation and station, as an expression of a larger order rather than merely personal preference.

## Karma

Literally "action." In Hindu and Buddhist thought, the principle that actions carry moral consequences that shape one's future experience and character. Read here as a philosophy of living rather than a metaphysical ledger: it directs attention to the quality and intention of what one does now, on the view that a life is largely made of its accumulated acts.

## Nishkama karma

The *Bhagavad Gita*'s teaching of action without attachment to its fruits — doing what your dharma requires wholeheartedly while releasing your grip on the result. It is neither indifference nor half-heartedness: Krishna counsels Arjuna to act fully and skilfully, but to surrender the anxious craving to control how the action turns out.

## The absurd

Albert Camus' term for the collision between the human demand for meaning and a universe that returns no answer. The absurd is not in the world alone or in us alone but in the confrontation between the two. Camus' response is neither suicide nor a leap of faith but revolt: to live lucidly and fully in spite of the silence.

## Radical freedom

Jean-Paul Sartre's claim that human beings are "condemned to be free": with no fixed essence handed down in advance, we are the authors of what we become through our choices, and cannot offload that responsibility onto nature, society, or God. Freedom in this sense is a burden as much as a gift, since it leaves us answerable for who we make ourselves.

## Logotherapy

Viktor Frankl's approach to psychology, developed partly out of his survival of Nazi concentration camps, holding that the primary human drive is a "will to meaning." On this view, meaning is not invented from nothing but discovered in concrete commitments — to work, to people we love, and to the stance we take toward unavoidable suffering.

## Samatha

A family of Buddhist meditation practices, often translated "calm abiding," in which attention rests on a single object — classically the breath — and is returned there each time the mind wanders. The aim is not an empty mind but a trained one: the practice is the returning itself, repeated until attention becomes steadier and less easily hijacked. Traditionally paired with vipassana, which uses the steadied attention to observe experience.

## Vipassana

"Insight" — the Buddhist family of meditation practices that use steadied attention to observe experience closely: sensations, moods, and thoughts arising and passing. Its aim is not calm but seeing, for oneself, the tradition's central claims about impermanence, the friction of clinging, and the absence of a fixed inner manager. Classical lineages generally braid it with samatha rather than opposing the two.

## Askesis

Greek for "training" or "exercise" — the same word an athlete would use — denoting any structured practice performed on one's own character. Stoic examples include written self-examination (Marcus Aurelius' *Meditations* is a surviving specimen), the evening review of the day's conduct, and rehearsal of hardship. Only later did the word narrow into "asceticism" in the sense of severe self-denial.

## McMindfulness

A critics' label for secular, often corporate or app-mediated mindfulness that extracts meditation techniques from their traditional ethical framework, community, and diagnosis of craving. The steel-manned critique holds that the extraction changes the practice's function — turning a discipline aimed at loosening craving into a stress-management tool that can help people absorb conditions (overwork, precarity) that ought instead to be contested. Defenders reply that the relief is real and access widened; the debate is live.

## Pascal's wager

Blaise Pascal's argument, in the *Pensées*, that where reason cannot settle God's existence it can still be rational to commit to a religious life, because the stakes are radically asymmetric: a finite cost risked against an infinite gain. Not a proof of God but an exercise in decision under uncertainty. Standard objections include the many-gods problem (the arithmetic cannot select among rival deities) and the charge that belief adopted for payoff is the wrong kind of belief.

## Evidentialism

The thesis, classically stated in W. K. Clifford's "The Ethics of Belief," that one is justified in believing only in proportion to one's evidence — in Clifford's famous escalation, that it is wrong "always, everywhere, and for anyone" to believe on insufficient evidence, because beliefs feed actions and spread to others. William James's "The Will to Believe" defends a narrow exception for options that are live, forced, and momentous and that evidence cannot settle.

## The problem of evil

The oldest and most serious challenge to belief in an all-powerful, all-knowing, wholly good God: why, then, does the world contain the suffering it does? The logical version (strict incompatibility) is widely judged answerable by mere possibility, such as the free-will defence; the evidential version — that the sheer quantity and distribution of apparently pointless suffering is strong evidence against the hypothesis — remains the live and serious form of the challenge.

## Theodicy

An attempted explanation of why a good and omnipotent God would permit evil. Prominent examples include the free-will defence (genuine freedom entails the possibility of wrongdoing) and soul-making theodicy (courage, compassion, and forgiveness are logically impossible in a world without real stakes). Each faces the counter-challenge of the actual scale and distribution of suffering, which appears to destroy at least as often as it forms.

## Agnosticism

The definite epistemic position that the evidence concerning God's existence, honestly weighed, does not license confidence either way — a considered suspension of judgement, not fence-sitting weakness. On evidentialist principles it is what intellectual honesty requires when the data genuinely run out; critics on both sides reply that living, as one must, either inside or outside religious practice makes pure suspension harder than it sounds.

## Secularisation

The predicted process by which modernisation — science, prosperity, urban life — steadily dissolves religious belief and practice. The prediction's record is mixed: steep decline in some prosperous regions, persistence or intensification elsewhere, and the growth of hybrid "spiritual but not religious" identities that fit neither category. Most contemporary sociologists of religion regard the simple version of the thesis as at best incomplete.

## Credo

Latin for "I believe": a stated set of one's own commitments on the load-bearing questions — control, desire, duty, suffering and death, and doubt. As used in this book, a credo must be stated in one's own words, tested against hard cases and strong objections, and operative in actual conduct; it is written, dated, and held as provisional — revisable for reasons, though not for mere costs.

## Syncretism

The combining of elements from distinct religious or philosophical traditions. Historically the normal condition of religious life — every surviving tradition absorbed predecessors and neighbours — rather than a modern invention. As deliberate individual practice it carries a specific risk, named by the "cafeteria" critique: the selector tends to keep only what the present self finds congenial, stripping out the frictions through which traditions form character.

## Reflective equilibrium

A method for testing and revising one's commitments: hold general principles against firm judgements about particular cases, and where they clash, revise whichever — on reflection — deserves it less, continuing in both directions over time. It underwrites this book's test for distinguishing growth from drift: a revision made for reasons survives the method; a revision made because a commitment became expensive does not.
