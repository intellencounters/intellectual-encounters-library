# Citations and sources

This page lists the primary texts referred to in the chapters. All are canonical works whose existence and broad content are well established. What is *not* certified here is any specific quotation, verse number, section number, page, or translation wording: those vary widely across editions and must be checked against a named edition before being quoted or attributed in print.

## Stoicism (Chapter 1)

- **Epictetus, *Enchiridion* (*Handbook*)** — a short manual of Stoic practice compiled by his student Arrian from the *Discourses*. Its opening section states the "dichotomy of control": some things are up to us, some are not. Used for the core Stoic distinction.
- **Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*** — the private notebooks of the Roman emperor, not intended for publication, returning repeatedly to acceptance of fate (*amor fati*) and the contemplation of death (*memento mori*). Book and section numbering varies by edition.

## Buddhist and Hindu sources (Chapter 2)

- **The *Dhammapada*** — an early anthology of verses in the Buddhist tradition, opening with the teaching that mind shapes experience and that suffering follows a grasping mind. Used for the diagnosis of dukkha and craving. Verse numbering and wording differ across translations.
- **The *Bhagavad Gita*** (part of the *Mahabharata*) — Krishna's counsel to Arjuna on doing one's dharma without attachment to the fruits of action (*nishkama karma*): "your right is to action alone, never to its fruits." Chapter and verse numbering are standardised, but translations differ markedly.

## Existentialism and meaning (Chapter 3)

- **Albert Camus, *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942)** — develops "the absurd" and closes with "one must imagine Sisyphus happy." Used for the absurd and the response of revolt.
- **Jean-Paul Sartre, *Existentialism Is a Humanism* (1946)** and ***Being and Nothingness* (1943)** — sources for "existence precedes essence," "condemned to be free," and "bad faith." Used for radical freedom and responsibility.
- **Viktor Frankl, *Man's Search for Meaning* (first published 1946)** — Frankl's account of surviving Nazi concentration camps and the therapy (logotherapy) built on the "will to meaning" and the freedom to choose one's attitude toward unavoidable suffering. Used for meaning through purpose.

## Practice (Chapter 4)

- **The *Satipatthana Sutta* ("The Foundations of Mindfulness")**, in the Pali canon — the classic Buddhist discourse on mindfulness practice; used for the traditional pairing of calm (*samatha*) and insight (*vipassana*). Textual details and translations vary across editions of the canon.
- **Seneca, *Letters to Lucilius* (*Epistulae Morales*)** and **Seneca, *On Anger* (*De Ira*)** — sources for the Stoic practices of rehearsing hardship (set days of plain food and rough dress: "is this the condition I feared?") and the nightly review of the day's conduct. Letter and section numbering vary by edition.
- **Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*** — cited again in Chapters 4 and 6 as a surviving specimen of Stoic written practice (*askesis*): a private notebook of self-addressed exercises, traditionally titled *Ta eis heauton*, "to himself." Listed above under Chapter 1.

## Faith, reason, and doubt (Chapter 5)

- **Blaise Pascal, *Pensées*** (first published posthumously, 1670) — fragments toward an unfinished defence of Christianity, containing the wager. Fragment numbering differs across the standard editions (Brunschvicg, Lafuma, Sellier).
- **W. K. Clifford, "The Ethics of Belief" (1877)** — the shipowner example and the evidentialist thesis that it is wrong "always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence."
- **William James, "The Will to Believe" (1896)** — the classic reply to Clifford, defending the right to believe ahead of the evidence when an option is live, forced, and momentous and cannot be settled intellectually.
- **William James, *The Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902)** — the descriptive study of mystical, conversion, and prayerful states across traditions; used in Chapters 4 and 5 for the comparative view of contemplative practice and the evidential standing of religious experience.
- **David Hume, *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*** (published posthumously, 1779) — Part X restates the ancient trilemma about God and evil traditionally traced to Epicurus (an attribution that is conventional rather than certain).

## The credo (Chapter 6)

- **Ralph Waldo Emerson, "Self-Reliance" (1841)**, in *Essays: First Series* — source of "a foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of little minds," read in context as an attack on conformity to one's own past utterances for others' sake.

## A note on verification

Every specific wording, verse, section, and date above should be confirmed against a named, reputable edition or translation before republication. Where the chapters make interpretive or empirical claims that go beyond what these primary texts plainly say — for example, how far a tradition counsels worldly engagement versus withdrawal — those claims are flagged in the chapters themselves inside `:::uncertainty` blocks with the note "citation needed — verify before publishing." No quotations, page numbers, or study results have been invented; where precise attribution was not certain, the text refers to the work in general rather than fabricating a citation.
