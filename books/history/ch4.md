# 4. Entangled: India and Germany in Each Other's Histories

A German colleague mentions, between orders, that she does yoga on Tuesdays and once read the Bhagavad Gita. The same week, your integration course covers the darkest chapter of German history, and a word surfaces — *Aryan* — that you last heard in an Indian schoolbook, meaning something quite different. It can feel as though you personally are the first bridge between these two worlds. The bridge is far older than you. India and Germany have been reading, imagining, using, and misusing each other for over two centuries — a history that explains oddities of German culture you will keep meeting, hands you the true story behind one of the century's most abused words, and retires the idea that your presence here is new.

## 4.1 Two countries that imagined each other

Cultures that meet at a distance build images of each other, and the images say as much about the imaginer as the imagined. For much of the nineteenth century, educated Germans imagined India as the childhood of humanity — primordial wisdom that Europe had supposedly lost on its way to factories. Many Indians later imagined Germany as the serious Europe: the country that took Sanskrit seriously, whose engineering was worth studying, and — for some in the independence struggle — the enemy's enemy. Both images were partly true, partly projection, and both had consequences: Sanskrit chairs, Indian students on Berlin trams, and, at worst, a stolen word on a uniform.

The tool you need is :::glossary term="Orientalism"::: — the term, sharpened into a critique by Edward Said, for the West's habit of studying and representing "the East" in ways entangled with power over it: knowledge that was real, but framed by the interests and fantasies of the studier.[^said] Germany is an awkward, instructive case: German scholars led the European study of India while Germany, unlike Britain, held no Indian colony. Their India was mostly a textual dream — in some ways freer, in others more detached from living Indians than the colonial British variety.

## 4.2 The Romantics discover Sanskrit

The story begins with a linguistic shock. In 1786, the British judge and philologist William Jones, in Calcutta, argued that Sanskrit, Greek, and Latin were so alike in roots and grammar that they must have "sprung from some common source" — a founding moment for the idea of the Indo-European language family.[^jones] The startling implication: the sacred language of India and the classical languages of Europe were cousins.

Nowhere did this land harder than in the German lands. The Romantics — in revolt against the cold rationalism of the French Enlightenment — seized on India as the home of an older, warmer wisdom. Friedrich Schlegel published *Über die Sprache und Weisheit der Indier* ("On the Language and Wisdom of the Indians") in 1808, and German universities made :::glossary term="Indology"::: — the study of Indian languages, texts, and history — a national specialty, Sanskrit chairs multiplying through the century. Scholars such as Franz Bopp turned Jones's insight into systematic :::glossary term="Comparative philology":::, the discipline of reconstructing the family trees of languages; Schopenhauer, reading the Upanishads in a Latin translation of a Persian intermediary, praised them as a consolation of his life.

Notice, with Chapter 1's tools, what the Romantics were doing: building an India that served a German argument — against French rationalism, for national feeling rooted in deep pasts. Real philology, real admiration; woven through it, projection. Their India was a library, not a country. Its inhabitants appear mostly as descendants fallen from a golden age — a trope letting Europeans admire India while ruling or ignoring actual Indians.

## 4.3 Max Müller: the German who never went to India

The emblem of the entanglement is Friedrich Max Müller (1823–1900), a German philologist who spent his career at Oxford, produced a monumental edition of the Rig Veda, and edited the vast *Sacred Books of the East* series that shaped how the English-reading world — including English-educated Indians — encountered Asian scriptures.[^muller] Müller never visited India. He is a double figure: honoured by Indians of his era and since as the European who took Indian texts with utmost seriousness, and criticised, then and now, for reading India through Christian assumptions, and for the authority a foreign editor acquired over how India's scriptures reached the modern world.

Hold both truths. The scholarship was real: texts edited, preserved, translated on an unprecedented scale. And the frame was loaded: which texts counted as *the* sacred books, and what story of rise and decline they were slotted into, were choices made by European hands. Not a contradiction to resolve — just Chapter 1's honest reading of a source.

## 4.4 The stolen word: how "Aryan" went from grammar to atrocity

Now the word from the integration course. In the oldest Sanskrit texts, *ārya* is a self-description — conventionally rendered "noble" — used by the people of the Vedic hymns. Nineteenth-century philologists borrowed it: "Aryan" became a label for the Indo-European language family and, by a fateful slide, for the prehistoric people presumed to have spoken its ancestor. Two moves hide there. The first — the languages are related — is solid science. The second — therefore there was a single ancient *race* with identifiable pure descendants among modern nations — was a leap from grammar to blood that the evidence never supported.

Race theorists made the leap anyway. Across the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, writers transformed the "Aryans" from a reconstructed language community into a master race, relocated its imagined homeland to Europe, and ranked living peoples by their supposed share of its blood. National Socialism made this pseudo-science state doctrine: racial laws and ultimately genocide were administered under a word lifted from the Rig Veda. Be precise about what was stolen: the Nazis did not misread Sanskrit scholarship slightly; they inverted it. Müller, seeing the slide begin, protested publicly that "Aryan" named a family of languages, not a race.[^muller-aryan] The protest did not stop the theft — a sober lesson: scholars do not control what politics does with their vocabulary.

There is an Indian side too, and honesty requires naming it even-handedly. Some colonial-era Indian writers embraced the claimed kinship with Europeans as a badge of status; some anti-caste thinkers, notably in the south, inverted the story into one of Aryan invaders subjugating indigenous peoples — the word became a weapon *within* Indian politics; and how Indo-Aryan languages arrived in the subcontinent remains charged today. Chapter 6 returns to those battles. The structural point: on two continents, in opposite directions, a claim about verbs was pressed into service as a claim about blood — which philology could never underwrite.

:::argument title="The case that the scholarship and the atrocity must be judged separately"
conclusion: The genuine achievements of Indo-European philology are not discredited by the later racial abuse of the word "Aryan"; keeping the two separate is necessary for thinking clearly about both.
premise: The linguistic findings — systematic correspondences among Sanskrit, Greek, Latin, and their relatives — rest on methods that remain valid two centuries on.
premise: The racial doctrine required an additional leap, from shared language to pure ancestral race, which leading philologists — Müller among them — explicitly rejected when it was made.
premise: Holding a discipline responsible for inversions its practitioners publicly opposed would make all knowledge hostage to its worst hijackers, and leave us unable to say what the hijackers got wrong.
:::

:::counter title="Objection: the scholarship was not an innocent bystander" to="The case that the scholarship and the atrocity must be judged separately"
The strongest reply is that the theft was easier because the house was left unlocked. Nineteenth-century philology did not merely describe language families; much of it spoke freely of the "genius" of peoples, told of noble conquering Aryans and degraded descendants, and slid between "language" and "race" for decades before the racists arrived — Müller's own earlier writings used the loose racial idiom he later renounced. The frame of pure origins and fallen presents was built *by* the scholarship, not added to it. On this view, disciplines owe a duty of care for the metaphors they release into politics, and Indology's founders failed it. That does not make philologists the authors of what followed — the chasm between a loose metaphor and a genocidal state is absolute — but it forbids the conclusion that science was simply robbed by strangers. Rigour about evidence is not enough; be rigorous about your story too.
:::

## 4.5 Berlin as an Indian crossroads

The entanglement was never only textual. From the early twentieth century, Indian students — engineers, doctors, chemists in the making — appear in German university towns, drawn by strong institutions and a Europe beyond the empire that ruled them. During the First World War, Berlin briefly became a headquarters of Indian revolution: with German support, exiled nationalists organised a committee that worked, through propaganda and smuggled arms, against British rule — on the logic that Britain's war was India's opportunity.[^berlin]

:::uncertainty title="Details of the Berlin revolutionaries"
That an Indian independence committee operated in Berlin with German backing during the First World War, and that its plans (the "Hindu–German conspiracy" of later trials) largely failed, is standard history. I cannot vouch for the precise founding date, membership, or organisational names. Citation needed — verify before publishing.
:::

The Second World War brought a darker chapter: the nationalist leader Subhas Chandra Bose escaped British surveillance and reached Berlin in 1941, raised a legion from Indian prisoners of war, and broadcast against British rule before departing for Japanese-held Asia. Say it plainly: Bose sought help from Nazi Germany, a regime whose crimes were then in progress. Historians and Indians still argue over how to weigh his anticolonial ends against that alliance — a hard case, previewing Chapter 6's question of how nations remember complicated heroes. For now, the simpler point: by the 1940s, Berlin was a place where Indian futures had repeatedly been plotted.

## 4.6 Trade, machines, and the layers beneath you

Beneath the scholars and revolutionaries ran the quieter entanglement of commerce and skill. German firms worked on Indian infrastructure and telegraphy in the nineteenth century; German dyes and machines entered Indian markets; Indian trade flowed through Hamburg. After 1945 the layers thickened: Indian professionals and students in West German industry, and, in an often-forgotten strand, Indian nurses, many from Kerala, recruited into German hospitals in the 1960s and 1970s.[^nurses] Today's migration of IT specialists, analysts, and students — your migration — is the newest stratum of a two-century deposit.

The thread through it all — scholars, thieves of words, revolutionaries, nurses, and you — is that the space between India and Germany has never been empty. It has been a workshop, each side building versions of the other for its own purposes, with results ranging from magnificent scholarship to catastrophe. You do not stand between two separate worlds; you stand on two centuries of traffic. The next chapter turns to something closer to the skin: the history of an ordinary working day — including the night shift.
