# 5. A Day's Work: A History of Labour and the Everyday

Think about what your shift will leave behind. A line in a payroll system. A staffing plan with your initials. Perhaps a closed complaint ticket. In a hundred years, a historian wanting to know what your working night was like — the sore feet, the rush and lull, the joke that got the team through the 3 a.m. slump — will find almost no trace. Now run the implication backwards: everything you were taught as "history" was written from the traces that *did* survive, and for most of the past, the people whose days looked most like yours left the fewest. Kings generate archives. Fryers do not. This chapter is about the historians who decided that your kind of day *is* history — and what they found in the working days, meals, sleep, and family lives of ordinary people. The short version: almost everything called "how work and family have always been" has a birthday, usually a recent one.

## 5.1 The slow sea: history at the pace of the everyday

In 1929, two French historians — Marc Bloch, the examining magistrate of Chapter 1, and Lucien Febvre — founded the journal *Annales* around a rebellious idea: history should be about more than states, wars, and great men; it should study prices, harvests, diets, tools, beliefs — the texture of ordinary life.[^annales] Their successor Fernand Braudel pushed the idea to its extreme with the :::glossary term="Longue durée"::: — the "long duration": beneath the fast foam of political events lie slower currents (economic cycles, trade patterns) and, deeper still, an almost motionless sea of structures — climate, geography, crops and diseases, the habits of daily life — that constrain everything above them and change over centuries, not years.[^braudel]

The picture is worth keeping. The news presents history as a chase scene. Braudel's ocean says: most of what shapes a life moves far more slowly and anonymously than the news can see — what people eat, when they sleep, how they work, whom they may marry. And it implies something quietly radical: the deep layers, where ordinary life happens, are the *load-bearing* ones. The kings surf on them.

## 5.2 History from below

The English historian E. P. Thompson — whose account of clock-time you met in Chapter 2 — gave the complementary movement its manifesto. *The Making of the English Working Class* (1963) set out to rescue weavers, artisans, and labourers "from the enormous condescension of posterity": to treat the defeated and obscure as historical actors with their own reasoning, not raw material processed by great events.[^thompson] The approach came to be called :::glossary term="History from below":::, and it changed what counts as a source: court records, wage books, songs, pawnshop ledgers, strike pamphlets — read against the grain, these let historians reconstruct lives that left no memoirs.

The stakes are direct. History from below is the scholarly version of a claim you may make about your own life: a night shift, a shared flat, a remittance sent home, a call across continents is not the boring background to history. It is the substance. Whether anyone archives it is a question about power, not importance.

## 5.3 Guild, factory, counter: three shapes of a working life

Set three working lives side by side. First, the craft world of the guilds, which organised skilled urban trades in medieval and early modern Europe and whose ghost survives in the German *Ausbildung* system of apprentices, examinations, and master craftsmen. A :::glossary term="Guild"::: regulated who could practise a trade, at what quality and price, and how a boy — almost always a boy — moved from apprentice to journeyman to, perhaps, master. Work was identity: lifelong, inherited, communal; also closed, hierarchical, and jealously exclusionary, toward women and outsiders above all. Second, the factory: work as measured time (Chapter 2), sold by the hour to an employer you might never meet — interchangeable, supervised, and, the other side of the coin, leaveable. Third, the service and platform economy you know from inside: fragmented shifts and metrics, flexible for the employer, precarious for the worker, invisible to customers who see only the counter.

The sequence is not a simple fall or rise. The guild journeyman had belonging and standards but no freedom to leave his station; the factory hand had monotony but, eventually, the concentrated numbers that made unions possible; the service worker has real flexibility and real insecurity. The point is the one this book keeps making: each arrangement was *built*, contested, and changed — so the current one is not the end of the story either.

## 5.4 The invention of the working day

A fact easy to miss from inside a rota: the length of the working day is one of the great political achievements of modern history, fought for over a century. Early industrial days of twelve to sixteen hours, including for children, were treated by employers as the natural price of competitiveness. Against this, the labour movement raised a demand of beautiful simplicity — eight hours' labour, eight hours' recreation, eight hours' rest. The campaign ran through decades of strikes, laws, and setbacks: factory acts limiting children's and women's hours came first in Britain; May Day commemorates the American eight-hour agitation of the 1880s and the violence in Chicago that followed; Germany conceded the eight-hour day in the upheaval after the First World War; and the very first convention of the new International Labour Organization, in 1919, addressed the eight-hour day.[^hours]

:::uncertainty title="Dates and details of the eight-hour struggle"
The arc in 5.4 — factory acts, the 1880s American eight-hour campaign and the Haymarket events, Germany's post-1918 concession, the ILO's first convention — is standard labour history, but I cannot vouch for the precise years of specific factory acts, the exact origin of the eight-hours slogan (often associated with Robert Owen), or the details of the German settlement of 1918. Citation needed — verify before publishing. Reliable in shape, unverified in its particulars.
:::

Why does this matter to someone whose shifts are set by an app? It corrects two opposite mistakes. The first is fatalism: hours, breaks, night and Sunday premiums feel like weather, but every one was won, is defended in law and contract, and can be eroded — the boundary of the working day has moved in both directions and is moving now, as flexible scheduling quietly hands time back to employers. The second is nostalgia for a golden age of secure work: the "standard" eight-hour, permanent job was a high-water mark reached in some countries for some decades — and mostly for *some workers*, built around a male, native-born norm many others never enjoyed. Your rota has a history. It is not finished.

## 5.5 Women's work: visible, invisible, and rewritten

Now the claim heard at family gatherings on two continents: men have always been the providers, women have always kept the home, and recent decades upset a timeless order. History from below has examined this claim, and it fails — instructively.

For most of the recorded past, in Europe as in India, ordinary households were units of *joint* production, and women's work was everywhere: in the fields at harvest, in dairying, brewing, and market selling, in spinning so universal that "spinster" became a legal term for an unmarried woman, in the books of artisan workshops. What women rarely had was *ownership* of the work: guild membership, property, and the title of "head" generally belonged to men, and much female work was recorded — when recorded at all — under a husband's or father's name. The archive, as Chapter 1 warned, mirrors the pen-holders.

The strict split into a male "breadwinner" and a female "homemaker" whose work does not count as work is, by contrast, datable. It crystallised in the nineteenth century as the ideology historians call :::glossary term="Separate spheres"::: — men belong to the public world of work and politics, women to the private world of home and virtue. It was preached most confidently by and about the middle classes; poor women, as ever, worked for wages regardless of the sermon. As a *mass* reality, the sole-male-earner household peaked in the mid-twentieth century in industrial countries — a couple of generations, in a couple of regions — before economics dissolved it again. And be even-handed about who the script burdened: the arrangement that erased women's work from the ledger also loaded men with sole responsibility for the family's survival and measured a man's worth by his wage — a pressure many male readers will recognise arriving, undiminished, in a call from home. Meanwhile the unpaid half of the economy — cooking, cleaning, raising children, tending the old — remained real work, statistically invisible, and overwhelmingly female, as feminist economists have long insisted.

:::argument title="The case that 'traditional family roles' are a recent construction"
conclusion: The strict breadwinner-husband / homemaker-wife model is not a timeless human arrangement but a historically specific one, at its strongest for roughly a century in particular classes and countries.
premise: Pre-industrial households across many regions were joint productive units in which women's labour — agricultural, textile, commercial — was routine and economically necessary.
premise: The ideology of separate spheres can be dated, located socially in the rising middle classes, and shown to have been contradicted throughout by the waged work of poorer women.
premise: An arrangement that appears, spreads, peaks, and recedes within documented history is by definition not a constant of human nature.
:::

:::counter title="Objection: recency is not the same as arbitrariness" to="The case that 'traditional family roles' are a recent construction"
The strongest reply grants the history and challenges the implied conclusion. That the breadwinner–homemaker split is recent does not show it was baseless: across a wide range of societies, however work was divided, childbearing and nursing fell to women, and many arrangements clustered around that biological fact — so a defender can argue the nineteenth century rigidified a much older, partly practical division rather than inventing one. And "recent" cuts both ways: today's dual-earner norm is the *most* recent arrangement of all, and can be criticised on its own terms — households often need two incomes where one sufficed, and care work remains unpaid and unequally shared. The honest claim is narrower than slogans on either side: history refutes the *timelessness* of any family script, old or new, but cannot tell you which is best. That remains an argument about values and evidence in the present, to be had without borrowing false authority from "always."
:::

## 5.6 A history of the night

Finally, the part of the day this book's readers know better than most historians: the night. Even sleep has a history. The historian A. Roger Ekirch, working through diaries and court depositions, argued that pre-industrial Europeans commonly slept in two shifts — a "first sleep" and "second sleep" divided by an hour of wakefulness used for prayer, talk, chores, or sex — and that the consolidated eight-hour block we now treat as the only healthy sleep spread with artificial lighting and industrial schedules.[^ekirch] The finding is debated in its details, but its lesson fits the whole chapter: even the shape of a night's sleep is partly a historical artefact, not a biological constant with one correct form.

Night *work* is ancient in the small — watchmen, bakers, sailors on watch — but modern in the large. Cheap artificial light, and the economics of machines too expensive to let sleep, made the night shift a mass experience: first in mills and steelworks, then in transport, hospitals, and eventually the 24-hour service economy in which some readers are standing right now. The ledger here is double, and honesty requires both columns. Night work made possible things worth having — emergency wards, night trains, warm bread. It also detached millions of lives from the shared rhythm of family meals, festivals, and sleep, and it has always fallen disproportionately on those with the least power to refuse it: the young, the poor, and the migrant.

:::reflect
Do a piece of history from below on the household you grew up in. Take one ordinary working day of your mother's and one of your father's (or whoever raised you): what counted as their "work," what was paid, what appeared in any record — payslip, licence, land document — and what survives only in memory. Write down one piece of labour from each life that no archive will ever show. Then note, honestly: in your current week, which of your labours would be similarly invisible to a future historian — and to your family's telling of your story?
:::

The everyday, then, has a history, mostly of things fought over: the hour, the night, the ledger, who cooks and who is recorded. Nothing about your rota or your family's division of labour descends unchanged from time immemorial; it descends from struggles, most of them recent, some still open. One question remains. If even the working day had to be fought into existence, who decides which version of the past a family, or a nation, gets to keep? That is the final chapter: what we choose to remember.
