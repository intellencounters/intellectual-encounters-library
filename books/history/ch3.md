# 3. A Species That Moves

The waiting room at the immigration office has a particular silence. You hold a folder of documents that prove, in the language of the state, that your presence here is conditional — a permit, a purpose, a date by which something must be renewed. It is easy, in that room, to absorb the room's premise: that moving is the exception, that people *belong* to a place, that you are a departure from the normal order of things. This chapter argues, from the evidence, the opposite. Movement is not the exception in human history. It is very close to the rule. The person who stays put for generations is the rarer creature. If that is true, then the anxious sense of being out of place is worth re-examining — not because your difficulties are imaginary, but because the story that frames them may be false.

## 3.1 The oldest human story

Our species did not begin spread across the world; it spread. The broad scientific picture is that anatomically modern humans arose in Africa and, over many thousands of years, moved outward — across Asia, into Europe and the Pacific, and eventually into the Americas — encountering, displacing, and mixing with other human populations along the way. The details are revised constantly as new fossils and, above all, new genetic evidence arrive. But the shape is not seriously in doubt: to be human is to descend from people who went somewhere else.

:::uncertainty title="Dates for the peopling of the world"
Specific figures — when modern humans first left Africa, when they reached Australia or the Americas, how many separate waves there were — are actively debated and repeatedly revised as evidence accumulates. I have deliberately avoided putting exact numbers of years in the prose above, because the honest state of the field is one of moving estimates rather than fixed dates. Citation needed — verify before publishing. Treat Section 3.1 as a claim about the *shape* of human dispersal, not about precise chronology.
:::

## 3.2 There are no pure peoples

The more powerful finding of recent decades is not just that humans moved, but that they *mixed* — thoroughly, repeatedly, everywhere. Advances in reading ancient DNA have let researchers compare the genomes of past and present populations directly, and the recurring result is that today's peoples are almost all the product of ancient minglings between groups that were themselves products of earlier minglings. The geneticist David Reich, summarising this research for general readers, describes population after population once imagined as ancient and unmixed turning out to be a relatively recent blend.[^reich]

This matters far beyond the laboratory. The idea that a nation or a people is a pure, fixed lineage stretching unbroken into the deep past — the idea that some belong by blood and others are intruders — is not merely morally ugly; it is, as a matter of evidence, false. The "native" population that a newcomer is told they dilute is itself, on inspection, a sediment of earlier newcomers. This is one of the clearest cases where the historian's habit from Chapter 1 pays off: "our people have always been here, pure and separate" fails the test of the primary evidence, which is now written in the genome itself.

## 3.3 Migration is not the modern exception

Zoom in from prehistory to recorded history and the pattern holds. Trade routes carried not only goods but people, languages, foods, and gods across continents. Empires moved populations by conquest, settlement, and forced labour. Famine, war, and opportunity pushed and pulled people across borders that were themselves always shifting. The spice trade, the great overland and maritime routes, the movements of workers, merchants, soldiers, scholars, and the enslaved — the map of the human past is a map of motion. Cuisine is a fossil record of it: the chilli that feels essential to your home cooking crossed an ocean to get there a few centuries ago.

So when migration is spoken of as a modern crisis, a disruption of a settled world, the history simply does not support the "settled world" part. What is genuinely modern is not movement but the *machinery for controlling it*: the passport, the visa, the hardened border, the immigration file in your folder. Those are recent inventions — a technology, like the clock in Chapter 2 — laid over a species that has always moved. Understanding this does not make a border disappear or a permit renew itself. But it relocates the strangeness: the barrier is the novelty, not the traveller.

## 3.4 The Indian diaspora as one chapter of a very old story

Set the movement of Indians across the world into this long frame and it stops looking like an aberration and starts looking like a recent verse of an ancient song. A :::glossary term="Diaspora"::: — a population settled far from an ancestral homeland while retaining ties to it — is one of the oldest social forms there is. The dispersal of people from the Indian subcontinent has many layers: ancient trade across the Indian Ocean; the movement of labour, often under harsh and coerced conditions, within and beyond empires in the nineteenth century; and, in recent decades, the migration of students, professionals, and workers to the Gulf, to Europe, to North America, and far beyond.

To be a young Indian studying and working in Germany is, seen this way, to be doing something both intensely personal and deeply un-original — in the best sense. You are not the first of your line to leave in search of a future, only the most recent, and you sit within one of the largest and oldest patterns of human movement there is. That does not dissolve the loneliness of a Diwali far from home or the sting of a stranger's assumption that you do not belong. But it answers the immigration office's silent premise directly: you are not a deviation from how humans live. You are an example of it.

:::uncertainty title="The scale of the Indian diaspora"
It is common to read that the Indian diaspora is among the largest in the world, numbering in the tens of millions. I cannot here verify a specific figure or ranking, and such numbers depend heavily on definitions (who counts as "diaspora" — citizens abroad, people of Indian origin, both?) and on the source. Citation needed — verify before publishing. Treat the claim in Section 3.4 as a qualitative one — that this is a large and historically layered dispersal — rather than a precise count.
:::

## 3.5 What history can, and cannot, teach

It would be dishonest to end with history as a comfort blanket, so here is the honest ledger.

:::argument title="The liberating reading of a mobile human past"
conclusion: Knowing that movement and mixing are the human norm can rightly loosen the shame and sense of trespass that migrants are so often made to feel.
premise: The evidence shows dispersal and admixture to be near-universal features of human history, not deviations from a settled baseline.
premise: Much of the felt "wrongness" of being a migrant comes from a false background story — that peoples are fixed to places and movement is exceptional — which the evidence contradicts.
premise: Correcting a false story that produces shame is a legitimate and humane use of historical knowledge.
:::

:::counter title="Objection: 'we've always migrated' can gloss over real, unequal suffering" to="The liberating reading of a mobile human past"
The strongest reply is that a cheerful "humans have always moved" can flatten enormous moral differences and quietly excuse present harm. Much historical migration was not free adventure but conquest, expulsion, famine flight, and enslavement — movement inflicted *on* people, not chosen by them. To tell someone struggling under a hostile visa regime that "migration is the norm" can slide into telling them their specific, engineered hardship is natural and therefore acceptable — the exact error Chapter 1 warned against, dressed up as consolation. The past's normality of movement says nothing about whether *this* border, *this* wage, *this* exclusion is just. History can remove a false shame; it cannot, by itself, tell you a single thing about what you are owed now, or what you owe others.
:::

That counter is not a footnote; it is the boundary of the whole book. History is powerful in a narrow, precise way. It can show that a thing you were told was eternal has a birthday. It can dissolve a "always" and an "everyone." It can strip the false authority from a script and hand the pen back to you. What it cannot do is write the next line for you. Knowing that the clock, the career, the timetable of adulthood, and the immigration file are all recent inventions does not tell you whether to chase the promotion, when or whether to marry, whether to stay or return, what you owe the people who raised you. Those remain genuine, weighty, open questions — and they are *yours*, in a way they could not be while you believed the answers were fixed by an unchanging past.

:::reflect
Return to the single expectation you wrote down in Chapter 1 and again in Chapter 2. Now, having seen how much of the "always" behind it turns out to be recent and made, write down two things, separately and honestly. First: what part of that expectation, freed from the false claim of timelessness, do you still choose to keep — and for what reason you could state aloud? Second: what part were you carrying only because you believed it was eternal and unquestionable? You do not have to resolve anything today. The aim is only to notice which weights you are choosing to carry and which you were merely handed.
:::

The long view does not make life lighter by pretending its burdens are unreal. It makes life more *yours* by showing which burdens are ancient and unavoidable, which are recent and negotiable, and which — on close inspection — were never true at all. That is the whole freedom on offer here: not escape from choosing, but the clear ground on which a choice can finally be your own.
