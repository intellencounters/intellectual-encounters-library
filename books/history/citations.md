# Citations and further reading

These are the works referred to in this book. Only works the editors are genuinely confident exist are listed; specific dates, statistics, and rankings that could not be verified are flagged inside `:::uncertainty` blocks in the chapters rather than asserted here. Bibliographic details below are given from memory and general knowledge — **verify every detail (exact edition, year, page, and wording) against the original before citing this material in published work.**

## On the nature and method of history

- **E. H. Carr, *What Is History?*** — The classic short reflection on what history is, on the relationship between the historian and the facts, and on why there is no view from nowhere. Based on a lecture series and widely read as a first book on historical method. (Referenced in the book's argument that history is reconstruction and interpretation, not a neutral mirror of the past.)

- **Marc Bloch, *The Historian's Craft* (*Apologie pour l'histoire*)** — An unfinished meditation on the historian's method, written while Bloch was in the French Resistance and published posthumously after his execution by the Gestapo in 1944. Source of the image of the historian as an examining magistrate who cross-examines every witness. (Cited in Chapter 1.)

- **Herbert Butterfield, *The Whig Interpretation of History*** — The essay that named and criticised the habit of reading the past as a triumphant march toward the enlightened present. Background to this book's warnings about presentism and grand narratives.

## On the making of the modern world

- **E. P. Thompson, "Time, Work-Discipline, and Industrial Capitalism," *Past & Present*** — A foundational essay on how industrial wage labour transformed the human experience of time, replacing task-oriented work with clock-time discipline enforced by bells and fines. (Cited in Chapter 2.)

- **Philippe Ariès, *Centuries of Childhood: A Social History of Family Life*** — An influential and much-contested argument that "childhood" as a distinct, sheltered stage was not sharply marked in medieval Europe and emerged more clearly later. Used in Chapter 2 with an explicit note that the thesis has been challenged.

- **Jeffrey Jensen Arnett** — The psychologist associated with the concept of "emerging adulthood," describing an extended exploratory life-stage through the twenties in many wealthy societies. Referred to in Chapter 2; the exact publication details are flagged as unverified in an uncertainty block.

- **Eric Hobsbawm and Terence Ranger (eds.), *The Invention of Tradition* (1983)** — A collection documenting how practices presented as ancient and timeless often have recent, datable origins. Background to Chapter 1's argument that "it's always been this way" is usually false, and cited directly in Chapter 6 for the concept of invented tradition.

## On migration and the human past

- **David Reich, *Who We Are and How We Got Here: Ancient DNA and the New Science of the Human Past*** — A geneticist's overview, for general readers, of how ancient-DNA research has revealed pervasive population mixing throughout human history, undermining the idea of pure, fixed lineages. (Cited in Chapter 3.)

## On India, Germany, and Orientalism (Chapter 4)

- **Edward W. Said, *Orientalism* (1978)** — The founding critique of Western knowledge of "the East" as entangled with power over it. Said's focus was chiefly British and French engagement with the Middle East; the fit of his framework to Germany's colony-less Indology is itself a scholarly debate, which Chapter 4 flags rather than resolves.

- **William Jones, third anniversary discourse to the Asiatic Society (Calcutta, 1786)** — The address containing the celebrated observation that Sanskrit, Greek, and Latin appear "sprung from some common source," a founding moment of Indo-European philology. (Cited in Chapter 4.)

- **Friedrich Schlegel, *Über die Sprache und Weisheit der Indier* (1808)** — The Romantic manifesto of German enthusiasm for Sanskrit and India. (Cited in Chapter 4.)

- **Friedrich Max Müller** — German-born Oxford philologist (1823–1900); editor of the Rig Veda and of the *Sacred Books of the East* series, and a late, public opponent of conflating the "Aryan" language family with race. Chapter 4 cites his career and his protest as general, well-attested facts; no specific edition, lecture, or exact quotation is asserted, and none should be reproduced without verification against his published works.

## On labour and the everyday (Chapter 5)

- **E. P. Thompson, *The Making of the English Working Class* (1963)** — The manifesto of history from below; source of the phrase "the enormous condescension of posterity" in its preface. (Cited in Chapter 5; Thompson's time-discipline essay is cited separately above.)

- **Marc Bloch and Lucien Febvre (founders), the *Annales* journal (1929)** — The journal (originally *Annales d'histoire économique et sociale*) that gave its name to the Annales school of history — economies, societies, and everyday structures rather than political events alone. (Cited in Chapter 5; Bloch's *The Historian's Craft* is cited separately above.)

- **Fernand Braudel, *The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II* (first French edition 1949)** — The work most associated with layered historical time and the *longue durée*. (Cited in Chapter 5.)

- **A. Roger Ekirch, *At Day's Close: Night in Times Past* (2005)** — The history of night in pre-industrial Europe, including the much-discussed evidence for segmented "first" and "second" sleep; the strength of that pattern remains debated, as Chapter 5 notes. (Cited in Chapter 5.)

## On memory and the uses of the past (Chapter 6)

- **Maurice Halbwachs, *Les cadres sociaux de la mémoire* (1925; English editions as *On Collective Memory*)** — The founding work on collective memory. Halbwachs died in the Buchenwald concentration camp in 1945. (Cited in Chapter 6.)

- **Pierre Nora (ed.), *Les lieux de mémoire* (from 1984; partially translated as *Realms of Memory*)** — The collective project mapping the "sites of memory" in which French national memory is deposited. (Cited in Chapter 6.)

- **Svetlana Boym, *The Future of Nostalgia* (2001)** — Source of the distinction between restorative and reflective nostalgia used in Chapter 6; exact quotations should be checked against the original before reuse.
